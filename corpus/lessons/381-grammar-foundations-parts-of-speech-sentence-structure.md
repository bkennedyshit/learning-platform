# 38.1 — Grammar Foundations: Parts of Speech & Sentence Structure

> *"Grammar is the logic of speech, even as logic is the grammar of reason."* — Richard C. Trench

Grammar is the operating system of language. You already run it — you produce grammatical sentences without consciously knowing the rules. This chapter makes the implicit explicit. When you know *why* something is correct, you can make deliberate choices instead of lucky guesses, and you can diagnose your own errors instead of relying on spell-check.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Name and identify all eight parts of speech and explain the function of each.
2. Distinguish between phrases and clauses; identify independent and dependent clauses.
3. Classify any sentence as simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex.
4. Apply subject-verb agreement rules including tricky cases (collective nouns, indefinite pronouns, inverted sentences).
5. Identify and correct sentence fragments and run-on sentences.
6. Diagram the underlying grammatical pattern (S-V, S-V-O, S-V-C, S-V-IO-DO, S-V-O-C) of any sentence.

## 📚 1. The Eight Parts of Speech

Every word in English belongs to one of eight **parts of speech** — its grammatical category based on its function in a sentence.

### 1.1 Noun
A word naming a person, place, thing, idea, or quality.

| Subtype | Definition | Examples |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Proper noun | Specific, named entity | *Bill, Tokyo, Python, Monday* |
| Common noun | General category | *engineer, city, language, day* |
| Concrete noun | Physically perceivable | *keyboard, coffee, desk* |
| Abstract noun | Concept, quality, idea | *freedom, intelligence, momentum* |
| Collective noun | Group treated as unit | *team, jury, data, committee* |
| Count noun | Countable (has plural) | *file, bug, feature* |
| Mass (non-count) noun | Not individually counted | *information, software, water* |

> **Grammar note:** Mass nouns don't take an indefinite article (*a*, *an*) or a simple plural. You can't write *an information* or *three softwares*. You write *a piece of information*, *three software packages*.

### 1.2 Pronoun
A word that substitutes for a noun (its **antecedent**).

| Subtype | Examples |
|---------|---------|
| Personal | *I, you, he, she, it, we, they; me, him, her, us, them* |
| Possessive | *my, your, his, her, its, our, their; mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs* |
| Reflexive | *myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, themselves* |
| Demonstrative | *this, that, these, those* |
| Interrogative | *who, whom, which, what, whose* |
| Relative | *who, whom, which, that, whose* |
| Indefinite | *anyone, everyone, someone, no one, each, either, neither, both, several, many, few, all* |
| Reciprocal | *each other, one another* |

### 1.3 Verb
A word expressing action, occurrence, or state of being.

| Subtype | Definition | Examples |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Action verb | Describes what subject does | *writes, compiles, pushes, runs* |
| Linking verb | Connects subject to complement | *is, are, was, were, seem, become, appear, feel, look, smell, sound, taste* |
| Helping (auxiliary) verb | Assists main verb | *can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would, do, does, did, have, has, had, be, am, is, are, was, were, been, being* |
| Transitive verb | Takes a direct object | *She wrote **the code**.* |
| Intransitive verb | Takes no direct object | *The server **crashed**.* |

> **Grammar note:** Many verbs can be transitive or intransitive depending on context. *She runs the program* (transitive) vs. *She runs every morning* (intransitive).

### 1.4 Adjective
Modifies a noun or pronoun; answers *which one?, what kind?, how many?*

- **Attributive** (directly before noun): *the **broken** build*, *a **fast** algorithm*
- **Predicative** (after linking verb): *The server is **down**. The code looks **clean**.*
- **Degrees:** positive (*fast*) → comparative (*faster*) → superlative (*fastest*)

### 1.5 Adverb
Modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause; answers *how?, when?, where?, to what degree?*

- *She typed **quickly**.* (modifies verb)
- *The code is **surprisingly** clean.* (modifies adjective)
- *He finished **very** quickly.* (modifies adverb)
- ***Honestly**, I didn't see that bug coming.* (modifies clause — "sentence adverb")

> **Common trap:** Adjectives modify nouns; adverbs modify everything else. *She ran **quick*** (wrong — *quick* is an adjective modifying the pronoun *she*). *She ran **quickly*** (correct — adverb modifying the verb *ran*).

### 1.6 Preposition
Shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word in the sentence.

**Common prepositions:** *about, above, across, after, against, along, among, around, at, before, behind, below, beneath, beside, between, beyond, by, despite, down, during, except, for, from, in, inside, into, like, near, of, off, on, onto, out, outside, over, past, since, through, throughout, till, to, toward, under, until, up, upon, with, within, without*

> **Preposition vs. adverb trap:** *The plane flew **over**.* (adverb — no object) vs. *The plane flew **over** the runway.* (preposition — object: *the runway*)

### 1.7 Conjunction
Joins words, phrases, or clauses.

| Subtype | Words | Use |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Coordinating | *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so* (FANBOYS) | Joins equal grammatical elements |
| Subordinating | *although, because, since, when, while, if, unless, until, after, before, as, though, whereas* | Introduces dependent clause |
| Correlative | *either…or, neither…nor, both…and, not only…but also, whether…or* | Pairs; connects equal elements |
| Conjunctive adverb | *however, therefore, moreover, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, thus, hence, indeed* | Connects independent clauses (needs semicolon + comma) |

### 1.8 Interjection
An exclamatory word or phrase with no grammatical connection to the sentence.

*Oh! Wow. Well, that's interesting. Hey — watch out.*

## 📐 2. Phrases and Clauses

### 2.1 Phrase
A group of related words **without** a subject-verb pair. Cannot stand alone as a sentence.

| Phrase Type | Definition | Example |
|------------|-----------|---------|
| Noun phrase | Noun + modifiers | *the corrupted database file* |
| Verb phrase | Main verb + auxiliaries | *has been running for hours* |
| Prepositional phrase | Preposition + object | *in the production environment* |
| Participial phrase | Participle + complements | *crashed by the memory leak* |
| Infinitive phrase | *to* + verb + complements | *to refactor the entire module* |
| Gerund phrase | Gerund (verb+-ing) as noun | *debugging at 2 AM* |
| Absolute phrase | Noun + participle, modifies whole sentence | *The build finally passing, we merged the PR.* |

### 2.2 Clause
A group of related words **with** a subject-verb pair.

| Clause Type | Definition | Can stand alone? |
|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Independent (main) clause | Complete thought | **Yes** — it's a sentence |
| Dependent (subordinate) clause | Introduced by subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun; incomplete thought | **No** — sentence fragment if isolated |

**Dependent clause subtypes:**

- **Noun clause** (functions as a noun): *What you build defines you.*
- **Adjective (relative) clause** (modifies a noun): *The bug **that crashed production** was a race condition.*
- **Adverb clause** (modifies a verb/adjective/adverb): *The deploy failed **because the config was missing**.*

## 📐 3. The Four Sentence Types

| Type | Structure | Example |
|------|----------|---------|
| **Simple** | One independent clause | *The server went down.* |
| **Compound** | Two+ independent clauses joined by coordinator or semicolon | *The server went down, but the backup took over.* |
| **Complex** | One independent + one+ dependent clauses | *Because the config was wrong, the server went down.* |
| **Compound-complex** | Two+ independent + one+ dependent clauses | *Because the config was wrong, the server went down, and the team was paged.* |

## 📐 4. The Five Sentence Patterns

Every English sentence maps to one of five underlying patterns:

| Pattern | Abbreviation | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Subject — Verb | S-V | *The server **crashed**.* |
| Subject — Verb — Object | S-V-O | *The engineer **fixed** the bug.* |
| Subject — Verb — Complement | S-V-C | *The error message **was** cryptic.* |
| Subject — Verb — Indirect Object — Direct Object | S-V-IO-DO | *She **sent** me the logs.* |
| Subject — Verb — Object — Complement | S-V-O-C | *The team **called** the outage a disaster.* |

> **Why this matters:** Sentence patterns determine what verbs can take what complements. Linking verbs (pattern 3) never take objects — they take *complements* (nouns or adjectives that re-describe the subject). This is why *She is **good*** is correct but *She is **well*** means she has recovered from illness (where *well* is the adjective meaning "healthy," not the adverb meaning "skillfully").

## 🔑 5. Subject-Verb Agreement

The subject and verb of a clause must agree in **person** (1st/2nd/3rd) and **number** (singular/plural).

### Core rule
- Singular subject → singular verb: *The algorithm **works**.*
- Plural subject → plural verb: *The algorithms **work**.*

### Tricky cases

**1. Collective nouns** — treat as singular when acting as a unit; plural when acting individually:
- *The team **is** meeting at noon.* (unit)
- *The team **are** taking their separate flights.* (individuals — British English; American English prefers "members of the team are")

**2. Indefinite pronouns:**

| Always singular | Always plural | Singular or plural (by context) |
|----------------|--------------|--------------------------------|
| *anyone, everyone, someone, no one, anybody, everybody, somebody, nobody, each, either, neither, one* | *both, few, many, others, several* | *all, any, more, most, none, some* |

*Each of the tests **is** failing.* (singular)
*Both of the servers **are** online.* (plural)
*Some of the data **is** missing.* (mass noun → singular)
*Some of the files **are** missing.* (count noun → plural)

**3. Compound subjects:**
- Joined by *and* → plural: *The frontend **and** the backend **are** both down.*
- Joined by *or/nor* → agree with nearest subject: *Neither the dev **nor** the ops team **was** notified.*

**4. Inverted sentences (verb before subject):**
- *There **are** three bugs in the queue.* (subject: *three bugs* — plural)
- *Here **is** the report.* (subject: *the report* — singular)

**5. Titles, names, and words-as-words:**
- *"Clean Code" **is** on my reading list.* (title = singular)
- *"Best practices" **is** a vague term.* (phrase = singular)

## ⚠️ 6. Fragments and Run-Ons

### Fragment
A group of words that lacks a subject, a verb, or a complete thought — punctuated as a sentence.

| Fragment | Problem | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| *Running the test suite.* | No subject | *She was running the test suite.* |
| *Because the config was wrong.* | Dependent clause alone | *The deploy failed because the config was wrong.* |
| *The engineer who fixed the bug.* | No verb in main clause | *The engineer who fixed the bug received a bonus.* |

> **Intentional fragments:** Professional writers use fragments deliberately for effect. *And then nothing. Silence.* This is a stylistic choice, not an error — but only after you've mastered the rule can you break it intentionally.

### Run-on Sentence
Two or more independent clauses joined without proper punctuation or a conjunction.

| Type | Example | Fix |
|------|---------|-----|
| Fused (comma splice with no comma) | *The server crashed the team was paged.* | Add period, semicolon, or conjunction |
| Comma splice | *The server crashed, the team was paged.* | *The server crashed; the team was paged.* **OR** *The server crashed, and the team was paged.* **OR** *When the server crashed, the team was paged.* |

## ✍️ 7. Worked Examples

### Example 38.1.1 — Identifying Parts of Speech

**Sentence:** *The exhausted engineer quickly deployed a hotfix before the customers noticed the outage.*

| Word | Part of Speech | Notes |
|------|--------------|-------|
| The | Article (adjective) | Modifies *engineer* |
| exhausted | Adjective | Modifies *engineer* |
| engineer | Noun (common, count) | Subject |
| quickly | Adverb | Modifies *deployed* |
| deployed | Verb (action, transitive) | Main verb |
| a | Article (adjective) | Modifies *hotfix* |
| hotfix | Noun | Direct object |
| before | Subordinating conjunction | Introduces dependent clause |
| the | Article | Modifies *customers* |
| customers | Noun | Subject of dependent clause |
| noticed | Verb | Verb of dependent clause |
| the | Article | Modifies *outage* |
| outage | Noun | Object of *noticed* |

**Pattern:** S-V-O (with adverb and dependent adverb clause)

### Example 38.1.2 — Classifying Sentences

### Example 38.1.3 — Correcting Agreement Errors

<details>
<summary>🔍 Find and fix the errors</summary>

1. *Everyone on the team have their own branch.* → *Everyone … **has** their own branch.* (*Everyone* = always singular)

2. *The list of issues are long.* → *The list … **is** long.* (Subject is *list*, not *issues*)

3. *Neither the tests nor the build script are passing.* → *Neither … nor the build script **is** passing.* (Agreement with nearest subject: *build script* = singular)

4. *There's three unresolved tickets.* → *There **are** three unresolved tickets.* (Subject = *three tickets* = plural)

</details>

## 💻 8. Common Errors (Native Speaker Edition)

These are the mistakes native English speakers make most often — not from not knowing English, but from not knowing the explicit rules:

| Error | Example | Rule |
|-------|---------|------|
| **Who vs. Whom** | *Who did you call?* ❌ | Use *whom* when it's the object: *Whom did you call?* (You called **him** → **whom**) |
| **Lay vs. Lie** | *I'm going to lay down.* ❌ | *Lie* = recline (intransitive); *lay* = to place something (transitive, needs object): *Lay the cable down. Lie down.* |
| **I vs. Me** | *Between you and I* ❌ | Prepositions take object pronouns: *between you and **me*** |
| **Fewer vs. Less** | *Less bugs* ❌ | *Fewer* for countable nouns: *fewer bugs*. *Less* for mass nouns: *less latency*. |
| **That vs. Which** | Using interchangeably | *That* introduces restrictive clauses (no commas). *Which* introduces non-restrictive clauses (with commas): *The file **that** crashed the build was deleted. The file, **which** was corrupted, was deleted.* |
| **Dangling modifier** | *Running the tests, the build failed.* | The modifier must attach to the subject: *Running the tests, **I** found the failure.* |
