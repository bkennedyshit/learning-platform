# 43.1 — The Latin Alphabet, Pronunciation & Quantities

*Back to [Subject_Plan](Subject_Plan) | [43.2 →](43.2---The-Case-System-—-Nouns-&-The-5-Declensions)*

> **Chapter goal:** Learn to read Latin aloud with reconstructed classical pronunciation, understand vowel quantities (long vs. short), and master the accent rule — the tools needed before any grammatical study.

---

## 1. Why Pronunciation Matters in Latin

Latin may be a written language for most purposes, but pronunciation matters for three reasons:
1. **Vowel quantities** (long vs. short) affect which syllable is accented — and are critical for reading poetry (dactylic hexameter)
2. **Consistent pronunciation** builds audio memory that aids vocabulary retention
3. **Reading aloud** activates a different cognitive pathway — students who read Latin silently plateau faster than those who vocalize

This curriculum uses **reconstructed classical pronunciation** (the pronunciation of educated Romans in the Late Republic and early Imperial period, ~100 BCE–100 CE). This is the pronunciation used in most modern university Latin programs and reconstructable from ancient grammatical descriptions and metrical poetry.

---

## 2. The Latin Alphabet

Classical Latin used **23 letters** (the modern Latin alphabet minus J, U, W — these were later additions):

```
A  B  C  D  E  F  G  H  I  K  L  M  N  O  P  Q  R  S  T  V  X  Y  Z
```

- **I** serves as both vowel /i/ and consonant /j/ (called "consonantal i")
- **V** serves as both vowel /u/ and consonant /w/ (called "consonantal v" or "u")
- **K** is rare (only in a few traditional words: *Kalendae*, *Carthago*)
- **Y** and **Z** appear only in Greek loanwords (*lyra*, *zona*)

> **Convention:** Modern editions often distinguish *u* (vowel) from *v* (consonant) and *i* from *j* (consonant). Wheelock's Latin uses this modern convention. Ancient inscriptions show no such distinction.

---

## 3. Classical Pronunciation — Consonants

| Latin letter | Classical sound | Notes |
|-------------|----------------|-------|
| **C** | Always /k/ | *Caesar* = /ˈkae.sar/, never "see-zer." *Cicero* = /ˈki.ke.ro/, not "Sis-ero" |
| **G** | Always /g/ | Always hard, even before e/i: *gens* = /gɛns/ (not /dʒɛns/) |
| **S** | Always /s/ | Never /z/ between vowels: *rosa* = /ˈro.sa/, not /ˈro.za/ |
| **V** (consonantal) | /w/ | *veni, vidi, vici* = /ˈweː.niː ˈwiː.diː ˈwiː.kiː/. Caesar said "Weni, Widi, Wiki." |
| **I** (consonantal) | /j/ | *iam* = /jam/ ("yahm"); *Iulius* = /ˈju.li.us/ |
| **H** | Breathed /h/ | Faint; often weakening by Late Republic but retained in classical prose |
| **R** | Trilled /r/ | Like Italian or Spanish *r*; never English retroflex |
| **QU** | /kw/ | *qui* = /kwiː/, *aqua* = /ˈa.kwa/ |
| **GU** (after n) | /gw/ | *lingua* = /ˈliŋ.gwa/ |
| **X** | /ks/ | *rex* = /rɛks/ |
| **PH** | /pʰ/ | Aspirated p; in Greek loanwords; by Late Latin → /f/ |
| **TH** | /tʰ/ | Aspirated t; Greek loanwords; by Late Latin → /t/ |
| **CH** | /kʰ/ | Aspirated k; Greek loanwords: *pulcher* /ˈpul.kʰer/ |

## 4. Classical Pronunciation — Vowels

### 4.1 Short Vowels

| Letter | IPA | Sound | Example |
|--------|-----|-------|---------|
| a | /a/ | Short "ah" | *caput* /ˈka.put/ |
| e | /ɛ/ | Short "bed" | *et* /ɛt/ |
| i | /ɪ/ | Short "bit" | *iter* /ˈɪ.ter/ |
| o | /ɔ/ | Short "off" | *domus* /ˈdɔ.mus/ |
| u | /ʊ/ | Short "put" | *sub* /sʊb/ |

### 4.2 Long Vowels

| Letter | IPA | Sound | Example |
|--------|-----|-------|---------|
| ā | /aː/ | Long "father" | *māter* /ˈmaː.ter/ |
| ē | /eː/ | Long "may" (pure) | *rēx* /reːks/ |
| ī | /iː/ | Long "see" | *vīta* /ˈwiː.ta/ |
| ō | /oː/ | Long "go" (pure) | *mōs* /moːs/ |
| ū | /uː/ | Long "boot" | *lūna* /ˈluː.na/ |

> **Long vowels are held approximately twice as long as short vowels.** This is not a quality difference (like English "bit" vs. "beat") but a **duration** difference. The distinction affects accent placement and poetic meter.

### 4.3 Diphthongs

| Diphthong | Sound | Examples |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| **ae** | /ae/ → /ɛː/ (approximately "eye") | *Caesar, puellae, caelum* |
| **oe** | /oe/ → /øː/ | *poena, foedus* |
| **au** | /au/ ("ow" as in "house") | *aurum, causa, laudare* |
| **eu** | /eu/ (rare; *heu!*, *seu*) | |
| **ui** | /ui/ (rare) | *cui, huic* |

## 5. Vowel Quantities in Practice

Dictionaries and textbooks mark long vowels with a **macron** (ā, ē, ī, ō, ū). Short vowels are unmarked, or marked with a **breve** (ă, ĕ, ĭ, ŏ, ŭ) in teaching texts.

**Why quantities matter:**
1. **Accent placement** — determined by the weight of the penultimate syllable (see §6)
2. **Meaning distinction** — minimal pairs exist: *mālum* (apple) vs. *malum* (evil); *lēvis* (smooth) vs. *levis* (light); *hic* (this, masculine nominative) vs. *hīc* (here)
3. **Poetic meter** — classical Latin poetry (Virgil, Ovid, Horace) is built on **quantity-based meter**, not stress-based. You cannot scan Virgil without knowing vowel lengths.

---

## 6. The Latin Accent Rule

Latin stress accent follows a strict rule based on **syllable weight**:

### Step 1: Define syllable weight
- A **heavy (long) syllable** = a syllable containing a long vowel OR a short vowel followed by two+ consonants
- A **light (short) syllable** = a syllable containing a short vowel followed by at most one consonant

### Step 2: Apply the accent rule
- **Words of 2 syllables:** accent the first syllable always: *RĒ-gis*, *BO-num*
- **Words of 3+ syllables:** if the **penultimate** (second-to-last) syllable is **heavy** → accent it. If the penultimate is **light** → accent the **antepenultimate** (third-to-last)

| Word | Penultimate | Accent falls on | IPA |
|------|------------|----------------|-----|
| *amāre* | ā (long = heavy) | penultimate: -**mā**- | /aˈmaː.re/ |
| *amare* | a (short, one consonant = light) | antepenultimate: **a**- | /ˈa.ma.re/ |
| *mittere* | mit- (short vowel + tt = heavy) | penultimate: **mit**- | /ˈmit.te.re/ |
| *fēmina* | -mi- (short, one consonant = light) | antepenultimate: **fē**- | /ˈfeː.mi.na/ |
| *agricola* | -co- (short, one consonant = light) | antepenultimate: **a**-gri- | wait: 4 syllables: a-**grī**-co-la → penultimate is short → **a**- | /ˈa.gri.ko.la/ |

## 7. Classical vs. Ecclesiastical Pronunciation

| Feature | Classical | Ecclesiastical (Church Latin) |
|---------|----------|-------------------------------|
| **C before e/i** | /k/ | /tʃ/ — *Cicero* = "Chichero" |
| **G before e/i** | /g/ | /dʒ/ — *Regina* = "Rejeena" |
| **V** (consonantal) | /w/ | /v/ — *veni* = "veni" |
| **AE** | /ae/ ~ /ɛː/ | /e/ — *caelum* = "chelum" |
| **TI before vowel** | /ti/ | /tsi/ — *natio* = "natsio" |
| **Y** | /y/ (French u) | /i/ |

> Both systems are valid. Classical is standard for Roman literature; Ecclesiastical is used for the Mass, Gregorian chant, and Church documents. This curriculum uses **Classical**. Switching to Ecclesiastical requires only learning the table above.

## 8. Reading Aloud Practice

Begin immediately with simple Latin sentences. Prioritize:
1. **All vowels fully pronounced** — no schwa, no silent vowels
2. **C always /k/, V always /w/** — the two biggest breaking points
3. **Trill the R** — even if lightly at first
4. **Mark stresses** on each word while reading until the accent rule is automatic

**Practice sentences (with stress marked):**
- *"Puella aquam portat."* → /ˈpwɛl.la ˈa.kwam ˈpɔr.tat/ — "The girl carries water"
- *"Gallia est omnis divīsa in partēs trēs."* → /ˈgal.li.a ɛst ˈɔm.nis diˈwiː.sa ɪn ˈpar.teːs treːs/ — Caesar *BG* I.1
