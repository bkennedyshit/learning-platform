# 18.6 - Industrialization & Imperialism

In 1750, almost everything people used was made by hand or with the help of water, wind, or animal power. By 1900, steam engines and electricity powered factories, railroads crossed continents, and steamships crossed oceans in days. This **Industrial Revolution** made some nations far richer and more powerful than others, and those nations used their new power to conquer. By the end of the nineteenth century, European states controlled about 90 percent of Africa, and Britain ruled India directly. People in Africa and Asia resisted, adapted, or both.

This lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its historical-thinking skill is **causation with multiple factors**: explaining a large change by weighing several causes instead of naming just one.

**Content note:** this lesson discusses racist ideas used to justify empire and describes violence in the Congo Free State. These are included because they are evidence of how imperial rule worked.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain how **industrialization began in Britain**, weighing at least four factors such as coal and steam power, capital (including profits from slavery), free-market ideas, and markets.
2. Describe how industrialization **changed work and cities**, with evidence such as factory discipline, the Luddites (from 1811), and Britain's urban majority by the mid-1800s.
3. Explain the **motives and means of imperialism**, including the civilizing mission, social Darwinism, quinine, and the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885.
4. **Compare** how China, Japan, India, and African states such as Ethiopia responded to imperial pressure.

## Core ideas

### What industrialization changed

**Industrialization** is the shift from hand production to machine production in factories, powered by new energy sources. In the first phase, steam engines fueled by burning coal drove machinery. By 1776, British factories were powering some operations with improved steam engines designed by **James Watt**. Steamboats (from 1807) and steam railways (from 1812) followed.

A **Second Industrial Revolution** in the later nineteenth century brought mass-produced steel (from the Bessemer process of the 1850s) and **electricity**, which "supplant[ed] steam by the end of the nineteenth century."

### Capitalism and its critics

In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), the Scottish economist **Adam Smith** argued that free markets, not government regulation, should set prices. His ideas, later called **capitalism**, challenged mercantilism, and British leaders adopted many of them. OpenStax notes a connection to the previous lesson: historians debate whether slave plantations were capitalist enterprises, but "what no one disputes is that the profits earned from the sale of sugar and other plantation products grown by enslaved people were often invested in capitalist enterprises, including the factories."

Industrialization created wealth for owners and the middle class, "but their profits often came at a high cost to workers." Skilled artisans were replaced by low-paid machine operators; workers had long hours, dangerous conditions, and little control over their work. One 1848 American factory handbook refused to employ anyone "habitually absent from public worship on the Sabbath." Beginning in **1811**, British textile workers called **Luddites** destroyed machines that threatened their trades. Critics such as Karl Marx argued that capitalism exploited workers and would be replaced.

### The industrial city

By the middle of the nineteenth century, **more British people lived in cities and towns than in rural areas**. Housing construction "did not keep up," so working-class neighborhoods were crowded, while the middle class moved to new suburbs. In 1889, the reformer **Charles Booth** mapped London street by street by wealth and poverty, a source historians still use. Over the century, public health improved through discoveries such as Koch's and Pasteur's identification of disease-causing germs.

### Motives and means of imperialism

**Imperialism** is the policy of extending a nation's power by taking control of other territories and peoples. Nineteenth-century imperialists had several motives:

| Motive | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Economic** | Raw materials for factories (rubber, cotton, minerals) and markets for manufactured goods |
| **Political and military** | National prestige and bases to compete with rival powers |
| **Ideological** | The French called it "the civilizing mission": the belief that it was the duty of the "civilized" to spread Western culture and Christianity; **social Darwinism** claimed some peoples were "naturally superior" |

Industrial technology supplied the **means**: steamships and railroads; telegraph lines; breech-loading, repeating rifles; and **quinine**, from cinchona bark. In the 1840s the British discovered that taking quinine before exposure could prevent malaria, and by the 1870s Dutch plantations in Indonesia were producing more of it, which "assisted Europeans in their exploitation of the African interior."

### The Scramble for Africa

At the beginning of the Second Industrial Revolution, Europeans controlled **about 10 percent** of Africa. By the end of the century, they controlled **90 percent**. At the **Berlin Conference** (1884–1885), "without input from Africans, European nations simply allotted different parts of the continent to one another."

The worst abuses occurred in the **Congo Free State**, which the conference let King Leopold II of Belgium control personally. Forced laborers had to collect huge quotas of wild rubber, and Leopold's army cut off hands to tally the people it killed and to punish those who failed to meet quotas. Public outrage, including the Congo Reform Association, led the Belgian Parliament to take the colony from Leopold in 1908. OpenStax cautions that the Congo "differed from most other colonies largely in degree, not in kind."

### Asia: China, India, and Japan

- **China:** Western merchants wanted Chinese tea and silk, but China bought few Western goods. British merchants found "a great market in southern China for smuggled opium," and the trade meant that "for the first time, China imported more goods than it exported." China's defeat in the **First Opium War (1839–1842)** ended with the **Treaty of Nanjing** (1842), which opened five **treaty ports** to Western trade and gave Britain Hong Kong. A second Opium War (from 1856) forced China to open more ports.
- **India:** the British East India Company ruled about half of India directly. In **1857**, Muslim and Hindu soldiers in its army, called **sepoys**, revolted. After crushing the revolt, Parliament ended the Company's rule in **1858**, and the British government governed India directly. Many middle-class Indians later turned to political organizing instead of armed revolt.
- **Japan:** in **July 1853**, U.S. Commodore Matthew Perry arrived in Edo (Tokyo) Bay with gunships and forced Japan to open to trade. Japanese modernizers, remembering "China's humbling by Britain's navy in the first of the Opium Wars," overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate. In the **Meiji Restoration** (from 1868), the new government abolished class privileges, built a modern army, and led rapid industrialization; by 1872 a rail line linked Tokyo and Yokohama. Japan became the one non-Western state of the century to turn itself into a major industrial and imperial power.

### Resistance

Resistance took many forms: armed wars, diplomacy, religious movements, and political organizing. The most decisive military victory came at the **Battle of Adwa** in **1896**, where Emperor **Menelik II**'s much larger Ethiopian army defeated an Italian invasion. About six thousand Italians were killed, the peace treaty "reaffirmed Ethiopia's independence," and Adwa "largely marked the end of Italy's expansion in Africa." In Sudan, followers of the Mahdi defeated British efforts at control for years.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Weighing causes of Britain's industrial lead

**Question:** Why did industrialization begin in Britain?

| Factor | Evidence | How it helped |
|---|---|---|
| Energy and technology | Coal-fired steam engines; Watt's improved engines in British factories by 1776 | Machines could run anywhere, not only beside rivers |
| Capital | Profits from plantation products grown by enslaved people "were often invested" in factories | Money to build factories and machines |
| Ideas and policy | British leaders "embraced Smith's ideas" about free markets | Fewer restrictions on investment and trade |
| Markets and empire | Colonies and global trade (18.4) | Raw materials such as cotton, and buyers for cloth |

1. **Separate necessary from helpful causes:** without new energy and machines, no industrial revolution; without capital, few factories.
2. **Ask what was distinctive:** many places had trade or wealth. Britain combined all four factors at the same time.
3. **Avoid a single-cause answer:** "Britain had coal" is true, but other regions had coal too. Coal mattered because it combined with capital, ideas, and markets.
4. **Claim:** Britain industrialized first because it combined accessible energy and new technology with large pools of capital, partly from slavery and empire, and with policies favoring free markets.

### Example 2: Sourcing Commissioner Lin's letter (1839)

Lin Zexu, the Qing official sent to stop the opium trade, wrote to Queen Victoria in 1839:

> "We have heard that in your own country opium is prohibited with the utmost strictness and severity:—this is a strong proof that you know full well how hurtful it is to mankind. Since then you do not permit it to injure your own country, you ought not to have the injurious drug transferred to another."

1. **Author, audience, purpose:** a high Chinese official writing a public letter to a foreign monarch to stop the opium trade.
2. **The argument:** it appeals to consistency and conscience. If opium is harmful enough to ban in Britain, selling it to China is wrong.
3. **Context:** the letter also describes China as a "heavenly dynasty" generously allowing foreign trade, the same tribute-system worldview seen in the 1793 Qianlong letter (18.3).
4. **What happened:** Britain went to war rather than end the trade, and the 1842 Treaty of Nanjing opened treaty ports.
5. **Conclusion:** the letter shows how Qing officials understood the opium trade as a moral and legal wrong. It also shows that moral arguments alone could not stop an industrial power with a stronger navy.

### Example 3: Reading imperial ideology as evidence

In 1899, the English writer Rudyard Kipling wrote "The White Man's Burden" in response to the U.S. acquisition of the Philippines. One line describes colonized peoples as "Half devil and half child."

1. **What the source is:** a popular poem, not a government policy.
2. **What it shows:** the racist belief that colonized peoples were inferior and needed rule by White Westerners, which OpenStax connects to the civilizing mission and social Darwinism.
3. **Why historians use it:** it was widely read, so it is evidence of attitudes held by many readers in Britain and the United States.
4. **What it hides:** it presents empire as a burden on the colonizer. It says nothing about the Congo's forced rubber quotas, for example, or about colonized people's own views.
5. **Conclusion:** quote such sources as evidence of their authors' beliefs, not as descriptions of the people they insult.

### Example 4: Comparing responses to imperial pressure

| | China | Japan | Ethiopia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure | Opium trade and British navy | Perry's gunships, 1853 | Italian invasion after 1895 |
| Response | Tried to enforce bans; defeated in 1842 and again after 1856 | Overthrew the shogunate; rapid state-led industrialization from 1868 | Large, well-equipped army under Menelik II |
| Result | Forced treaty ports and foreign privileges | Became an industrial and imperial power | Independence reaffirmed after Adwa, 1896 |

1. **Similarity:** all three faced industrial powers with superior or threatening weapons.
2. **Difference:** Japan rapidly adopted industrial technology, and Ethiopia fielded a large modern army; China, weakened by internal problems, could not match British naval power in 1839–1842.
3. **Claim:** states that could quickly acquire industrial-era military power were most likely to keep their independence.
4. **Qualify it:** this explains outcomes, not moral worth, and many other factors (geography, internal unity, rivalries among European powers) also mattered.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Industrial Revolution happened because of one invention."** It depended on many inventions plus energy, capital, markets, and policy.
- **"Industrialization made everyone richer right away."** It created wealth for owners and the middle class while many workers faced long hours, danger, and crowded housing. Living standards for workers improved only gradually and unevenly.
- **"Imperialism was only about money."** Economic motives were central, but prestige, military rivalry, religion, and racist ideology also drove it.
- **"Africans and Asians were passive."** People resisted in many ways, from the 1857 revolt in India to Ethiopia's victory at Adwa and political organizing by educated Indians.
- **"The Congo was a unique exception."** Its abuses were extreme, but OpenStax notes it "differed from most other colonies largely in degree, not in kind."
- **"Japan avoided Western influence."** Japan adopted Western technology, military methods, and industry quickly in order to stay independent, and then built its own empire.
- **Boundary: "90 percent of Africa" describes claims.** European control on maps was often weaker on the ground, especially far from coasts and railways, and resistance continued for decades.
