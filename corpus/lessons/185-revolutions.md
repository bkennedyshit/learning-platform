# 18.5 - Revolutions

Between 1775 and the 1820s, a wave of revolutions swept the Atlantic world. British colonists in North America declared independence. The French overthrew a monarchy that had ruled for centuries. Enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue destroyed slavery and founded Haiti. Across Spanish and Portuguese America, new nations replaced colonies. Many revolutionaries used the same language of natural rights and popular sovereignty, but they disagreed sharply about whom those rights included. In the nineteenth century, a new force, **nationalism**, unified Italy and Germany and reshaped Europe.

This lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its guiding question comes from the OpenStax textbook: **"Revolution for whom?"** You will practice **comparison** and **causation** by asking, for each revolution, who led it, why, and who gained rights.

## Prerequisites

- [18.4 - Transoceanic Connections](/184-transoceanic-connections), especially plantation slavery in the Caribbean and the colonial societies of Spanish America.
- Helpful: [19.2 - Revolution & Early Republic](/192-revolution-early-republic), which covers the American Revolution in detail. This lesson treats it briefly and focuses on the other revolutions.
- The terms **monarchy** (rule by a king or queen) and **republic** (a state without a monarch, governed by elected representatives).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain the Enlightenment ideas of **natural rights**, the **social contract**, and the **separation of powers**, linking each to a thinker (Locke, Rousseau, Montesquieu).
2. Describe the main **phases of the French Revolution** from 1789 to Napoleon's rise in 1799 and analyze the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** as a primary source.
3. Explain the causes, course, and results of the **Haitian Revolution** (1791–1804), including why historians consider it unique.
4. **Compare** the American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions by asking who gained rights and who did not, and explain the rise of **nationalism** in the nineteenth century.

## Core ideas

### Enlightenment ideas

The **Enlightenment** was an eighteenth-century intellectual movement that applied reason to government, religion, and society. Three ideas mattered most for revolutions:

| Idea | Thinker | Key work | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Natural rights** | John Locke (England) | *Two Treatises of Government* (Second Treatise published 1690) | People have fundamental rights "to life, liberty, and property" that governments must protect |
| **Separation of powers** | Montesquieu (France) | *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) | Dividing government power among separate branches protects political liberty |
| **Social contract** | Jean-Jacques Rousseau (Geneva/France) | *The Social Contract* | Legitimate government rests on an agreement among the people; sovereignty belongs to them |

Voltaire, another French writer, mocked established religion and government in the satire *Candide* (1759) and defended religious tolerance.

OpenStax points out a contradiction: many Enlightenment thinkers proclaimed universal rights while, "in practice," they "supported the unequal institutions of colonialism and the Atlantic slave trade."

### The American Revolution, briefly

Twelve of the thirteen colonies approved the Declaration of Independence in 1776 (New York abstained), and the Continental Army under George Washington eventually defeated Britain. The new republic's achievements were "inherently contradictory": political liberty "coexisted with the institution of slavery," and full rights extended "only to White men of property." The American example influenced revolutionaries in France and Latin America.

### The French Revolution: phases

| Phase | Years | Key events |
|---|---|---|
| **Moderate (liberal)** | 1789–1791 | Parisians capture the **Bastille** on July 14, 1789; the National Assembly issues the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** (August 1789); a 1791 constitution makes France a constitutional monarchy |
| **Radical** | 1792–1794 | France becomes a republic and executes Louis XVI; the **Committee of Public Safety**, led by **Maximilien Robespierre**, rules from 1793 to 1794 during the **Reign of Terror**; slavery is abolished in French colonies in **1794**; the Terror ends in July 1794 when Robespierre is executed |
| **Directory and Napoleon** | 1795–1815 | The Directory invites General **Napoleon Bonaparte** to strengthen the government in **1799**; Napoleon keeps some revolutionary gains, **reinstates slavery** in the colonies, declares himself emperor in 1804, and is eventually defeated after failed campaigns, including the invasion of Russia |

The radical phase combined real reforms (price controls on grain and legalized divorce, for example) with mass repression of dissent. In 1791, the playwright **Olympe de Gouges** published the *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen*, criticizing the 1789 Declaration "for failing to address women's rights."

### The Haitian Revolution

**Saint-Domingue**, on the western part of Hispaniola, was France's wealthiest colony. It produced "roughly 40 percent of the sugar and nearly half the coffee imported to Europe." Its society in 1789:

| Group | Approximate size | Goals in 1789–1791 |
|---|---|---|
| Enslaved people, mostly of African birth or descent | about 500,000 | Freedom |
| Whites (planters, professionals, poor laborers) | about 40,000 | Wealthy planters: autonomy and free trade; poor Whites: equal citizenship for themselves |
| *Gens de couleur libres* (free people of color) | about 30,000 | Equal rights for all free people regardless of race; some owned enslaved people and did not call for abolition |

**Course of the revolution:**

1. **1791:** conflict broke out after White colonists refused to recognize rights France had granted to wealthy people of color. In August, enslaved rebels met at **Bois Caïman** in a Vodou ceremony led by **Dutty Boukman**, and an uprising spread through the north; by September it reached Port-au-Prince.
2. **1792–1793:** France sent troops and extended citizenship to all free men of color; Britain and Spain, at war with France, landed troops to support the White colonists.
3. **1794:** France abolished slavery.
4. **1801:** **Toussaint Louverture**, a formerly enslaved military leader, freed the enslaved people across Hispaniola and promoted a constitution that made him governor-general for life, while keeping Haiti part of the French Empire. He also forced formerly enslaved peasants to keep working in the sugarcane fields.
5. **1802:** Napoleon sent an army to restore French control and slavery; Louverture was arrested and died in a French prison.
6. **January 1, 1804:** **Jean-Jacques Dessalines** declared independence. The country took the name *Haiti*, from a Taíno word meaning "mountainous."

OpenStax calls it "the first uprising of enslaved people in history that not only toppled a colonial regime but also established national independence." Independence had a heavy price: in 1825 France imposed an "exorbitant independence debt" that devastated the new country.

### Latin American independence

In Spanish America, society was ranked by birth and ancestry. Men born in Spain, called **peninsulares**, held the highest offices in church and state; American-born men of European descent, called **creoles**, made up most of the landed elite and resented their exclusion. When Napoleon invaded Spain in 1808, colonial elites set up their own governing councils (*juntas*), and independence movements followed:

- **Mexico:** in 1810 the creole priest **Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla** began a movement for independence, appealing to the poor and to the Virgin of Guadalupe. Mexico won independence in 1821.
- **South America:** **Simón Bolívar** in the north and **José de San Martín** in the south led wars that created several independent republics in the 1810s and 1820s.
- **Brazil:** independence came "relatively peacefully" in September 1822 under Pedro I, a Portuguese-born ruler, as a constitutional monarchy that "did not challenge colonial hierarchies." Brazil remained one nation while Spanish America split into many.

### Nineteenth-century nationalism

**Nationalism** is the belief that people who share a language, culture, and history should form their own nation-state. After Napoleon's defeat, the **Congress of Vienna** (1814–1815) tried to restore monarchies and a balance of power, but nationalism kept growing.

- **Italy:** Giuseppe Mazzini promoted unity; Giuseppe Garibaldi fought for it; and the kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, under Victor Emmanuel II and his prime minister Cavour, led it. Italy was unified as a kingdom in **1861**.
- **Germany:** Prussia's conservative prime minister **Otto von Bismarck** (appointed 1862) unified Germany through wars, ending with the German Empire in **1871**. OpenStax describes this as "the triumph of militarism and authoritarianism," a contrast with Mazzini's democratic nationalism.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading the Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789)

> "1. Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. ... 3. The principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation. ... 6. Law is the expression of the general will. Every citizen has a right to participate personally, or through his representative, in its foundation."

1. **Meet the document:** issued by the National Assembly in August 1789, early in the revolution.
2. **Observe:** Article 2 names the natural rights as "liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression." Article 3 moves sovereignty from the king to "the nation." Article 6 uses Rousseau's phrase "general will."
3. **Connect to ideas:** Locke (natural rights, property), Rousseau (general will, popular sovereignty), and Montesquieu's concern with limiting power all appear.
4. **Test its scope:** the text says "men" and "citizens." Olympe de Gouges's 1791 response shows contemporaries noticed that women were not included, and France kept slavery in its colonies except for 1794–1802.
5. **Use as evidence:** the Declaration is strong evidence of the revolution's **ideals** in 1789. It is weak evidence of how rights were actually applied.

### Example 2: Weighing causes of the Haitian Revolution

**Question:** Was the Haitian Revolution caused mainly by Enlightenment and French revolutionary ideas?

1. **Evidence for:** news of the French Revolution reached the colony in 1789; delegates went to Paris; free people of color used "the revolutionary rhetoric of liberty" to claim equal rights.
2. **Evidence for other causes:** OpenStax argues that "much of the inspiration actually came from rumors that France had outlawed slavery, the existence of enslaved leaders poised to rebel," and Vodou, which "united different rebel groups."
3. **Underlying condition:** a brutal plantation system in which about 500,000 enslaved people were ruled by about 40,000 Whites.
4. **Trigger:** the 1791 conflict between Whites and free people of color, which created instability.
5. **Claim:** French revolutionary ideas and events created an **opening**, but enslaved people's own leadership, networks, and religion drove the revolution. A strong answer distinguishes **underlying causes**, **triggers**, and **motivations**.

### Example 3: Sourcing a revolutionary leader's defense

After his arrest in 1802, Toussaint Louverture wrote memoirs in a French prison to answer charges of treason:

> "If I did oblige my fellow-countrymen to work; it was to teach them the value of true liberty without license ... Doubtless, I owe this treatment to my color."

1. **Author and situation:** a prisoner defending himself to French authorities.
2. **Purpose:** to justify his policies, including forced labor, and to show loyalty to the French Republic.
3. **What it shows:** how Louverture explained forced labor (as protecting "true liberty" and the colony's prosperity) and his belief that racism shaped his treatment.
4. **What to check:** a self-defense is likely to present the author favorably. Formerly enslaved workers who were forced back into the cane fields might have described the same policy very differently.
5. **Conclusion:** the memoir is excellent evidence of Louverture's reasoning and point of view, but not neutral evidence of how his policies affected others.

### Example 4: "Revolution for whom?" A four-way comparison

| Revolution | Led mainly by | Who gained rights | Who was excluded or lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| American (1775–1783) | Colonial elites | White men of property | Enslaved people, women, Native Americans |
| French (1789–1799) | Middle-class and urban radicals | Male citizens (with changes over time) | Women; enslaved people in the colonies after 1802 |
| Haitian (1791–1804) | Enslaved and formerly enslaved people | All citizens regardless of race; slavery abolished | Women could not vote; peasants faced forced labor; the new state was burdened by the 1825 debt |
| Spanish America (1810–1820s) | Creole elites | Creoles replaced peninsulares at the top | Racial hierarchies largely remained |

1. **Similarity:** all four used ideas of popular sovereignty and liberty.
2. **Main difference:** only the Haitian Revolution abolished slavery and granted citizenship regardless of race, because enslaved people themselves led it.
3. **Claim:** the more a revolution was led by people at the bottom of the social order, the more it changed that order.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"All revolutionaries wanted equality for everyone."** Most leaders in the American, French, and Spanish American revolutions sought rights for some groups, not all. Asking "rights for whom?" reveals the differences.
- **"The French Revolution was one event in 1789."** It passed through moderate, radical, and Napoleonic phases over a decade, and its policies on slavery and rights changed more than once.
- **"The Haitian Revolution simply copied the French Revolution."** French events opened opportunities, but enslaved leaders, rumors of abolition, and Vodou networks drove it.
- **"Haiti was the only slave revolt ever to succeed."** Many revolts of enslaved people occurred in history. The precise claim is that Haiti was "the first uprising of enslaved people in history that not only toppled a colonial regime but also established national independence."
- **"Independence in Latin America changed society completely."** Creoles replaced peninsulares at the top, but racial and class hierarchies largely continued, and Brazil kept a monarchy.
- **"Nationalism was always democratic."** Mazzini's nationalism emphasized popular sovereignty, but Germany was unified through Bismarck's wars and authoritarian rule.
- **Boundary: "Enlightenment" ideas were debated, not uniform.** Thinkers disagreed with one another, and some defended slavery or excluded women while praising liberty.
