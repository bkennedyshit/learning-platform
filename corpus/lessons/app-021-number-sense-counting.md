# APP-02.1 — Number Sense & Counting

Number sense is knowing what numbers mean: how many things a number stands for, which number is bigger, and how a number can be broken into parts. This first lesson of the Early Numeracy course builds that sense with real objects you can touch and move. Every later lesson, from adding to fractions, depends on it.

This lesson has two audiences. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the parent, tutor, or teacher sitting beside them.

## Prerequisites

The learner should be able to say the number words from one to ten in order, even if they sometimes slip, and be willing to touch and move small objects. No reading or writing of numerals is required at the start; writing numerals is practised during the lesson.

> **Adult note:** Gather 20 to 30 small, safe objects of the same kind (counters, buttons, dry beans, or blocks), a paper ten frame (a 2 × 5 grid of boxes), and about 12 craft sticks with rubber bands for bundling tens. Large objects are better than tiny ones for young children.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Count a group of up to 20 objects, touching or moving each object exactly once, and answer "How many?" with the last number said.
2. Count on from a number other than 1, up to 120, and skip count by 2s, 5s, and 10s.
3. Build a two-digit number from tens and ones, and compare two numbers with >, <, or =.
4. Say how many dots are on a ten frame without counting one by one, and show two different ways to make 10.

## Core ideas

### Three rules of counting

Counting looks easy, but it uses three separate ideas:

- **Same order every time.** The number words always go one, two, three, four, and so on. Skipping a word or repeating one gives the wrong answer.
- **One number for each thing.** Each object gets exactly one number word. Touching an object, or sliding it to a new pile, helps you not to count it twice or miss it.
- **The last number tells how many.** When you count five apples, "five" is not the name of the last apple. It tells you how many apples there are in the whole group.

The total stays the same if you move the objects around, spread them out, or start counting from a different object.

### Counting past 10 and up to 120

After nine comes a new ten: 10, 20, 30. Each **decade** (a group of ten numbers) follows the same pattern: twenty-one, twenty-two, … twenty-nine, then **thirty**. The hardest places are where the tens change: 29 → 30, 39 → 40, 99 → 100, and 109 → 110. After 100 the pattern starts again: one hundred one, one hundred two, … one hundred twenty.

**Counting on** means starting from a number you already know instead of starting at 1. If you have 7 blocks and get 3 more, you can say "seven… eight, nine, ten."

**Skip counting** means counting in equal jumps: by 2s (2, 4, 6, 8, 10), by 5s (5, 10, 15, 20) and by 10s (10, 20, 30, 40). Skip counting by 10s from any number keeps the ones digit the same: 4, 14, 24, 34.

### Tens and ones

When we have 10 single objects, we can tie them into one bundle called a **ten**. The number 34 means 3 tens and 4 ones. The left digit tells how many tens; the right digit tells how many ones. That is why 34 and 43 are different numbers even though they use the same digits.

### Comparing numbers

To compare two numbers, compare the tens first. The number with more tens is greater. If the tens are the same, compare the ones. We write the result with symbols:

- $>$ means "is greater than" (for example, 43 > 34)
- $<$ means "is less than" (for example, 34 < 43)
- $=$ means "is the same amount as" (for example, 10 = 10)

The open side of $>$ or $<$ faces the bigger number.

### Seeing amounts at a glance

**Subitizing** means knowing how many are in a small group just by looking, without counting one by one. A ten frame makes this easier: a full top row is 5, so 7 dots on a ten frame can be seen as "5 and 2 more." Dice patterns work the same way.

### Number bonds: numbers have parts

A **number bond** shows a whole split into two parts. Ten can be 7 and 3, 6 and 4, or 5 and 5. Knowing the ways to make 10 is one of the most useful number facts for the next lesson, [APP-02.2 — Addition & Subtraction](/app-022-addition-subtraction).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Counting a messy pile

*Illustrative example.* Twelve buttons are scattered on a table. The learner says "twelve" but actually skipped two buttons.

1. Make an empty "counted" area on the table.
2. Slide one button at a time into the counted area while saying one number word for it: one, two, three, …
3. Stop when no buttons are left. The last word said was "fourteen."
4. Ask, "How many buttons are there?" The answer is 14, because the last number counted tells how many.
5. Check: arrange the buttons as one full ten frame (10) and 4 extra. Ten and 4 is 14, so the count is confirmed.

Moving each button solved the problem of skipping. The ten frame gave a second way to check.

### Example 2: Building and comparing 34 and 43

1. Build 34: take 3 bundles of ten sticks and 4 loose sticks. Count the bundles by tens, "10, 20, 30," then count on the loose ones, "31, 32, 33, 34."
2. Build 43: take 4 bundles and 3 loose sticks. "10, 20, 30, 40, 41, 42, 43."
3. Compare the tens: 4 tens is more than 3 tens. It does not matter that 4 ones is more than 3 ones, because a single ten is worth more than all the ones together here.
4. Write it: 43 > 34, or 34 < 43.

### Example 3: Ways to make 10 on a ten frame

1. Put 7 red counters on a ten frame: a full top row of 5 and 2 more on the bottom row.
2. Without counting, the learner sees "5 and 2, that's 7."
3. How many empty boxes? The bottom row has 3 empty boxes, so 7 and 3 make 10.
4. Now move one red counter off and fill in with yellow: 6 red and 4 yellow. Another number bond for 10 is 6 and 4.
5. Record the bonds: 10 = 7 + 3, 10 = 6 + 4, 10 = 5 + 5.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **Counting the same object twice or missing one.** This usually happens with scattered objects. Moving each object into a "counted" pile fixes it.
- **Re-counting from 1 after being asked "How many?"** If a child counts 8 cars and, when asked how many, counts again, they may not yet see that the last number is the total. Ask "How many?" right after the count and accept "eight" as the answer.
- **Thinking spreading things out makes more.** A long row of 6 coins is still 6 coins. Push the row together and count again to show the amount did not change.
- **Mixing up 14 and 41, or writing "one hundred five" as 1005.** Use tens bundles: 14 is 1 ten and 4 ones, 41 is 4 tens and 1 one. One hundred five is 1 hundred, 0 tens, 5 ones: 105.
- **Stalling at decade changes**, such as saying "twenty-nine, twenty-ten." Practise the "next ten" by counting bundles: after 2 tens and 9 ones, one more stick makes a new bundle, 3 tens, thirty.
- **Turning the comparison symbol the wrong way.** Remind the learner that the open side faces the bigger number, and read the sentence aloud from left to right.

## Practice

1. Put 17 counters in a scattered pile. Count them by moving each one. How many are there? Check with a ten frame.
2. Count on from 96 to 112, out loud. Which two numbers come right after 99 and 109?
3. Skip count by 5s from 5 to 50, then by 10s from 3 to 93.
4. Build 52 and 25 with tens bundles and loose sticks. Write the comparison using > or <.
5. Fill in >, <, or =: 67 __ 76, 80 __ 79, 45 __ 45.
6. A ten frame shows 8 dots. How many more dots make 10? Write the number bond.
7. Show three different ways to split 9 into two parts.
