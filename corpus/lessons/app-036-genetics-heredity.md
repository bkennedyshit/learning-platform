# APP-03.6 — Genetics & Heredity

Why do kittens in one litter look different from each other but still resemble their parents? Why can two parents with dark hair have a child with light hair? **Heredity** is the passing of traits from parents to offspring, and **genetics** is the science that studies it. In the 1850s and 1860s, Gregor Mendel worked out the basic rules by breeding nearly 30,000 pea plants, long before anyone knew what DNA was. This lesson connects Mendel's rules to DNA and chromosomes and shows how to predict offspring with a Punnett square.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 7-8.

## Core ideas

### DNA, genes, and chromosomes

- **DNA** is the molecule that carries hereditary instructions. Its information is stored in the order of four chemical bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and thymine (T), much as the order of letters spells words. Human DNA has about 3 billion bases, and more than 99 percent of them are the same in all people.
- A **gene** is a section of DNA that holds the instructions for a particular product, often a protein. The Human Genome Project estimated 20,000 to 25,000 human protein-coding genes; later work puts the number at about 19,900.
- A **chromosome** is a long, tightly packaged strand of DNA. Human cells normally have **23 pairs**, or **46 chromosomes**. Twenty-two pairs are **autosomes**. The 23rd pair is the **sex chromosomes**: typically XX in females and XY in males.

You get one chromosome of each pair from each parent. So for most genes you have **two copies**, one from each parent.

### Alleles, genotype, and phenotype

Different versions of the same gene are called **alleles**. Scientists write alleles with letters: a capital letter for a **dominant** allele and the lowercase letter for a **recessive** allele.

- **Dominant:** shows its trait even if only one copy is present.
- **Recessive:** shows its trait only when both copies are the recessive allele.
- **Genotype:** the pair of alleles an organism has, such as Vv.
- **Phenotype:** the trait you can observe, such as violet flowers.
- **Homozygous:** two identical alleles (VV or vv).
- **Heterozygous:** two different alleles (Vv).

In Mendel's peas, violet flowers (V) are dominant to white flowers (v):

| Genotype | Description | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| VV | homozygous dominant | violet |
| Vv | heterozygous | violet |
| vv | homozygous recessive | white |

### Mendel's results

Mendel crossed true-breeding violet plants with true-breeding white plants. All of the first generation (F₁) had violet flowers; the white trait seemed to disappear. When he let the F₁ plants self-fertilize, the white trait reappeared in the second generation (F₂): **705 violet and 224 white**, a ratio of about **3.15 to 1**, close to **3:1**. He found similar 3:1 patterns for seven different pea traits. He presented his work in 1865 and published it in 1866.

### Punnett squares

A **Punnett square** lists the possible alleles from one parent across the top and from the other parent down the side. Each box shows one possible offspring genotype. With one gene and four boxes, each box is an equally likely outcome with probability 1/4. A Punnett square gives **probabilities**, not guaranteed counts.

### Beyond simple dominance

- **Incomplete dominance:** the heterozygote has an in-between phenotype. In snapdragons, red-flowered plants (CᴿCᴿ) crossed with white-flowered plants (CᵂCᵂ) give **pink** offspring (CᴿCᵂ).
- **X-linked traits:** some genes are on the X chromosome but not the Y. Males (XY) have only one X, so a single recessive allele on that X shows its trait. Females (XX) need two copies. Thomas Hunt Morgan identified one of the first X-linked traits, eye color in fruit flies, in 1910. In people, red-green color vision deficiency is X-linked; among people of Northern European ancestry it affects about 1 in 12 males and 1 in 200 females. A father passes his X chromosome to his daughters and his Y to his sons, so fathers cannot pass X-linked traits to their sons.

### Where variation comes from

Offspring of the same parents differ because each child gets a different random combination of each parent's chromosomes, and occasionally because of new changes in DNA called **mutations**. Many traits, such as height, are influenced by many genes *and* by the environment, such as nutrition, so they do not follow simple one-gene ratios.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A monohybrid cross, Vv × Vv

Two heterozygous violet pea plants are crossed.

1. Each parent can pass on **V** or **v**.
2. Fill the square:

| | V | v |
|---|---|---|
| **V** | VV | Vv |
| **v** | Vv | vv |

3. **Genotypes:** 1 VV : 2 Vv : 1 vv. As fractions: 1/4 VV, 2/4 = 1/2 Vv, 1/4 vv.
4. **Phenotypes:** VV and Vv are violet (3 boxes); vv is white (1 box). Expected ratio **3 violet : 1 white**, or 75 percent violet and 25 percent white.
5. **Compare with Mendel:** 705 ÷ 224 ≈ 3.15. Out of 929 plants, 3/4 would be about 697 violet and 232 white. His real counts were close to, but not exactly, the prediction, which is what we expect from chance.

### Example 2: A test cross

A violet plant could be VV or Vv. To find out, cross it with a white plant (vv), which can only pass on **v**.

1. **If the violet plant is VV:** every offspring gets V from it and v from the white parent, so all are Vv: **100 percent violet.**
2. **If the violet plant is Vv:**

| | V | v |
|---|---|---|
| **v** | Vv | vv |
| **v** | Vv | vv |

   Half are Vv (violet) and half are vv (white): **1 violet : 1 white.**
3. **Interpret:** if any white offspring appear, the violet parent must carry v, so it is Vv. If many offspring are all violet, the parent is very likely VV.

### Example 3: Incomplete dominance in snapdragons

Two pink snapdragons (CᴿCᵂ × CᴿCᵂ) are crossed.

| | Cᴿ | Cᵂ |
|---|---|---|
| **Cᴿ** | CᴿCᴿ | CᴿCᵂ |
| **Cᵂ** | CᴿCᵂ | CᵂCᵂ |

1. Genotypes: 1 CᴿCᴿ : 2 CᴿCᵂ : 1 CᵂCᵂ.
2. Phenotypes: CᴿCᴿ red, CᴿCᵂ pink, CᵂCᵂ white, so **1 red : 2 pink : 1 white**.
3. Notice: with incomplete dominance, the phenotype ratio matches the genotype ratio, because each genotype looks different.
4. Boundary: pink is not a "blend" that is permanent. The red and white alleles stay separate and can reappear in the next generation, as this cross shows.

### Example 4: An X-linked recessive trait

Let Xᴮ be the allele for typical color vision and Xᵇ the recessive allele for red-green color vision deficiency. A mother with typical vision who carries one recessive allele (XᴮXᵇ) has children with a father with typical vision (XᴮY).

| | Xᴮ (from father) | Y (from father) |
|---|---|---|
| **Xᴮ (from mother)** | XᴮXᴮ | XᴮY |
| **Xᵇ (from mother)** | XᴮXᵇ | XᵇY |

1. **Daughters** (XX boxes): XᴮXᴮ or XᴮXᵇ. Every daughter gets her father's Xᴮ, so **no daughters** have the condition; half are expected to be carriers.
2. **Sons** (XY boxes): XᴮY or XᵇY. Sons get their only X from their mother, so each son has a **1/2 chance** of the condition.
3. This explains why X-linked recessive traits show up more often in males: one recessive copy is enough in a male.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Dominant means more common."** Dominant describes which allele shows its trait in a heterozygote, not how common the allele is. A dominant allele can be rare in a population, and a recessive one can be very common.
- **"A 3:1 ratio means a family with four children will have exactly three with the dominant trait."** Each child is a separate chance event. A Punnett square predicts probabilities; real counts vary, especially in small families.
- **"Each trait is controlled by one gene."** Some traits, like pea flower color, follow one gene. Many human traits, like height and skin color, depend on many genes plus the environment.
- **"Traits you develop during life are inherited."** A parent's muscles from training or a scar from an injury are not passed on, because they do not change the DNA in eggs or sperm.
- **"Recessive traits always skip a generation."** A recessive trait can show up in any generation where a child gets two recessive alleles, or in no generation for a long time. There is no fixed pattern of skipping.
- **"Genes are only in humans."** Every living thing has DNA and genes, including bacteria and plants. Mendel did his work with peas, and Morgan with fruit flies.

## Practice

1. How many chromosomes are in a typical human body cell, and how many pairs?
2. In pea plants, tall (T) is dominant to short (t). Give the phenotype of TT, Tt, and tt.
3. Complete a Punnett square for Tt × tt. What fraction of offspring are expected to be short?
4. Two Tt plants are crossed and produce 200 offspring. About how many do you expect to be tall?
5. A breeder wants to know whether a tall plant is TT or Tt. Describe a test cross and how to read its result.
6. A red snapdragon (CᴿCᴿ) is crossed with a pink one (CᴿCᵂ). What phenotypes are expected, and in what ratio?
7. A woman with red-green color vision deficiency (XᵇXᵇ) and a man with typical vision (XᴮY) have a son. What is the chance the son has the condition? Explain.
8. Explain the difference between genotype and phenotype using eye color or flower color as an example.
