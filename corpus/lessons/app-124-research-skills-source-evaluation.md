# APP-12.4 — Research Skills & Source Evaluation

**Research** in a writing course means finding, judging, and recording sources so that your claims rest on evidence a reader can check. Good research starts with a question, not a topic; it uses search tools deliberately; and it evaluates each source before trusting it. A fast check of who is behind a source often matters more than a careful reading of the source itself: in a 2019 study, professional fact checkers judged unfamiliar websites more accurately than historians and students because they left the site to see what others said about it.

This lesson covers research questions, search strategies, kinds of sources, source evaluation, note-taking, and annotated bibliographies. It is written for high school students preparing research papers.

## Prerequisites

[APP-12.3 — Evidence, Analysis & Integration](/app-123-evidence-analysis-integration): you should know the CRAAP criteria, the difference between quoting and paraphrasing, and why notes must keep copied wording in quotation marks.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Narrow a topic into a **research question** and turn it into keyword searches.
2. Use library databases and **Google Scholar** features to find scholarly sources.
3. Classify sources as **scholarly** or **popular** and as **primary**, **secondary**, or **tertiary** for a given question.
4. Evaluate a source with **CRAAP** and **lateral reading**, take traceable notes, and write an **annotated bibliography** entry.

## Core ideas

### From topic to research question

A **topic** is a subject (*social media and news*). A **research question** is something specific you can investigate and answer with evidence (*How do high school students decide whether a news post on social media is trustworthy?*). Good research questions are:

- **Focused** enough to answer in the length you have.
- **Open**, meaning not answerable with yes/no or a single fact.
- **Researchable** with sources you can actually get.
- **Arguable**, so the answer can become a thesis.

Expect to adjust your question as you learn. If you find almost nothing, broaden it; if you find thousands of results on every aspect, narrow it by population, place, time period, or angle.

### Search strategies

1. **List key concepts** from the question: *high school students*, *news*, *social media*, *trust* or *credibility*.
2. **Brainstorm synonyms** for each: *teenagers*, *adolescents*; *misinformation*, *news literacy*; *credibility*, *evaluation*.
3. **Combine terms.** Many library databases accept **Boolean operators**: AND narrows (*adolescents AND misinformation*), OR broadens (*teenagers OR adolescents*), and NOT excludes. Quotation marks search for an exact phrase (*"news literacy"*). Check each database's help page, because syntax varies.
4. **Mine good sources.** A useful article's reference list points to earlier work; its keywords suggest new search terms.

### Library databases and Google Scholar

A **library database** is a searchable collection of articles, books, or other records that a library provides. Many let you filter results to **scholarly** or **peer-reviewed** publications; UC Berkeley's library guide recommends looking for that checkbox. Your school or public library may provide databases free with a library card.

**Google Scholar** searches scholarly literature across the web. Its official help page explains several features:

| Feature | How | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Author search | `author:"d knuth"` | find work by a specific researcher |
| Title search | put the title in quotation marks | locate a known article |
| Recent work | click "Since Year" in the sidebar, or "Sort by date" | focus on current research |
| Cited by | link under a result | find newer papers that cite it |
| Related articles | link under a result | find similar work |
| All versions | link under a result | find another copy, sometimes a free one |

Scholar's help page notes that abstracts are freely available for most articles but "reading the entire article may require a subscription"; it suggests library links, a "[PDF]" link, or "All versions." It also suggests that if you are new to a subject, you can pick up terminology from secondary sources, giving the example that a Wikipedia article might suggest better search terms. That makes encyclopedias a good starting point for vocabulary, even when they are not sources you cite.

### Kinds of sources

**Scholarly vs. popular** (adapted from UC Berkeley Library):

| | Scholarly | Popular |
|---|---|---|
| Authors | experts such as scientists, faculty, historians | generalists such as journalists, staff writers, bloggers; not always named |
| Before publication | evaluated by peers (other scholars) | edited by in-house editors, or not edited |
| Citations | bibliographies, footnotes, a formal style | usually no formal citations |
| Audience | specialists | general readers |
| Purpose | communicating research findings | news, entertainment, general information |

A **peer-reviewed** journal is one in which, in Berkeley's words, "a group of widely acknowledged experts in a field reviews the content for scholarly soundness and academic value" before publication. Peer review reduces errors; it does not guarantee that every finding is correct or permanent.

**Primary, secondary, tertiary:**

- **Primary sources** were created during the time studied or by a participant (Berkeley), or are firsthand accounts and original data (Purdue OWL): letters, diaries, speeches, government documents, original research articles, data sets, works of literature and art.
- **Secondary sources** interpret or analyze primary sources: scholarly books and articles about an event or text, biographies, reviews of research.
- **Tertiary sources** "summarize and interpret other resources" (Berkeley): encyclopedias, dictionaries, textbooks. They are good starting points for unfamiliar topics.

The category depends on your question. A 1933 newspaper editorial is a secondary account of the Great Depression but a primary source if your question is how newspapers reacted to Roosevelt's inauguration.

### Evaluating sources: CRAAP and lateral reading

The **CRAAP Test** from CSU Chico's Meriam Library (Lesson 12.3) asks about Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, and Purpose, mostly by examining the source itself.

**Lateral reading** examines the source from the outside. In Wineburg and McGrew's study of 10 historians, 10 professional fact checkers, and 25 Stanford undergraduates, historians and students tended to read **vertically**, staying on a website to judge it, and were often misled by "official-looking logos and domain names." The fact checkers read **laterally**: after a quick scan, they opened new tabs to find out what other sources said about the site, and they reached more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time.

Mike Caulfield's **SIFT** turns lateral reading into four moves:

1. **Stop.** Before reading or sharing, ask whether you know the source and its reputation.
2. **Investigate the source.** Spend a minute finding out who is behind it, using other sites rather than the source's own "About" page alone.
3. **Find better coverage.** If you care about the claim more than this particular article, look for trusted reporting or analysis of the claim.
4. **Trace claims, quotes, and media to the original context.** Find the original study, full speech, or unedited image, because much online content has been stripped of context.

CRAAP and SIFT work well together: SIFT tells you quickly whether a source deserves your time; CRAAP helps you judge one you plan to use.

### Note-taking you can trace

- Keep a **source log**: full citation information for every source the moment you open it.
- For every note, record the source and the **page, section, or paragraph**.
- Put **quotation marks** around any wording you copy, and label your own ideas (for example, start them with *Me:*).
- Organize notes by **subtopic or question**, not just by source, so you can see where sources agree and disagree.

### Annotated bibliographies

A **bibliography** lists sources; an **annotated bibliography** adds a paragraph after each citation. According to Purdue OWL, an annotation may **summarize** (main argument and scope), **assess** (reliability, usefulness, bias, comparison with other sources), and **reflect** (how the source fits your project). Lengths vary from a couple of sentences to a couple of pages; follow your assignment.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Narrowing a topic and building a search

**Topic:** *misinformation.*

1. **Too broad:** *How does misinformation affect society?* Thousands of angles.
2. **Narrow by population and behavior:** *How do high school students decide whether a news post on social media is trustworthy?*
3. **Key concepts and synonyms:** (high school students OR teenagers OR adolescents), (news OR information), (credibility OR trustworthiness OR evaluation), (social media).
4. **Database search:** *(teenagers OR adolescents) AND "social media" AND (credibility OR evaluation)*.
5. **Scholar follow-up:** search *lateral reading fact checkers*; open the Wineburg and McGrew article; click **Cited by** to find newer research that builds on it.

Reasoning: each concept gets its own synonym group joined by OR, and the groups are joined by AND so every result must touch all concepts.

### Example 2: Reading laterally

**Illustrative example, not a real organization:** you find a polished site for the "National Institute for Healthy Screens" claiming that a particular phone app improves teenagers' grades. It has a *.org* address, a logo with a seal, and an "About Us" page describing it as independent.

1. **Stop.** You have never heard of it, and the claim is surprising.
2. **Investigate the source.** In a new tab, search the organization's name plus words like *funding* or *who runs*. Suppose reputable reporting shows it is run by the company that sells the app. The domain and seal told you nothing reliable.
3. **Find better coverage.** Search the claim itself. Look for research summaries from universities, government agencies, or established news outlets.
4. **Trace the claim.** The site mentions "a study," so find the original. Check who conducted it and what it actually measured.
5. **Decision:** do not use the site as evidence. If the original study is credible, cite the study, not the site's summary of it.

### Example 3: Classifying sources for a specific question

**Question:** *How did readers respond to* Frankenstein *when it was first published?*

| Source | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| An 1818 magazine review of the novel | primary, popular | created at the time by a reader responding to the book |
| The novel itself (Project Gutenberg text) | primary | the work being responded to |
| A peer-reviewed article analyzing early reviews | secondary, scholarly | interprets the primary reviews |
| An encyclopedia entry on Mary Shelley | tertiary | summarizes other sources; useful for background and vocabulary |

For a different question, such as *How does the novel present responsibility?*, the reviews would become secondary context and the novel would be the central primary source.

### Example 4: Writing an annotated bibliography entry

**Citation (APA):**

> Wineburg, S., & McGrew, S. (2019). Lateral reading and the nature of expertise: Reading less and learning more when evaluating digital information. *Teachers College Record*, *121*(11), 1–40. https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102

**Annotation:**

> *Summarize:* Wineburg and McGrew asked 10 historians, 10 professional fact checkers, and 25 Stanford undergraduates to think aloud while evaluating live websites on social and political issues. Historians and students tended to stay on a site and were misled by features such as logos and domain names, while fact checkers left the site to investigate it and reached better-supported conclusions faster. *Assess:* The study is peer-reviewed and directly compares skilled groups, but its sample is small and it does not test a classroom lesson. *Reflect:* It supports my argument that students should be taught to read laterally, but I will need classroom research to argue that teaching works.

Reasoning: each part of the annotation does a different job, and the assessment states a limit that will shape how the source is used. **Note:** this example annotation is based on the article's published abstract. In a real annotated bibliography, read the full source before summarizing and assessing it.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **".gov and .edu sites are always reliable; .com sites are not."** Domain names are a weak clue. The CRAAP Test asks whether the URL reveals anything, but Wineburg and McGrew found that skilled readers were misled by domain names. Investigate who is behind the content.
- **"Peer-reviewed means proven true."** Peer review is a quality check by experts. Findings can still be limited, disputed, or later revised.
- **"Google Scholar results are all peer-reviewed."** Scholar's help page says it includes journal and conference papers, theses and dissertations, academic books, pre-prints, abstracts, technical reports, court opinions, and patents. Pre-prints, for example, have not yet been through journal peer review. Check each source.
- **"I can cite Wikipedia like any source."** Use encyclopedias for background and search terms, then cite the original sources they point to unless your teacher allows otherwise.
- **"Primary means best."** Primary means original, not most important. Many questions need strong secondary sources.
- **"The 'About Us' page tells me who runs a site."** It tells you what the site says about itself. Lateral reading checks what others say.
- **Boundary: older sources.** "Currency" matters for fast-moving science but much less for historical questions, where older sources may be exactly what you need.

## Practice

1. Turn each topic into a focused, open, researchable question: (a) *school lunches*; (b) *electric cars*.
2. Write a Boolean search for the question *How does sleep affect memory in teenagers?* using at least one OR group and one AND.
3. Which Google Scholar feature would you use to (a) find newer research that responds to an article; (b) find all papers by a researcher named Jane Q. Smith; (c) limit results to work published since 2022?
4. Classify each as primary, secondary, or tertiary for the question *How did Americans react to Roosevelt's first inaugural address?*: (a) a transcript of the address; (b) a 1933 letter to a newspaper about the speech; (c) a historian's book chapter on the first hundred days; (d) a textbook paragraph on the New Deal.
5. **Illustrative scenario:** a website called "Teen Study Facts" says students who drink a certain energy drink score higher on tests. It has no author, lists no sources, and sells the drink. Apply one SIFT move and two CRAAP criteria, and decide whether to use it.
6. Write a source-log entry and two notes (one quotation, one paraphrase, each with a locator) for any source your teacher approves.
7. **Extended task.** Write an annotated bibliography of three sources for a research question of your own, each with a full citation (MLA or APA) and a summarize-assess-reflect annotation of 80-150 words. Score it with the rubric.
