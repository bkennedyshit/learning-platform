# APP-12.8 — Advanced Rhetoric & Style

**Rhetoric** is the study of how communication works on an audience. Aristotle defined it as "an ability, in each particular case, to see the available means of persuasion," and Purdue OWL notes that modern scholars use the term more broadly, for any communication intended to change another person's perspective. **Style** is the set of choices a writer makes at the level of words and sentences: how concise, how formal, how varied, how vivid. Rhetorical analysis asks *what choices did this writer make, and why did they work (or not) for this audience?* Good style asks the same question of your own writing.

This is the final lesson of the Research Writing & Academic Literacy course. It is written for high school students, including those preparing for the AP English Language and Composition exam, though it does not require that course.

## Core ideas

### The rhetorical situation

Purdue OWL describes a **rhetorical situation** as any set of circumstances in which at least one person uses communication to modify the perspective of at least one other person. Its elements, which all affect one another:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| **Author** (speaker, writer, designer) | Who is communicating, and what is their position or reputation? |
| **Audience** | Who receives it? What do they already believe, know, and care about? |
| **Purpose** | What is the author trying to achieve: inform, persuade, unite, provoke? |
| **Setting / context** | When, where, and under what circumstances? What just happened? |
| **Text** | What form does it take (speech, essay, ad, chart), and how is it organized? |

Analyze the situation first. A choice that looks odd in isolation, such as a very short sentence, an emotional story, or a list of statistics, often makes sense once you know who the audience was and what had just happened.

### The appeals

Purdue OWL's guide to classical argument summarizes four appeals associated with Aristotle:

- **Ethos:** persuasion through the author's character or credibility, built by citing sources, using field-specific language, and showing knowledge and fairness.
- **Logos:** persuasion through logic, using data, facts, and a clearly sequenced argument.
- **Pathos:** persuasion through emotion or disposition, often through storytelling, vivid imagery, or an impassioned voice; in academic writing, also the audience's intellectual disposition toward a topic.
- **Kairos:** an appeal through the opportune moment, such as a call to immediate action or describing a moment as decisive.

Two cautions from the OWL. First, a writer does not "appeal to pathos"; the writer appeals to the audience's emotions, and pathos is the name of that appeal. Second, its page on Aristotle's rhetorical situation points out that the original Greek terms were broader than the usual translations: logos referred to the content and structure of the text, ethos to the author's perspective, and pathos to the audience's perspective. Strong analysis goes beyond labeling appeals to explaining **how** a specific choice works on **this** audience.

### Visual rhetoric

Images, charts, infographics, and advertisements make arguments too. Analyze them with the same rhetorical situation, plus questions about design:

- **Emphasis:** What does the eye see first (size, color, position, contrast)?
- **Framing and selection:** What is included, cropped out, or left unlabeled?
- **Text-image relationship:** Does the caption or headline direct how you read the image?
- **Data accuracy:** Do the axes start at zero? Are the scales consistent? Is the source of the data given?

### Style choices

**Concision.** Purdue OWL defines concise writing this way: it "does not always have the fewest words, but it always uses the strongest ones." Common fixes: replace vague phrases with precise words, delete filler (*the fact that*, *in my opinion*, *it is important to note that*), and combine sentences that repeat information.

**Active and passive voice.** In the **active voice**, the subject performs the action (*The council approved the budget*). In the **passive voice**, the subject receives the action (*The budget was approved*), and the actor may disappear. Active voice is usually clearer, but the UNC Writing Center lists good reasons to use the passive: to **emphasize the object** (*One hundred votes are required to pass the bill*) and when the **actor is unknown** (*Over 120 different contaminants have been dumped into the river*). Scientific methods sections often use it because the actor is unimportant (Lesson 12.7). Avoid the passive when it hides responsibility the reader needs to know about.

**Sentence variety.** Mix sentence lengths and openings. A short sentence after several long ones creates emphasis. Parallel structure (repeating a grammatical pattern) creates rhythm and links ideas, as in a list of three clauses with the same form.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Rhetorical analysis of a speech passage

**Text:** from Franklin D. Roosevelt's first inaugural address, delivered March 4, 1933, during the Great Depression:

> "This is preeminently the time to speak the truth, the whole truth, frankly and boldly. ... This great Nation will endure as it has endured, will revive and will prosper. So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself--nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance."

1. **Situation.** Author: a newly inaugurated president. Audience: a nation in economic crisis, many of whom have lost savings or jobs. Purpose: restore confidence and prepare people to support action. Setting: the inauguration itself, a moment when a new leader traditionally speaks to the whole country.
2. **Ethos.** He promises to "speak the truth, the whole truth, frankly and boldly." The phrase echoes a courtroom oath, presenting the president as an honest witness rather than a salesman of good news.
3. **Kairos.** He frames the inauguration as "preeminently the time," turning the ceremony into a turning point.
4. **Pathos and style.** The three verbs "endure... revive... prosper" form a **tricolon** that rises from survival to success. The famous line then **redefines the enemy**: not the economy but "fear itself." The three adjectives "nameless, unreasoning, unjustified" strip fear of any justification, and "convert retreat into advance" uses military language to make recovery sound like a campaign the audience can join.
5. **Logos.** Later in the speech, Roosevelt lists concrete conditions ("taxes have risen; our ability to pay has fallen"), so his confidence does not depend on ignoring the facts.

**Analysis paragraph:**

> Roosevelt opens his first inaugural address by establishing himself as a truthful witness before he offers any hope. Promising to "speak the truth, the whole truth, frankly and boldly," he borrows the language of a sworn oath, which allows his next claim, that the nation "will endure... will revive and will prosper," to sound like testimony rather than cheerleading. He then redefines the crisis itself: "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself." By describing that fear as "nameless, unreasoning, unjustified," Roosevelt denies panic any rational basis, and by calling recovery an effort "to convert retreat into advance," he casts listeners as participants in a campaign rather than victims of an economy. For an audience that had lost confidence in institutions, this sequence of credibility, reassurance, and a shared mission prepares them to accept the bold action the rest of the speech demands.

Reasoning: the paragraph makes a claim about the writer's **strategy**, quotes briefly, explains the effect of specific words, and ties every choice to the audience and moment, not just to a list of appeals.

### Example 2: Revising for concision and voice

Word counts were checked by counting space-separated words.

| Draft | Words | Revision | Words | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *In my opinion, it is a fact that the reason why students are often unable to finish their research papers on time is due to the fact that they do not start working on them early enough.* | 37 | *Students often miss research-paper deadlines because they start too late.* | 10 | removed filler (*in my opinion, it is a fact that, the reason why... is due to the fact that*); replaced *are unable to finish... on time* with *miss... deadlines* |
| *There are many students who feel that the new schedule is something that makes it harder for them to be able to participate in after-school activities.* | 26 | *Many students say the new schedule makes after-school activities harder to attend.* | 12 | cut the *there are... who* opening and *something that*; *say* is more precise than *feel that* |
| *Mistakes were made in the grading of the exams.* | 9 | *The grading team made mistakes on the exams.* | 8 | passive hid who was responsible; active names the actor |

When to **keep** the passive: *The samples were stored at 4 °C for 24 hours.* In a methods section the actor is unimportant, and the passive keeps the focus on the samples.

### Example 3: Analyzing a misleading chart

**Illustrative chart, not real data:** a school newsletter shows two bars, "Last year: 92% attendance" and "This year: 96% attendance." The vertical axis starts at 90%, not 0%.

1. **Emphasis.** The "This year" bar looks three times as tall as "Last year": measured from the 90% baseline, the bars are 6 points and 2 points tall, and 6 ÷ 2 = 3.
2. **Accuracy.** The actual change is 4 percentage points (96 − 92). On an axis starting at 0, the bars would look nearly equal.
3. **Purpose.** A newsletter celebrating a new policy has a reason to make the change look large.
4. **Judgment.** The numbers may be accurate, but the design exaggerates the change. An honest version starts the axis at zero or clearly marks a broken axis and reports the difference in the caption.

### Example 4: Planning a synthesis response

**Illustrative prompt:** *Should high schools require a course in media literacy? Use at least three of the six sources provided.*

1. **Read the sources rhetorically.** For each, note the author, purpose, and what it can support (for example, a study, an opinion column, a chart).
2. **Take a position** that qualifies the issue: *Schools should require media literacy instruction, but embedding it in existing English and social studies courses is more practical than a separate required course.*
3. **Group sources by idea**, not one per paragraph: two sources on the skill gap; one on schedule constraints, which supports the qualification.
4. **Answer the strongest opposing source** directly instead of ignoring it.
5. **Cite as the exam directs**, typically by the source label the prompt assigns, and explain each source's role in your own words.

Reasoning: the plan applies synthesis (Lesson 12.6) and refutation (Lesson 12.2) under time pressure.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Rhetoric means manipulation or empty talk."** Purdue OWL notes that many people hear "rhetoric" as trickery or even lying, but describes it more fully as communication used to shape others' perspectives, studied and used in every field.
- **"Rhetorical analysis means finding ethos, pathos, and logos."** Labeling is the start, not the analysis. Explain how a specific choice affects a specific audience in a specific situation.
- **"Pathos is always manipulative."** Emotional appeals are legitimate when they fit the evidence and the stakes. They become a problem when they replace evidence or distort it.
- **"Passive voice is grammatically wrong."** It is grammatically correct and sometimes the better choice. The problem is overuse, or using it to hide who is responsible.
- **"Concise means as short as possible."** Concise means every word does work. Sometimes adding a precise word makes a sentence clearer.
- **"Charts are objective."** Every chart reflects choices about scale, selection, and labeling.
- **Boundary: historical texts.** When analyzing older speeches, judge choices against their original audience and moment, and avoid assuming the speaker knew how events would turn out.
- **Boundary: exam formats change.** Check AP Central for the current exam format before test day; the details here reflect the College Board's page as of this lesson's sources.

## Practice

1. Identify the rhetorical situation (author, audience, purpose, setting) for: *a principal's email to families the night before a snow day.*
2. Name the main appeal and explain its effect: (a) *"As a nurse who has worked night shifts for twelve years, I can tell you what fatigue does to judgment."* (b) *"Three of the five bus routes now arrive after the first bell."* (c) *"Imagine your little sister waiting alone at a dark bus stop."* (d) *"The council votes Tuesday; after that, the budget is locked for two years."*
3. Rewrite in the active voice, or explain why the passive is better: (a) *The decision was made to cancel the trip.* (b) *The new reading program was first tested in two classrooms.* (c) *The window was broken sometime overnight.*
4. Cut each sentence to 12 words or fewer without losing meaning: (a) *It is important to note that the library will be closed for the purpose of renovations during the month of June.* (b) *Due to the fact that it was raining, the game that was scheduled was postponed until a later date.*
5. **Illustrative chart:** a bar graph shows "Phone A battery: 20 hours" and "Phone B battery: 22 hours," with the vertical axis starting at 19 hours. Explain how the design affects a viewer, using a calculation.
6. **Extended task.** Choose a speech, editorial, or advertisement your teacher approves (or use the longer passage of Roosevelt's address linked in the sources). Write a rhetorical analysis paragraph of 200-300 words. Score it with the rubric.
