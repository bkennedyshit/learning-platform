# APP-03.1 — Living vs Non-Living & Basic Needs

A dog, a tree, and a ladybug are **living things**. A rock, a toy car, and a cloud are **non-living things**. How can you tell the difference? Living things do certain jobs that non-living things never do: they grow, they need food or energy, they take in water and air, they notice and respond to the world around them, and they make more living things like themselves.

This is the first lesson in life science. The course plan places it in grades K-1. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

None in this course. It helps to be able to sort objects into groups and to count and compare, as in [APP-02.1 — Number Sense & Counting](/app-021-number-sense-counting).

> **Adult note:** Gather a few safe objects to sort: a rock, a leaf from the ground, a wooden spoon, a toy, an apple, and a picture of an animal. For Example 3 you will need two small potted plants of the same kind, or two cups of soil with a few dry bean seeds planted in each.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Sort things into **living**, **once-living**, and **never-living** groups and say why.
2. Name what plants need (light, water, air, nutrients, space) and what animals need (food, water, air, shelter, space).
3. Explain that a **habitat** is a home that gives a living thing what it needs.
4. Point to a body part and say how it helps the plant or animal get what it needs.

## Core ideas

### What living things do

Scientists look for a group of clues. One clue alone is not enough. Living things:

- **Grow and change.** A puppy grows into a dog. A seed grows into a plant.
- **Use food for energy.** Animals eat food. Plants make their own food using sunlight.
- **Take in water and air.** You breathe air. Plants take in air through tiny holes in their leaves.
- **Respond.** A cat runs when it hears a loud noise. A plant on a windowsill bends toward the light.
- **Make more of their own kind.** Dogs have puppies. Apple trees make seeds that can grow into new apple trees.
- **Get rid of wastes.** Animals breathe out and go to the bathroom.

Living things are also made of tiny parts called **cells**. You will look at cells with a microscope in [APP-03.5 — Cells — The Basic Unit of Life](/app-035-cells-the-basic-unit-of-life).

> **Adult note:** Older students often learn the memory aid **MRS GREN**: Movement, Respiration, Sensitivity (responding), Growth, Reproduction, Excretion (getting rid of waste), Nutrition. Biology textbooks list the properties of life a little differently, for example order, response to the environment, reproduction, adaptation, growth and development, regulation, energy processing, and evolution. The point at any level is the same: look for several clues together, not one.

### Living, once-living, and never-living

A third group helps with tricky objects:

| Group | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Living | Alive now | a dandelion, a goldfish, you |
| Once-living | Was part of a living thing, but is not alive now | a fallen dry leaf, a wooden spoon, a seashell |
| Never-living | Was never alive | a rock, a toy car, water, a cloud |

A wooden spoon came from a tree, so it is once-living. A rock was never alive.

### Basic needs

Every living thing needs certain things to stay alive.

| Plants need | Animals need |
|---|---|
| Light (to make food) | Food (they cannot make their own) |
| Water | Water |
| Air | Air (fish get the oxygen they need from water) |
| Nutrients from soil | Shelter (a safe place to rest and hide) |
| Space to grow | Space to move and find food |

Plants are special because they **make their own food**. Their leaves use light, water, and a gas from the air called carbon dioxide to make sugar. Animals cannot do that, so they eat plants or other animals. You will learn more in [APP-03.2 — Plants — Structure, Function & Life Cycles](/app-032-plants-structure-function-life-cycles).

### Habitats

A **habitat** is the place where a plant or animal lives. A good habitat gives it food, water, air, shelter, and space. A pond is a habitat for frogs: it has water, insects to eat, and plants to hide in. A forest is a habitat for squirrels: it has trees for shelter and nuts to eat.

### Body parts that help

Plants and animals have body parts that help them meet their needs:

- A duck has **webbed feet** that push water like paddles, so it can swim to find food.
- A fish has **gills** that take oxygen out of the water.
- A squirrel has **sharp front teeth** that crack open hard nuts.
- A plant has **roots** that hold it in the ground and soak up water.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Sorting a table of objects

*Activity to try.* Put out a rock, a dandelion, a wooden spoon, a ladybug picture, a toy car, and an apple seed.

1. **Rock:** Does it grow? No. Does it eat or drink? No. Was it ever part of a living thing? No. **Never-living.**
2. **Dandelion:** It grows, it needs water and light, and it makes seeds. **Living** while it is growing in the soil. A picked dandelion that has dried up is once-living.
3. **Wooden spoon:** Wood comes from a tree, but the spoon cannot grow or make new spoons. **Once-living.**
4. **Ladybug:** It eats, moves, grows, and lays eggs. **Living.**
5. **Toy car:** It moves when you push it, but it does not grow, eat, or make baby cars. **Never-living.** Moving is only one clue.
6. **Apple seed:** It looks like it does nothing. But if you plant it with water and warmth, it can grow. A seed is alive and resting. **Living.**

Check: each choice used more than one clue.

### Example 2: Is a candle flame alive?

A flame seems to do some living things: it moves, it gets bigger, and it needs air.

1. List the clues it seems to have: moving, growing, using air.
2. Look for the missing clues. A flame is not made of cells. It does not make baby flames from its own body the way a cat makes kittens. It does not have a body that grows and changes like a puppy.
3. A flame "grows" only because it is burning more fuel, not because it is taking in food to build its body.
4. Answer: a flame is **never-living**. This shows why scientists check several clues together.

### Example 3: Do plants need light?

*Activity to try.* Use two plants of the same kind, the same size, in the same kind of pot.

1. **Predict:** what will happen to a plant kept in a dark closet compared with one by a sunny window? Say why.
2. **Make it fair:** give both plants the same amount of water on the same days. Only the light is different.
3. **Observe:** every two days, look at the color of the leaves and whether the plant is leaning. Draw or write what you see.
4. **Think:** a plant uses light to make food. If it has no light, what would it run out of?
5. After one week, put the closet plant back by the window.

This is a prediction and a plan, not a result. Write down what *your* plants do.

> **Adult note:** Plants differ. Some change quickly in the dark and some slowly. Praise careful observing and fair testing rather than a "right" answer.

### Example 4: Matching a living thing to its habitat

Where would a frog live best: a desert, a pond, or a parking lot?

1. What does a frog need? Water to keep its skin wet and to lay eggs, insects to eat, and places to hide.
2. **Desert:** very little water. Not a good match.
3. **Parking lot:** no water, no plants, little food. Not a good match.
4. **Pond:** water, insects, and plants to hide in. **Best match.**

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"If it moves, it is alive."** A toy car, the wind, and a river all move, but they are not alive. A tree hardly moves, but it is alive.
- **"Plants are not alive because they do not walk or eat."** Plants grow, take in water and air, make their own food, respond to light, and make seeds. They are living.
- **"A seed is just a little rock."** Many seeds rest for months or even years and then grow when they get water and the right conditions. A resting seed is still alive.
- **"Once-living things are still alive."** A wooden table came from a tree, but it cannot grow or make new trees. It is once-living, not living.
- **"Animals only need food."** Animals also need water, air, shelter, and space. A pet with plenty of food but no water would not stay healthy.

> **Adult note:** Viruses are a famous boundary case for older learners. They can make more viruses only by getting inside living cells, and they are not made of cells, so biologists do not count them as living.

## Practice

1. Sort these into living, once-living, or never-living: a pinecone that fell off a tree, a goldfish, a metal spoon, a sunflower, a cotton T-shirt.
2. Name three things a plant needs.
3. Name two things an animal needs that a plant does not need in the same way.
4. A balloon gets bigger when you blow air into it. Is it alive? Use two clues to explain.
5. Why does a fish need gills?
6. A bird builds a nest in a tree. Which basic need is the nest helping with?
7. Draw a habitat for a rabbit. Show one thing for food, one for water, and one for shelter.

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. Living: goldfish, sunflower. Once-living: the fallen pinecone (it came from a tree), the cotton T-shirt (cotton comes from a plant). Never-living: the metal spoon (metal comes from rocks, not from a living thing).
2. Any three: light, water, air, nutrients from soil, space.
3. Food (animals must eat; plants make their own) and shelter.
4. No. It gets bigger only because you push air into it. It does not eat, grow on its own, or make baby balloons.
5. Gills take oxygen out of the water so the fish can breathe.
6. Shelter: a safe place to rest and keep eggs or babies.
7. Check that the drawing shows food (for example, grass or carrots), water (a puddle or stream), and shelter (a burrow or bushes).

**Adult self-check:** Ask "How do you know?" after each sorting answer. A strong answer gives at least two clues, such as "it grows and it eats," not just "it moves."

</details>

> **Safety note:** Do not eat seeds, leaves, berries, or mushrooms you find. Some plants are poisonous to eat or touch; the Poison Control helpline in the U.S. is 1-800-222-1222. Look at wild animals from a distance and do not touch or pick them up. Wash hands with soap and clean running water after touching soil, plants, pets, or animal homes.
