# APP-12.7 — Disciplinary Writing

Every academic field, or **discipline**, has its own ways of asking questions, its own standards of evidence, and its own conventions for organizing and styling writing. A chemistry lab report, a psychology study, a literary analysis, and a business memo can all be well written, but they would each fail if written like one of the others. The UNC Writing Center puts the principle simply: "Find out what counts as proof that something is true in that field."

This lesson compares four families of disciplines and gives practice in the core forms of each: the **IMRaD** report (sciences and many social sciences), the **analytical essay** (humanities), and the **memo** (business and workplace writing). It is written for high school students; the conventions shown here continue into college.

## Prerequisites

[APP-12.6 — Essay Structures & Genres](/app-126-essay-structures-genres) (genres and literature reviews) and [APP-12.5 — Citation & Documentation](/app-125-citation-documentation) (MLA, APA, and Chicago). For the lab-report sections, it helps to know how experiments are designed; see [17.3 — Sampling & Experimental Design](/173-sampling-experimental-design).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain how disciplines differ in questions, evidence, structure, citation style, and verb tense.
2. Write **Methods**, **Results**, and **Discussion** sections of an IMRaD lab report.
3. Write a humanities analysis paragraph using the **literary present** and textual evidence.
4. Write a workplace **memo** with a complete heading and a purpose-first opening.

## Core ideas

### Four families compared

| | Natural sciences | Social sciences | Humanities | Business and workplace |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical question | What happens, and why? | How do people and groups behave, and why? | What does this text, artifact, or event mean? | What should we decide or do? |
| Main evidence | measurements, experiments, observations | surveys, interviews, observations, statistics | primary texts, artworks, documents, and scholarship about them | data, costs, options, stakeholder needs |
| Common structure | IMRaD lab report or research article | IMRaD with a literature review | thesis-driven essay | memo, email, report, proposal |
| Common citation style | varies by field (APA, a journal's own style, or Chicago author-date) | APA | MLA (literature, languages); Chicago notes (history, arts) | varies by organization |
| Typical tense | past for what you did; present for established knowledge | present for what research shows; past for specific studies | present for what a text does (literary present); past for historical events | present and future for recommendations |

These are tendencies, not rules. Always check your assignment and look at model writing from the field.

### IMRaD in the sciences and social sciences

The UNC Writing Center explains that the research report format, "sometimes called 'IMRAD,'" was "devised to represent a textual version of the scientific method":

| Section | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Introduction** | states the hypothesis, explains how it was derived and how it connects to previous research, and gives the purpose of the study |
| **Methods** | details how the hypothesis was tested and why it was tested that way |
| **Results** | presents the data collected, uninterpreted, often in tables or figures |
| **Discussion** | considers whether the data support the hypothesis, explores implications, and judges limitations of the design |

Additional conventions from UNC and Purdue OWL:

- **Methods** describe an event that already happened, so they use the **simple past**: *We added 5 g of salt*, not the lab manual's imperative *Add 5 g of salt*. Include enough detail, especially measurements, for someone else to repeat the procedure.
- **Results** report what you found; save interpretation for the Discussion.
- **Voice:** older guidance favored the passive (*The solution was heated*), but UNC notes that "increasingly, especially in the social sciences, using first person and active voice is acceptable." Follow your teacher's or journal's preference.
- **Shape:** Purdue OWL describes experimental reports as **general to specific to general**: broad in the introduction, narrow through hypotheses, methods, and results, and broad again in the discussion.
- **Abstract:** many reports begin with a concise summary of the whole study, which Purdue OWL advises writing last.

### Social sciences

Social science papers often use APA style and IMRaD structure, with a **literature review** placed after the introduction and before the methods. The UNC Writing Center's verb-tense handout gives a useful pattern: use the **present tense** for what is known now (*The research shows...*, *The data suggest...*) and the **past tense** when describing a specific study by named researchers (*Wineburg and McGrew found...*).

### Humanities

Humanities writing is usually a **thesis-driven essay**. Purdue OWL's guidance on writing about literature says a literary paper "is essentially making an argument" that your interpretation is valid, with a debatable thesis supported by examples from the text and evidence from scholars. Historians, as the UNC Writing Center explains, build knowledge from **primary sources**, the "relics" that survive from the past, and then interpret them.

Two conventions matter most:

- **Literary present.** Describe what happens in a literary work in the present tense, because the text always says it: *Victor rushes from the room*, not *Victor rushed*. UNC's verb-tense handout calls this "the narrative present" or "the literary present."
- **Close attention to language.** Quote short passages and analyze specific words, images, and structures (see [38.8 — Literary Analysis & Close Reading](/388-literary-analysis-close-reading)).

History writing, by contrast, narrates past events in the **past tense** and distinguishes clearly between what a primary source says and what the historian infers.

### Business and workplace writing

Purdue OWL describes memos as brief documents for routine communication inside organizations, quoting Johnson-Sheehan that they "convey decisions, meeting agendas, policies, internal reports, and short proposals." Its guide to the parts of a memo:

- **Heading:** *TO:* (names and job titles), *FROM:*, *DATE:*, *SUBJECT:* (specific: "Fall Clothes Line Promotion," not "Clothes").
- **Opening:** the purpose of the memo, the context and problem, and the specific task, in about a short paragraph.
- **Context and task:** only the background the reader needs, and what you are doing or asking for.
- **Summary** (for memos longer than a page): key recommendations.
- **Discussion:** supporting details, most important first.

Business writing puts the main point first because busy readers may read only the opening.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: From lab notes to Methods, Results, and Discussion

**Activity to try (with an adult or teacher present):** compare how long sugar cubes take to dissolve in cold and warm water. Use cool tap water and warm tap water only, never boiling water, and wipe up spills. The numbers below are **illustrative data for writing practice, not results from a performed experiment.**

**Student's lab notes:** *3 cups cold water ~10 °C, 3 cups warm ~40 °C, 200 mL each, 1 cube each, no stirring, timed until no solid visible. Cold: 182 s, 175 s, 190 s. Warm: 61 s, 58 s, 66 s.*

**Methods (past tense, replicable detail):**

> We filled six cups with 200 mL of tap water each: three at about 10 °C and three at about 40 °C, measured with a kitchen thermometer. We dropped one standard sugar cube into each cup without stirring and timed how long it took until no solid sugar was visible.

**Results (data, no interpretation):**

> In cold water, cubes dissolved in 175-190 s (mean 182 s). In warm water, cubes dissolved in 58-66 s (mean 62 s). Every warm-water trial was faster than every cold-water trial.

The means come from (182 + 175 + 190) ÷ 3 ≈ 182.3 s and (61 + 58 + 66) ÷ 3 ≈ 61.7 s, rounded to whole seconds.

**Discussion (interpretation within the data):**

> These results are consistent with our hypothesis that sugar dissolves faster in warmer water: the mean time in cold water was about three times as long. Because we tested only two temperatures with three trials each, we cannot describe how dissolving time changes across a range of temperatures. Judging "no solid visible" by eye may also have varied between trials; a future test could use more trials and a fixed observation method.

Reasoning: the Methods could be repeated by someone else; the Results contain no "because"; the Discussion says *consistent with* rather than *proves*, and names two limitations. For mixtures and solutions, see [APP-04.4 — Matter — Properties & States](/app-044-matter-properties-states).

### Example 2: One topic, four disciplines

**Topic:** *school start times.*

| Discipline | Question | Evidence | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Biology / health science | How does sleep timing change during adolescence? | published sleep studies, measurements | research report or literature review |
| Social science | Is start time associated with attendance in our district? | attendance records, surveys (correlation, not causation unless it is an experiment) | IMRaD report in APA |
| Humanities (history) | How have arguments about school schedules changed over time? | old school board minutes, newspaper editorials (primary sources) | thesis-driven essay with Chicago notes |
| Business | Should the district change its bus contract to allow a later start? | contract costs, route options, stakeholder input | memo with a recommendation |

Reasoning: the same subject produces different questions because each field defines a good answer differently. The social science row also shows a discipline-specific caution: attendance data alone can show an association, not that start times caused a change (see [17.2 — Exploring Data (2-Variable)](/172-exploring-data-2-variable)).

### Example 3: A humanities paragraph in the literary present

**Draft (past tense, summary-heavy):**

> In Chapter 5, Victor finished the creature and then he ran out of the room. He was horrified. This showed he was scared.

**Revised:**

> Shelley stages Victor's failure at the very moment of his success. After nearly two years of work, Victor, who "had selected his features as beautiful," is filled with "breathless horror and disgust," and he admits, "Unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room" (Shelley, ch. 5). Victor's own words reveal the problem: he cannot "endure" even looking at what he made, so he flees rather than taking responsibility for it. The novel's tragedy begins not in the laboratory but in this doorway.

Reasoning: the writer's sentences use the present tense (*stages*, *is filled*, *admits*, *reveal*) while the quotation keeps Victor's original past tense; the paragraph opens with an interpretive claim, quotes briefly, analyzes specific words (*endure*), and ends with a claim about meaning.

### Example 4: Rewriting a vague memo

**Illustrative draft:**

> TO: Principal
> FROM: Student Council
> SUBJECT: Library
>
> We have been talking a lot about the library and lots of students have opinions about it, and some of us think it might be good to look into maybe changing things.

**Revised:**

> **TO:** Dr. A. Rivera, Principal
> **FROM:** Jordan Lee, Student Council President
> **DATE:** [date sent]
> **SUBJECT:** Request to pilot 5:00 p.m. library closing in spring semester
>
> The Student Council requests a six-week pilot keeping the library open until 5:00 p.m. on Tuesdays and Thursdays this spring. In our council survey of 120 students (illustrative numbers), 48 said they lack a quiet place to study after school. We propose two volunteer teacher supervisors per evening and will report sign-in counts at the end of the pilot. Could we meet next week to discuss staffing?

Reasoning: the heading names the reader and sender with titles and a specific subject; the opening states the request, the context, the plan, and the next step in one short paragraph. The survey numbers are labeled illustrative, as any real memo's numbers would need to come from actual data.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Good writing is the same in every class."** Clarity matters everywhere, but evidence, structure, tense, and citation style change by discipline.
- **"Scientific writing must always be passive."** Many fields now accept first person and active voice. Follow your teacher or the target journal.
- **"The Results section should explain why."** Results report what happened; the Discussion interprets it.
- **"My data prove my hypothesis."** Data can support or be consistent with a hypothesis. A few trials cannot prove a general rule.
- **"Use past tense to discuss a novel because it was written in the past."** In literary analysis, use the present tense for what happens in the text; use the past tense for historical facts about the author or publication.
- **"A memo should build up to the request."** Workplace readers expect the purpose first.
- **Boundary: lab report formats vary.** UNC notes that some courses ask for a separate hypothesis section, call the Discussion "Conclusions," or reorder sections. Use the format your teacher provides.
- **Boundary: interdisciplinary topics.** A paper on the history of science or the economics of health care may combine conventions. Decide which audience you are writing for and follow its expectations.
