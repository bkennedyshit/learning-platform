# 59.3 - Argument Structure

An **assertion** states a position: *The university should keep the library open until 2 a.m. during finals.* An **argument** gives the reader reasons to accept that position: evidence, an explanation of why the evidence supports the claim, honest limits, and an answer to the best objection. The UNC Writing Center defines an argument in academic writing as "a main idea, often called a 'claim' or 'thesis statement,' backed up with evidence that supports the idea." This lesson treats argument as a structure you can inspect, part by part, in your own drafts and in other people's.

It is written for college students and professionals who write papers, proposals, memos, design documents, or op-eds. The structure is the same in each; the evidence that counts changes by field.

Lessons [59.1 - Clarity — The Foundation](/591-clarity-the-foundation) and [59.2 - Sentence Mechanics & Rhythm](/592-sentence-mechanics-rhythm). It helps to have written a thesis-driven essay; [APP-12.2 — Thesis & Argument Construction](/app-122-thesis-argument-construction) introduces claims, Toulmin's model, and fallacies at a high-school level, and [38.5 — Argumentation & Rhetoric](/385-argumentation-rhetoric) covers the rhetorical appeals.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Distinguish an **assertion** from an **argument** and map an argument into **claim, grounds, and warrant**, including an unstated warrant.
2. Strengthen an argument with **backing, a qualifier, and a rebuttal**, and explain how each affects credibility.
3. Identify and repair five common **fallacies**.
4. **Plan** an argument that answers the strongest objection charitably and ends with a claim the evidence has earned.

## Core ideas

### The Toulmin model

Philosopher Stephen Toulmin's model, as summarized by Purdue OWL, breaks an argument into six parts. Three are fundamental:

| Part | Question it answers | Library example |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | What do you want the reader to accept? | The library should stay open until 2 a.m. during finals week. |
| **Grounds** | What evidence supports it? | *(Illustrative)* Entry counts show the building is near capacity at its current midnight closing during finals. |
| **Warrant** | Why does that evidence support that claim? | When demand for study space exceeds supply at closing time, extending hours meets a real need. |

The other three "are not fundamental ... but may be added as necessary":

| Part | Role |
|---|---|
| **Backing** | Support for the warrant itself (for example, evidence that students who leave at midnight have no other quiet place to study). |
| **Qualifier** | Words such as *presumably*, *some*, or *many* that show the claim may not hold in all cases (*during finals week*, *if staffing allows*). |
| **Rebuttal** | Acknowledgment of another valid view or of conditions under which the claim fails. |

OWL makes an important point about the last two: including a qualifier or rebuttal "helps build your ethos," presenting you as "a careful, unbiased thinker, rather than ... someone blindly pushing for a single interpretation."

### Unstated warrants

The warrant is often implied, and that is where arguments quietly fail. *We should require code review because last quarter's outage came from an unreviewed change* assumes that review would have caught that change. If the bug was one that reviewers routinely miss, the warrant is false and the argument collapses, even though the grounds are true. Writing the warrant out is the fastest way to test an argument.

### Evidence that counts in the field

UNC advises: "Find out what counts as proof that something is true in that field." Statistics persuade a sociologist; close reading of the text persuades a literary scholar; a reproducible benchmark persuades an engineer. UNC also asks you to "be consistent with your evidence," so each claim in a section is supported by evidence suited to that claim, rather than "an all-out blitz of every type of argument."

### Appeals and the classical arrangement

Purdue OWL's guide to classical argument summarizes four appeals associated with Aristotle: **ethos** (credibility), **logos** (logic), **pathos** (emotion or disposition), and **kairos** (the opportune moment). OWL's page on Aristotle's rhetorical situation adds that the original terms were broader: logos referred to the content and structure of a speech, ethos to the speaker's character and perspective, and pathos to any of an audience's sensibilities. In argument structure, ethos is earned mainly by fairness and accuracy, logos by the claim-grounds-warrant chain, and pathos by showing why the issue matters to this audience.

OWL also describes the classical arrangement: **exordium** (introduction), **narratio** (background), **propositio and partitio** (claim and outline of the argument), **confirmatio and/or refutatio** (proofs and answers to objections), and **peroratio** (conclusion and call to action). Modern essays and proposals compress this, but the logic survives: context, claim, demonstration, objection and response, consequences.

### Counterarguments: be charitable

UNC recommends anticipating objections and answering them, and warns: "When you are summarizing opposing arguments, be charitable." Attacking a caricature (a **straw man**) "suggests that your argument is only capable of defeating an extremely weak adversary." It is usually better to treat "one or two serious counterarguments in some depth" than to list many superficially. Your response can **concede** (the objection is right about something, but your claim still holds), **refute** (the objection is mistaken, and here is why), or **revise** (the objection changes your claim, so you narrow it).

### Proportion: broad claims need more proof

UNC's handout on fallacies notes that claims using sweeping words such as *all*, *never*, *always*, and *everyone* "require a lot more proof than less-sweeping claims" that use *some*, *many*, or *usually*. A well-built argument matches the size of the claim to the size of the evidence.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: From assertion to argument

**Assertion:** *Remote work is better.*

1. **Make the claim specific and debatable.** Better for whom, at what, compared with what? *For our five-person documentation team, a hybrid schedule with two in-office days would reduce interruptions during writing time without slowing reviews.*
2. **Supply grounds.** *(Illustrative)* Team members' calendars from the last month, showing how often writing blocks were interrupted on office days and remote days; the review turnaround times for both.
3. **State the warrant.** Fewer interruptions during writing blocks produce more finished drafts, and review speed is the main cost to watch.
4. **Add a qualifier.** *would likely reduce*; *for this team*; *for a three-month trial*.
5. **Anticipate a rebuttal.** New hires may learn more slowly without in-person contact. Response: concede, and schedule onboarding on office days.

Reasoning: the original assertion could not be supported because it did not say what "better" meant. The revised claim names its scope, so the grounds can actually reach it.

### Example 2: Finding and testing an unstated warrant

**Illustrative proposal sentence:** *We should switch the course to open textbooks, because students in two sections reported that they could not afford the assigned book.*

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Claim | Switch to open textbooks. |
| Grounds | Students in two sections reported they could not afford the book. |
| Unstated warrant | Cost keeps some students from accessing required readings, and an open textbook of adequate quality exists for this course. |
| Test | The first half is supported by the grounds. The second half (*adequate quality exists*) is not; the argument needs backing, such as a review of available open textbooks against the course objectives. |
| Qualifier | *for the introductory sections* (if an adequate open book exists only at that level) |

Reasoning: the grounds were true and relevant, but the argument depended on an assumption it never defended. Writing the warrant out revealed the missing evidence.

### Example 3: Repairing fallacies

The definitions follow the UNC Writing Center's handout on fallacies; the sentences are illustrative.

| Draft sentence | Fallacy | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| *Two students in my seminar used AI tools to cheat, so AI tools make students dishonest.* | **Hasty generalization**: a conclusion about a whole group from a small or atypical sample | Narrow the claim or gather representative evidence: *Two cases in one seminar suggest the course's integrity policy needs to address AI tools.* |
| *After the new dean arrived, applications fell. The dean's policies are driving students away.* | **Post hoc**: assuming that because B followed A, A caused B | Show a mechanism and rule out other causes (for example, regional enrollment trends or a tuition change). |
| *Either we cancel the program or we keep losing money.* | **False dichotomy**: presenting two options when more exist | Name and evaluate other options: restructure, seek a partner, reduce scope. |
| *Opponents of the new schedule just want to avoid working mornings.* | **Straw man**: attacking a weakened version of the other side | State their strongest reason (for example, childcare conflicts) and answer it. |
| *The policy is fair because it treats everyone equitably.* | **Begging the question**: the premise restates the conclusion | Supply independent reasons: what the policy does, and why that counts as fair. |

### Example 4: Answering the strongest objection

**Claim (illustrative op-ed):** *The city should convert one downtown lane to a protected bike lane.*

- **Straw-man version of the objection:** *Some people just hate cyclists.* (Easy to defeat, and persuades no one who holds the real objection.)
- **Charitable version:** *Downtown businesses depend on delivery access, and losing a lane could make loading harder and slow deliveries.*
- **Response (concede and revise):** *That is a real cost. The proposal should designate loading zones on cross streets and limit deliveries to early-morning windows, as part of the plan rather than an afterthought.*
- **Revised claim:** *The city should convert one downtown lane to a protected bike lane, with dedicated loading zones on cross streets.*

Reasoning: the response accepts part of the objection and improves the claim. That move usually builds more credibility with skeptical readers than refusing every point.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"An argument is a fight."** In academic and professional writing, an argument is a reasoned case. UNC frames it as "expressing a point of view on a subject and supporting it with evidence." The goal is to persuade a reader who is "perfectly smart but who doesn't necessarily agree with you."
- **"Acknowledging the other side weakens my case."** OWL's Toulmin summary says a qualifier or rebuttal helps build ethos. Ignoring the best objection leaves readers to supply it themselves.
- **"More kinds of evidence are always better."** UNC advises consistency: match each type of evidence to the claim it supports.
- **"Naming a fallacy wins the argument."** UNC presents arguments on a continuum from very weak to very strong and notes that "it is sometimes hard to evaluate whether an argument is fallacious." Explain *why* the reasoning fails; do not just attach a label.
- **"Qualifiers are hedging."** A qualifier that matches the evidence is accuracy. Stacked hedges on every sentence (*it might perhaps seem*) are a different problem, covered in Lesson 59.1.
- **Boundary: when a chain of consequences is real.** UNC notes that some predicted chains are reasonable (failing a required course can delay graduation). A slippery-slope objection applies only when the links are not supported.
- **Boundary: field conventions.** Some fields expect the claim in the first paragraph; some forms (Rogerian argument, certain policy memos) build common ground before stating a position. Learn the convention of the genre you are writing in.

## Practice

1. Label each as an assertion or an argument, and explain: (a) *Our onboarding process is broken.* (b) *Our onboarding process should add a mentor assignment, because six of the last ten new hires told us in exit interviews that they did not know whom to ask for help.* (c) *Social media is ruining democracy.*
2. Identify the claim, grounds, and warrant (state it if unstated): *Professor Lee's course should be cross-listed in the history department, since half of its readings are primary sources from the nineteenth century.*
3. Add a qualifier and a rebuttal to this argument without changing its claim beyond what the evidence supports: *The campus should replace paper parking permits with license-plate recognition, because the system would remove the need for permit printing and distribution.*
4. Name the fallacy and write a repaired version: (a) *Every expert I asked supports the plan, and I asked three people on the committee that wrote it.* (b) *If we allow one extension, every student will want one, and deadlines will mean nothing.* (c) *You can't trust her critique of the budget; she was late on her own reports last year.*
5. Write a charitable one-sentence version of the strongest objection to a claim you hold about a campus, workplace, or civic issue, then a two-sentence response that concedes, refutes, or revises.
6. **Extended task.** Write a 400-600 word argument (an op-ed, proposal, or short essay) on a debatable question in your field. Before submitting, write a Toulmin map (claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal) in a short table. Score it with the rubric.

> **Integrity note:** Represent opposing views accurately; if you quote or paraphrase someone you disagree with, cite them and do not trim their words to make them sound weaker.
