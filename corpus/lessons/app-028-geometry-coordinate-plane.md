# APP-02.8 — Geometry & Coordinate Plane

Geometry is the study of shapes and space. In this last lesson of the Early Numeracy course, the learner sorts flat shapes by their properties, counts the parts of solid shapes, finds lines of symmetry, and uses a coordinate grid to give every point an exact address.

The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-02.7 — Measurement & Data](/app-027-measurement-data): measuring lengths and finding perimeter and area. From [APP-02.4 — Fractions — Concepts & Operations](/app-024-fractions-concepts-operations): reading a number line, because each axis of a coordinate grid is a number line.

> **Adult note:** Materials: paper shapes to cut and fold (squares, rectangles, triangles, a regular hexagon if available), a few solid objects (a cube-shaped box, a cereal box, a can, a ball), a corner of a sheet of paper as a "right-angle tester," and grid paper for the coordinate plane.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Name and sort triangles and quadrilaterals by their sides and angles, and explain why a square is also a rectangle.
2. Count the faces, edges, and vertices of a cube, a rectangular prism, a triangular prism, and a square pyramid.
3. Find all lines of symmetry of a shape and check them by folding.
4. Plot points like (3, 5) and read the coordinates of a plotted point.

## Core ideas

### Words for describing shapes

- A **polygon** is a closed flat shape made of straight sides.
- A **vertex** (plural: vertices) is a corner where two sides meet.
- A **right angle** is a square corner, like the corner of a sheet of paper. An **acute** angle is smaller than a right angle; an **obtuse** angle is bigger than a right angle but less than a straight line.
- **Parallel** lines go in the same direction and never meet, like the two rails of a train track. **Perpendicular** lines meet at a right angle, like the edges at a corner of a page.

### Triangles

Triangles can be sorted by sides: **equilateral** (all 3 sides equal), **isosceles** (at least 2 equal), and **scalene** (no equal sides). They can also be sorted by angles: **right** (one right angle), **obtuse** (one obtuse angle), and **acute** (all three angles acute).

### The quadrilateral family

A **quadrilateral** is any polygon with 4 sides.

- A **parallelogram** has two pairs of parallel sides.
- A **rectangle** is a parallelogram with 4 right angles.
- A **rhombus** is a parallelogram with 4 equal sides.
- A **square** has 4 right angles **and** 4 equal sides, so it is a rectangle **and** a rhombus.
- A **trapezoid**, in the definition OpenStax uses, has one pair of parallel sides and one pair that is not parallel. Textbooks do not all use the same definition, so use the one in your learner's class.

Anything that is true for every member of a family is true for the smaller families inside it. Every rectangle has 4 right angles, so every square does too.

### Solid shapes

A **face** is a flat surface, an **edge** is where two faces meet, and a **vertex** is a corner where edges meet.

| Solid | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cube | 6 | 12 | 8 |
| Rectangular prism (like a cereal box) | 6 | 12 | 8 |
| Triangular prism | 5 | 9 | 6 |
| Square pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |

Cylinders, cones, and spheres have curved surfaces, so the face-edge-vertex count above does not apply to them in the same way.

### Symmetry

A **line of symmetry** splits a shape into two halves that match exactly when folded on the line. A rectangle that is not a square has 2 lines of symmetry. A square has 4. An equilateral triangle has 3. A circle has more than you could ever count: every line through its center works.

### The coordinate plane

A **coordinate plane** is made from two number lines that cross at a right angle. The flat one is the **x-axis**, the upright one is the **y-axis**, and they cross at the **origin**, (0, 0).

A point's address is an **ordered pair** (x, y). Start at the origin, move **across** x units, then **up** y units. The order matters: (3, 5) and (5, 3) are different points.

On the coordinate grid in this lesson, both numbers are 0 or more, so every point is in the upper-right part of the plane, called the **first quadrant**. The axes keep going past 0 in the other directions with negative numbers, which make three more quadrants. Points like (−2, 4) are studied in later grades: negative numbers in [13.2 - The Number System](/132-the-number-system) and all four quadrants in [13.6 - Geometry Foundations](/136-geometry-foundations).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Is it a rectangle? Is it a rhombus?

*Illustrative example.* A shape has 4 sides, each 5 cm long, and every corner fits the corner of a sheet of paper.

1. It has 4 sides, so it is a quadrilateral.
2. Opposite sides are parallel (they run in the same direction), so it is a parallelogram.
3. All 4 angles are right angles, so it is a rectangle.
4. All 4 sides are equal, so it is a rhombus.
5. Both together make it a square. The most exact name is "square," but "rectangle," "rhombus," "parallelogram," and "quadrilateral" are all true too.

### Example 2: Counting the parts of a triangular prism

1. Hold a model or draw it: two triangles at the ends joined by three rectangles.
2. **Faces:** 2 triangles + 3 rectangles = 5 faces.
3. **Vertices:** each triangle has 3 corners, and there are 2 triangles: 3 × 2 = 6 vertices.
4. **Edges:** 3 edges on each triangle (3 × 2 = 6) plus 3 edges joining the triangles: 6 + 3 = 9 edges.
5. Check with a pattern that works for prisms and pyramids like these: vertices − edges + faces = 6 − 9 + 5 = 2. The cube gives 8 − 12 + 6 = 2 as well. If the result is not 2 for one of these shapes, something was miscounted.

### Example 3: Plotting points and finding a distance

*Illustrative example.* On a map grid, the school is at (2, 3) and the library is at (7, 3).

1. School: from the origin, move across 2 and up 3. Mark the point.
2. Library: move across 7 and up 3. Mark the point.
3. Both points have the same y-coordinate, 3, so they are on the same horizontal line.
4. The distance between them along the grid is 7 − 2 = 5 units.
5. A park is 4 units straight up from the school. Its x-coordinate stays 2 and its y-coordinate becomes 3 + 4 = 7: the park is at (2, 7).

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A square is not a rectangle."** A rectangle only needs 4 right angles. A square has them, so it is a special rectangle.
- **"Turning a shape changes it."** A square turned on its corner is still a square, even though some people call it a "diamond." Check the sides and angles, not how it is sitting.
- **Swapping the numbers in an ordered pair.** (3, 5) is across 3, up 5. Saying "across the hall, then up the stairs" can help the learner remember the order.
- **Counting grid lines instead of moves.** From 0 to 3 on an axis is 3 moves. Start counting after leaving the origin, not at the origin.
- **Thinking every line through the middle is a line of symmetry.** For a rectangle that is not a square, the diagonal is not a line of symmetry: fold along it and the corners do not match.

## Practice

1. Draw a quadrilateral that has exactly one pair of parallel sides. What is it called in this lesson?
2. True or false: every rhombus is a square. Explain.
3. A triangle has sides 4 cm, 4 cm, and 6 cm. Is it equilateral, isosceles, or scalene?
4. Count the faces, edges, and vertices of a square pyramid. Check with vertices − edges + faces.
5. How many lines of symmetry does each have: (a) the capital letter A, (b) the capital letter H, (c) a square?
6. Plot A(1, 4), B(5, 4), C(5, 1), and D(1, 1), then connect them in order. What shape is it, and what are its perimeter and area in grid units?
7. Which point is 3 units to the right of (4, 2)? Which is 2 units down from (4, 2)?
