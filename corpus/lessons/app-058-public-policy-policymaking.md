# APP-05.8 — Public Policy & Policymaking

**Public policy** is what government chooses to do, or not to do, about a public problem: a law requiring seat belts, a tax credit, a grant program for schools, a regulation on drinking water, or a treaty. Policies are made by all three branches and at every level of government. This final lesson in the course pulls the earlier ones together. Congress (Lesson 5.3), the president and agencies (Lesson 5.4), courts (Lesson 5.5), and citizens and interest groups (Lesson 5.7) all play a part in turning a problem into a policy.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). It describes institutions and processes, not which policies are best. Economic examples are simplified to explain who decides what; they are not financial advice or forecasts.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Define **public policy**, classify policies by type, and identify the **policy tool** used.
2. Apply the **policy process** model to a policy and explain its limits.
3. Explain the **budget calendar**, **appropriations**, and **mandatory** versus **discretionary** spending, and distinguish **fiscal** from **monetary** policy.
4. Describe the main **foreign policy** institutions and how power over foreign affairs is shared.

## Core ideas

### Types of policy and policy tools

| Type | Examples | Main institutions |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic and social | Education, health care, transportation, criminal justice, environment | Congress, federal agencies, states and localities |
| Economic | Taxes and spending (fiscal policy), interest rates and money (monetary policy), regulation of business | Congress, the president, Treasury, the Federal Reserve, regulatory agencies |
| Foreign and defense | Diplomacy, treaties, trade agreements, military action, foreign aid | President, Congress, State Department, Department of Defense, National Security Council |

Governments use a handful of **tools**: laws that require or forbid behavior; **spending**, including grants to states; **taxes** and tax credits that encourage or discourage behavior; **regulations** written by agencies under authority from Congress; and **court decisions** that interpret laws and the Constitution. Because of federalism (Lesson 5.2), many national programs are carried out partly by state and local governments.

### The policy process model

Political scientists often describe policymaking in stages:

1. **Agenda setting:** a problem gains attention from officials and the public through events, data, media coverage, or advocacy.
2. **Formulation:** officials, experts, and interest groups develop options; congressional committees hold hearings; agencies analyze costs and effects.
3. **Adoption:** an authoritative decision is made, such as Congress passing and the president signing a law, an agency issuing a final rule, or a court ruling.
4. **Implementation:** agencies (and often states) carry out the policy: writing rules, issuing grants, enforcing requirements.
5. **Evaluation:** officials, auditors, researchers, and the public judge whether the policy works, which can put it back on the agenda for change.

This is a **model**, a simplified picture. Real policymaking often skips stages, runs several at once, or moves backward. It is still useful because it tells you which questions to ask at each point.

### How agencies make rules

When Congress passes a law, it often leaves details to an agency. Under the Administrative Procedure Act (5 U.S.C. § 553), an agency generally must publish a "general notice of proposed rule making" in the **Federal Register**, "give interested persons an opportunity to participate in the rule making through submission of written data, views, or arguments," explain the final rule's "basis and purpose," and publish a final substantive rule "not less than 30 days before its effective date," with listed exceptions. Anyone may submit a comment, which makes rulemaking one of the most direct ways the public can participate in policy.

### Fiscal policy: taxing, spending, and the budget

**Fiscal policy** is government decisions about taxes and spending. It belongs to Congress, with the president's participation:

- The Constitution gives Congress the power "To lay and collect Taxes" (Article I, Section 8) and says "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law" (Article I, Section 9).
- The federal **fiscal year** "begins on October 1 of each year and ends on September 30 of the following year" (31 U.S.C. § 1102). Fiscal year 2027, for example, runs from October 1, 2026, to September 30, 2027.
- The **president's budget** is a proposal. By law, "on or after the first Monday in January but not later than the first Monday in February of each year, the President shall submit a budget of the United States Government for the following fiscal year" (31 U.S.C. § 1105). The Office of Management and Budget, part of the Executive Office of the President, prepares it.
- **Congress's budget process** comes from the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, which declares it essential "to assure effective congressional control over the budgetary process" and "to provide for the congressional determination each year of the appropriate level of Federal revenues and expenditures" (2 U.S.C. § 621).
- **Appropriations** are "a provision of funds for federal agencies to make payments out of the Treasury for specified purposes." An **authorization** "establishes the terms and conditions under which the program or agency operates" (U.S. Senate glossary). A program usually needs both.
- If regular appropriations are not enacted by October 1, Congress can pass a **continuing resolution**, which provides funding "to continue operating in the absence of regular appropriations" (Senate glossary).

The Treasury's Fiscal Data site explains the two main kinds of spending. **Mandatory spending**, such as Social Security and Medicare, "represents nearly two-thirds of annual federal spending" and "does not require an annual vote by Congress" because it is set by existing law. **Discretionary spending** is "voted on in the annual appropriations process."

### Monetary policy: the Federal Reserve

**Monetary policy** is the management of interest rates and the money supply. It belongs to the **Federal Reserve** (the Fed), the nation's central bank, not to Congress or the president.

- **Goals:** "Congress has assigned the Fed to conduct the nation's monetary policy to support the goals of maximum employment and stable prices," often called the **dual mandate**. The statute lists "maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates" (12 U.S.C. § 225a).
- **Decision-makers:** the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) "consists of twelve members—the seven members of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System; the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York; and four of the remaining eleven Reserve Bank presidents," who serve in rotation.
- **Main tool:** the Fed "changes the stance of monetary policy primarily by raising or lowering its target range for the federal funds rate," an interest rate for overnight borrowing by banks. Lowering it is "easing"; raising it is "tightening," which "may be necessary if the economy is overheating or inflation is too high."
- **Independence and accountability:** Congress specifies the goals but "has also provided the Federal Reserve operational independence," and the Fed is accountable to Congress.

### Foreign policy institutions

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| President | Commander in chief; negotiates treaties; nominates ambassadors; receives foreign ambassadors; makes executive agreements |
| Congress | Declares war; raises and funds the military; regulates foreign commerce; the Senate approves treaties (two-thirds of senators present) and confirms ambassadors |
| Department of State | Conducts diplomacy; led by the secretary of state |
| Department of Defense | Directs the armed forces; a 2025 executive order authorized "Department of War" as a secondary title, while statutory references to the Department of Defense remain |
| National Security Council (NSC) | Created by the National Security Act of 1947; includes the president, vice president, secretary of state, secretary of defense, and others; its staff coordinates foreign-policy advice for the president |
| Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) | Also created by the 1947 Act; "the primary civilian intelligence-gathering organization in the government" (State Department Office of the Historian) |

The State Department historian notes that "each President has accorded the NSC with different degrees of importance," so how these institutions work together depends partly on the president.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Tracing an illustrative policy through the stages

**Illustrative problem:** news reports and a parent group raise concerns that drivers are illegally passing stopped school buses.

| Stage | What happens (illustrative) | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Agenda setting | A local news series and a petition bring the issue to a state legislator | Why did this issue get attention now, and who raised it? |
| Formulation | A legislative committee holds a hearing; options include higher fines, stop-arm cameras, and driver education | What options were considered, and what evidence supported each? |
| Adoption | The legislature passes and the governor signs a bill allowing districts to install cameras | Who had to agree, and what compromises were made? |
| Implementation | The state transportation agency writes rules on camera standards; districts decide whether to participate | Who carries it out, and with what money? |
| Evaluation | After a set period, the agency reports violation data to the legislature | What counts as success, and who measures it? |

**Reasoning:** the stages show that passing a law is the middle of the process, not the end. A policy can fail at implementation if no agency funds or enforces it. Nothing in this example describes a real state's law.

### Example 2: Fiscal or monetary?

| Action | Type | Who decides | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Congress passes a tax cut | Fiscal | Congress and the president | Taxes are fiscal |
| The FOMC raises the target range for the federal funds rate | Monetary | Federal Reserve | Interest-rate policy is the Fed's main tool |
| Congress appropriates money for highway construction | Fiscal | Congress and the president | Spending through appropriations |
| The president proposes a budget increasing defense spending | Fiscal (a proposal) | President proposes; Congress decides | The budget is a request until Congress appropriates |
| The FOMC lowers its target range because the economy is slowing | Monetary | Federal Reserve | Easing |

**Common trap:** presidents and members of Congress often comment on interest rates, but under the law the FOMC sets the target range.

### Example 3: A rulemaking timeline

**Illustrative case:** an agency publishes a final substantive rule in the Federal Register on March 2. No exception applies.

1. The rule must be published "not less than 30 days before its effective date."
2. March 2 + 30 days: 29 remaining days in March brings the count to March 31; one more day is **April 1**.
3. So the earliest effective date is **April 1**. The agency may choose a later date.
4. **Earlier steps:** before the final rule, the agency had to publish a proposed rule and accept comments. If it skipped those steps without a valid exception, a court could set the rule aside.

### Example 4: Reading the budget calendar

**Question:** for fiscal year 2027, when did the president's budget have to be submitted, and when did the fiscal year begin?

1. A budget is submitted for "the following fiscal year." FY2027 begins October 1, 2026, so its budget was due during calendar year 2026.
2. The deadline is "not later than the first Monday in February." In 2026, February 1 fell on a Sunday, so the first Monday was **February 2, 2026**.
3. FY2027 began **October 1, 2026**, and ends September 30, 2027.
4. If Congress had not enacted all regular appropriations by October 1, a continuing resolution could keep agencies operating. Check Congress.gov or the Senate and House appropriations committees for what actually happened in a given year.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The president controls interest rates."** The FOMC sets the target range for the federal funds rate; Congress gave the Fed operational independence.
- **"The president's budget is the budget."** It is a proposal. Congress decides through appropriations and other laws.
- **"Fiscal policy and monetary policy are the same."** Fiscal policy is taxing and spending by Congress and the president; monetary policy is interest rates and money by the Fed.
- **"Once a law passes, the policy is finished."** Implementation and evaluation often determine whether the policy works and whether it changes.
- **"Agencies can make any rule they want."** Agencies need authority from a statute and must generally follow notice-and-comment procedures; courts can review whether they did.
- **"Mandatory spending can never change."** It does not need an annual vote, but Congress can change the laws that create it.
- **"The president makes foreign policy alone."** Congress declares war, funds the military, regulates foreign commerce, and the Senate approves treaties and confirms ambassadors.
- **Boundary: numbers and offices change.** Budget totals, interest rates, agency names, and NSC membership change over time. Use the date when you report any figure.
