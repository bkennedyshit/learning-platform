# 19.7 - Cold War America

After 1945 the United States and the Soviet Union, wartime allies, became rivals in a "Cold War" that lasted until 1991. It was "cold" because the two superpowers never fought each other directly, but it shaped wars in Korea and Vietnam, an arms race with nuclear weapons, and fears of communism at home. At the same time, Black Americans led a movement that ended legal segregation and won federal protection of voting rights. By the 1970s, Vietnam and Watergate had shaken many Americans' trust in their government.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). Its main skill is **contextualization**: placing an event in the larger situation of its time to explain it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain **containment** and its early tools, and how later crises raised the stakes.
2. Analyze the **Second Red Scare** as a conflict between security and civil liberties.
3. Trace the **civil rights movement**'s strategies and results.
4. Explain how **Vietnam** and **Watergate** changed trust in government.

## Core ideas

### Containment

In 1947, diplomat George F. Kennan argued in the anonymous "X-Article" that U.S. policy toward the Soviet Union "must be that of a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies" (Office of the Historian). **Containment** became the basic U.S. strategy for the Cold War.

| Year | Action | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1947 | **Truman Doctrine** (March 12) | Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars in aid for Greece and Turkey: "It must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." |
| 1948 | **Marshall Plan** (signed April 3) | Economic aid to rebuild Europe; over the next four years, Congress appropriated 13.3 billion dollars for European recovery (National Archives) |
| 1948–1949 | **Berlin Airlift** | When Soviet forces blockaded West Berlin starting June 24, 1948, the Western allies supplied the city by air |
| 1949 | **NATO** | "The first peacetime military alliance the United States entered into outside of the Western Hemisphere" |
| 1950–1953 | **Korean War** | Fighting erupted on June 25, 1950, between communist North Korea and U.S.-backed South Korea; U.S. and UN forces fought for the South; an armistice was signed July 27, 1953; more than 30,000 Americans and millions of Koreans died (*American Yawp*) |
| 1957 | **Sputnik** (October 4) | The first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union; it "fed fears that the U.S. military had generally fallen behind" |
| 1962 | **Cuban Missile Crisis** (October) | Soviet nuclear missiles discovered in Cuba; "the moment when the two superpowers came closest to nuclear conflict" |

In his farewell address on January 17, 1961, President Dwight Eisenhower, a former five-star general, warned against the influence of a "military-industrial complex."

### The Second Red Scare

Real Soviet espionage cases and the shock of the Soviet atomic bomb (1949) fed fear of communists inside the government. On February 9, 1950, in Wheeling, West Virginia, Senator Joseph McCarthy claimed: "I have here in my hand a list of 205 ... names that were made known to the Secretary of State as being members of the Communist party." He "had no actual list, when pressed, the number changed" (*American Yawp*). His accusations damaged careers and reputations for four years. On December 2, 1954, the Senate condemned McCarthy, 67–22, for "abuse and noncooperation" with the subcommittee that had investigated his conduct in 1952 and for abuse of the select committee studying his censure (U.S. Senate).

### The civil rights movement

| Year | Event | Strategy | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1948 | Executive Order 9981 (July 26) | Pressure on the president | Banned segregation in the armed forces |
| 1954 | ***Brown v. Board of Education*** (May 17) | Litigation by the NAACP | A unanimous Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional, overruling *Plessy*'s "separate but equal" |
| 1955–1956 | **Montgomery bus boycott** | Mass economic boycott, sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest on December 1, 1955 | After 13 months, the Supreme Court affirmed *Browder v. Gayle*, striking down bus segregation; the boycott ended December 20, 1956 |
| 1957 | Little Rock Central High School | Students integrating a school under threat | Executive Order 10730 (September 23) sent federal troops to protect integration |
| 1961 | **Freedom Rides** (May–December) | Bus trips organized by CORE to challenge segregated interstate buses and terminals | Riders met violent attacks; national attention |
| 1963 | **Birmingham campaign** and King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (April 16) | Nonviolent direct action | National pressure for a civil rights law |
| 1963 | **March on Washington** (August 28) | Mass demonstration of more than 250,000 people | King's "I Have a Dream" speech |
| 1964 | **Civil Rights Act** (July 2) | Legislation | Banned discrimination in public places and employment; supported school integration |
| 1965 | **Selma to Montgomery** marches (March) | Marches for voting rights; on "Bloody Sunday," March 7, about 600 marchers were attacked at the Edmund Pettus Bridge | National outrage |
| 1965 | **Voting Rights Act** (August 6) | Legislation | Outlawed literacy tests and other discriminatory voting practices, "95 years after" the 15th Amendment |

President Lyndon Johnson's "Great Society" also created **Medicare** and **Medicaid** (July 30, 1965). Other movements followed the civil rights model, including campaigns by women, Mexican Americans, Native Americans, and environmentalists; the Environmental Protection Agency began operating in December 1970.

### Vietnam

After the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, Vietnam was divided, and the United States supported South Vietnam against a communist insurgency.

1. **Tonkin Gulf Resolution (August 7, 1964).** After President Johnson reported attacks on U.S. ships, Congress approved "all necessary measures to repel any armed attack," with only two senators voting no. It became "the legal basis" for the war under Johnson and Nixon.
2. **Escalation and the Tet Offensive (January 1968).** Communist forces attacked more than one hundred sites in South Vietnam, including the U.S. embassy in Saigon. U.S. forces pushed them back, but Tet exposed a "credibility gap" between official claims and reality (*American Yawp*).
3. **Vietnamization.** President Nixon withdrew U.S. troops while building up South Vietnam's army. The peace agreement was signed January 27, 1973.
4. **The end.** On April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks entered the Presidential Palace in Saigon, "effectively ending the war." The National Archives records 58,220 U.S. military fatal casualties.

### Détente and Watergate

In the early 1970s Nixon pursued **détente** (a relaxation of tensions): he visited China in February 1972 and signed arms-control agreements with the Soviet Union (SALT I). At home, five men were arrested inside Democratic National Committee offices at the Watergate complex on June 17, 1972. Investigations revealed a cover-up. In ***United States v. Nixon*** (1974), the Supreme Court ruled 8–0 that the president had to turn over tape recordings. After the House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment, Nixon resigned, "the first and only American president to resign from office" (*American Yawp*). Congress had already passed the **War Powers Resolution** (1973) to limit the president's power to wage war without its consent.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Contextualizing the Truman Doctrine

1. **Immediate context:** on February 21, 1947, Britain told the United States it could no longer aid Greece and Turkey. Greece faced a communist-led insurgency; Turkey faced Soviet pressure over the Dardanelles (National Archives).
2. **Broader context:** Soviet control of Eastern Europe and Kennan's containment argument.
3. **The text:** "support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures."
4. **Why it mattered:** the language was general, not limited to two countries. The National Archives notes that the doctrine and the related "domino theory" "would guide U.S. foreign policy around the world for the next 40 years," including in Vietnam.

**Takeaway:** context explains why a specific aid request became a global policy.

### Example 2: Comparing two civil rights strategies

| | Litigation (*Brown*, 1954) | Direct action (Birmingham, 1963) |
|---|---|---|
| Who acted | NAACP lawyers and plaintiffs | Local activists, students, clergy, King and the SCLC |
| Method | Court cases challenging laws | Marches, sit-ins, boycotts, accepting arrest |
| Strength | Changed the law of the land | Created a crisis that forced negotiation and drew national attention |
| Limit | Enforcement depended on others; Little Rock required federal troops | Exposed participants, including children, to violence and jail |

In his letter, King answered eight Birmingham clergymen who had called the campaign "unwise and untimely." He wrote: "For years now, I have heard the word 'Wait!' ... This 'Wait' has almost always meant 'Never.'" He explained that the purpose of direct action was to create a crisis situation out of which negotiation could emerge.

**Claim:** the strategies worked together: court victories established rights, and direct action pressured governments to enforce them and Congress to pass the 1964 and 1965 laws.

### Example 3: The Tonkin Gulf Resolution as evidence

1. **What Congress was told:** U.S. ships were attacked twice, on August 2 and August 4, 1964.
2. **What later came out:** the *Maddox* was on an intelligence mission supporting South Vietnamese raids, which "wasn't publicly known at the time"; after the reported second attack, its captain cabled that "freak weather effects on radar and overeager sonarmen may have accounted for many reports," but Defense Secretary McNamara did not pass his doubts to the president (National Archives).
3. **Consequence:** Congress repealed the resolution in January 1971 "in an attempt to curtail President Nixon's power to continue the war," and passed the War Powers Resolution in 1973.
4. **Reasoning:** decisions made on incomplete or misleading information can grant broad powers that are hard to take back. This is one reason the "credibility gap" grew.

### Example 4: McCarthyism: security versus liberty

1. **The security concern:** Soviet espionage was real, and the Soviets tested an atomic bomb in 1949.
2. **McCarthy's method:** sweeping accusations with changing numbers and no list.
3. **The cost:** accusations damaged careers; many people lost jobs without proof of wrongdoing.
4. **The response:** the Senate, a political body, condemned him in 1954.

**Claim:** a real threat does not justify every response to it. Historians evaluate Cold War security measures by asking whether they targeted real dangers and whether they respected due process.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Cold War never involved fighting."** The superpowers never fought each other directly, but millions died in wars in Korea and Vietnam and in other conflicts connected to the Cold War.
- **"McCarthy started the Red Scare."** Fear of communism and loyalty investigations began before 1950; McCarthy exploited and intensified them.
- **"The civil rights movement was Martin Luther King Jr. alone."** Thousands of local leaders and organizations, including the Women's Political Council in Montgomery, CORE, SNCC, and the NAACP, planned and carried out the movement.
- **"*Brown* desegregated schools immediately."** The Court later ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed," and many districts resisted for years.
- **"The Tet Offensive was a military victory for North Vietnam."** U.S. and South Vietnamese forces repelled the attacks, but Tet badly damaged public trust in official claims about the war. Distinguish military and political results.
- **"Nixon was impeached."** The House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment, but Nixon resigned before the full House voted.
- **Boundary: "the 1950s were calm."** The decade included the Korean War, McCarthyism, *Brown*, Montgomery, and Little Rock.

## Practice

4. Explain what King meant by "This 'Wait' has almost always meant 'Never.'" Who was he responding to?
