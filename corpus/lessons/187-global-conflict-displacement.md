# 18.7 - Global Conflict & Displacement

The twentieth century was the deadliest in human history. Two world wars killed tens of millions of soldiers and civilians. Governments carried out genocides against people they defined as enemies. Empires that had ruled much of the world for centuries collapsed, and dozens of new nations were born. Throughout, war, persecution, and new borders forced huge numbers of people from their homes. This lesson follows these connected events from 1914 to the end of the Cold War.

This lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its historical-thinking skill is **contextualization**: explaining an event by placing it in the conditions of its time, without excusing it.

**Content note:** this lesson discusses war, genocide, and mass killing, including the Holocaust and the Armenian genocide. It uses factual summaries and avoids graphic description. Take breaks if you need them, and talk with a teacher or trusted adult if the material is distressing.

## Prerequisites

- [18.6 - Industrialization & Imperialism](/186-industrialization-imperialism), especially nationalism, imperial rivalry, and industrial weapons.
- Helpful: [19.6 - Depression & World Wars](/196-depression-world-wars) and [19.7 - Cold War America](/197-cold-war-america), which cover the same decades from the U.S. perspective. This lesson emphasizes global events.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain the long-term causes and the immediate trigger of **World War I** and analyze **Article 231** of the Treaty of Versailles as a primary source.
2. Trace how the **Russian Revolution**, the **Great Depression**, and dissatisfaction with the 1919 peace settlement contributed to the rise of authoritarian regimes and **World War II**.
3. Define **genocide** using the 1948 Genocide Convention and describe the **Armenian genocide** and the **Holocaust** with figures from the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum.
4. Explain how the **United Nations**, **decolonization**, and the **Cold War** reshaped the world after 1945, using UN data on the population of dependent territories.

## Core ideas

### World War I (1914–1918)

**Long-term causes.** Historians commonly group them under four headings:

| Cause | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Alliances** | By the 1900s, alliances "had solidified two opposed groups," so a local war could draw in all the great powers |
| **Imperial rivalry** | Competition for colonies "destabilized the balance of power" (18.6) |
| **Nationalism** | Minority groups in Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire sought their own states; Serbian nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary in the Balkans |
| **Militarism** | Great powers competed to build armies and navies |

**Trigger.** On **June 28, 1914**, Archduke **Franz Ferdinand**, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, was assassinated in **Sarajevo**, the capital of Bosnia-Herzegovina, which Austria-Hungary had taken over in 1908. Austria-Hungary's response against Serbia set the alliance system in motion.

**Total war.** Industrial weapons produced unprecedented losses. OpenStax reports that "more than 8.5 million soldiers died in the war, and twenty-one million were wounded," with civilian deaths and injuries "also in the millions." Counting the killed, wounded, captured, and missing, Russia had more than nine million casualties, France more than six million, and Germany and Austria-Hungary more than seven million each. The war's mass troop movements also helped spread the 1918–1919 influenza pandemic, which killed tens of millions worldwide.

**Collapse of empires.** Four empires fell: the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, and Ottoman. In **1917**, in the October Revolution, **Vladimir Lenin** and the **Bolsheviks** seized power in Russia, and in March 1918 they signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany to leave the war. The armistice on the western front took effect on **November 11, 1918**.

**The peace settlement.** At Versailles in 1919, U.S. President Woodrow Wilson promoted his **Fourteen Points**, including **self-determination** (the idea that each ethnic group should have its own government) and a **League of Nations**. New states appeared, including Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia, but their borders, drawn by diplomats in Paris, "did not always reflect where people of different nationalities lived." Britain and France also demanded **reparations**, which "totaled over 30 billion dollars in 1919 dollars."

### Between the wars

- **Great Depression:** the economic collapse that began in the United States in **1929** "sent the entire world's economy reeling." Unemployment and hunger spread, and many people lost faith in democratic governments.
- **Authoritarian regimes:** in Italy, **Benito Mussolini** rose partly "by denouncing the terms of the Treaty of Versailles." In Germany, the economic crisis helped Adolf Hitler's National Socialists (Nazis) take power in 1933. In the Soviet Union, Joseph Stalin ruled through terror. These regimes differed in ideology but all sought total control of society.
- **Appeasement:** at Munich in September 1938, Britain and France allowed Germany to take part of Czechoslovakia, hoping Hitler would be satisfied. He was not.

### World War II (1939–1945)

Germany invaded Poland in 1939, and the war expanded to Europe, North Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. OpenStax calls it "perhaps history's most globalizing event" and "the deadliest conflict in history," with millions of noncombatant victims. The United States dropped atomic bombs on **Hiroshima** on August 6, 1945, and on **Nagasaki** three days later; Japan surrendered soon after.

### Genocide

The word **genocide** was coined in **1944** by the Polish lawyer **Raphael Lemkin**, partly in response to Nazi crimes and to the earlier mass murder of Armenians. The 1948 **Genocide Convention** defines it as acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such," including killing members of the group.

| Genocide | Key facts (U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum unless noted) |
|---|---|
| **Armenian genocide** (1915–1916) | About 1.5 million Armenians lived in the Ottoman Empire in 1915. Beginning in spring 1915, the Ottoman government deported Armenians from nearly every province. "At least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million Armenians were killed," through massacres, starvation, and exposure. It is sometimes called the first genocide of the twentieth century |
| **The Holocaust** (1933–1945) | "The systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by the Nazi German regime and its allies and collaborators." During the war they killed "nearly two out of every three European Jews." The Nazis also targeted other groups for persecution and mass murder, including "Soviet POWs, ethnic Poles, Roma, and people with disabilities" |
| **Rwanda** (1994) | Beginning on April 6, 1994, extremists among the Hutu majority carried out a planned campaign against Tutsi and moderate Hutu. The UN's outreach program states that "more than one million people are estimated to have perished"; other widely cited estimates are lower, around 800,000, so report the range and name your source |

### After 1945: the UN, decolonization, and the Cold War

The **United Nations** officially came into existence on **October 24, 1945**, after delegates from 50 countries met in San Francisco. Its Charter begins, "WE THE PEOPLES OF THE UNITED NATIONS DETERMINED to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our lifetime has brought untold sorrow to mankind."

**Decolonization** followed rapidly. India became independent in **1947**, in a partition into India and Pakistan that forced millions of people to flee across the new borders. **Ghana** became independent on **March 6, 1957**, under Kwame Nkrumah, and many more African states followed; in **Algeria**, war against France lasted from 1954 to 1962. According to the UN, when it was founded in 1945, "some 750 million people, nearly a third of the world's population, lived in Territories that were dependent on colonial Powers." Today 17 Non-Self-Governing Territories remain, with fewer than 2 million people.

The **Cold War** (about 1947–1991) was a global rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union. It was "cold" in Europe, where the two sides did not fight directly, but hot elsewhere: wars in Korea and Vietnam (where more than one million people died) and conflicts across Africa, Asia, and Latin America were shaped by superpower competition.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles

> "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies."

1. **Meet the document:** Article 231 opens Part VIII, titled "Reparation," of the treaty signed on June 28, 1919.
2. **Observe the wording:** Germany "accepts the responsibility ... for causing all the loss and damage" due to "the aggression of Germany and her allies."
3. **Purpose:** this clause gave the legal basis for reparations. Britain and France "wanted Germany to literally pay for" the war.
4. **Context:** Germany had not been occupied or forced to surrender (the war ended in an armistice), so many Germans did not believe they had lost decisively. That made the clause especially resented, and it became known as the "war guilt" clause.
5. **Use as evidence:** it shows the victors' view of responsibility and their demand for payment. It does not settle the historical question of who caused the war, which historians still debate.

### Example 2: Contextualizing appeasement without excusing it

**Question:** Why did Britain and France agree to the Munich Pact in 1938?

1. **Conditions of the time:** memories of World War I's millions of dead; economic weakness during the Depression; the belief of some leaders that parts of the 1919 settlement were unfair to Germany.
2. **Their stated hope:** that Hitler "would be satisfied and cease to be aggressive."
3. **What happened:** Germany broke the agreement, and Britain and France then pledged to defend Poland.
4. **Contextualize vs. excuse:** context explains **why** leaders chose appeasement. It does not mean the choice was right, and later leaders have often invoked Munich as a warning.
5. **Write it:** "Shaped by the memory of World War I and the Depression, British and French leaders hoped concessions would prevent war, but Hitler's later aggression showed that the policy had failed."

### Example 3: Working with genocide statistics carefully

**USHMM data:** about 1.5 million Armenians lived in the Ottoman Empire in 1915; at least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million were killed.

1. **Calculate the range:** 664,000 ÷ 1,500,000 ≈ 44 percent; 1,200,000 ÷ 1,500,000 = 80 percent.
2. **Report it honestly:** "Between about 44 and 80 percent of the Ottoman Empire's Armenians were killed, according to USHMM estimates."
3. **Why a range:** records were destroyed or never kept, and deaths from starvation and exposure during deportations are hard to count.
4. **What the range does not mean:** uncertainty about the exact number does not create uncertainty about whether the killing happened or was intended. The evidence of deliberate destruction is extensive, which is why USHMM and the legal definition of genocide apply.

### Example 4: Decolonization by the numbers

**UN data:** in 1945, about 750 million people, "nearly a third of the world's population," lived in dependent territories; today, fewer than 2 million do.

1. **Estimate the world population in 1945:** if 750 million was nearly one-third, the total was a little over 2.25 billion (750 million × 3 = 2.25 billion).
2. **Measure the change:** from 750 million to fewer than 2 million is a decline of more than 99 percent (2 ÷ 750 ≈ 0.27 percent remain).
3. **Interpret:** within a few decades, formal colonial rule over hundreds of millions of people ended.
4. **Limit the claim:** the numbers measure formal political status. They do not show whether new states were free of outside economic or political influence, or whether independence brought peace; Algeria's war and the partition of India show that it often did not.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"World War I was caused by one assassination."** The assassination was the trigger. Alliances, imperial rivalry, nationalism, and militarism explain why a regional crisis became a world war.
- **"The Treaty of Versailles alone caused World War II."** Resentment of the treaty mattered, but so did the Great Depression, the choices of aggressive regimes in Germany, Italy, and Japan, and the failure of other states to stop them.
- **"Contextualizing means justifying."** Explaining why people acted as they did is different from approving of their actions.
- **"Genocide means any large-scale killing."** The legal definition requires intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such. Other atrocities may be war crimes or crimes against humanity.
- **"The Holocaust happened suddenly."** USHMM describes it as "an evolving process" between 1933 and 1945 that became increasingly radical, beginning with persecution and ending in mass murder.
- **"Decolonization was peaceful and orderly."** Some transitions were negotiated, but others involved long wars (Algeria) or mass displacement (the partition of India).
- **Boundary: casualty figures vary by source.** Figures for World War I, the Rwandan genocide, and other events differ depending on what is counted and how. Always name the source and, when sources disagree, report the range.
