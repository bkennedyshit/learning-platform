# APP-02.7 — Measurement & Data

Measuring means asking "how many units fit?" A length is measured by how many unit lengths fit end to end, an area by how many unit squares cover a shape, and a volume by how many unit cubes fill a box. Once measurements are collected, graphs help us see and compare them. This lesson covers all three kinds of measuring plus line plots, bar graphs, and picture graphs.

The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-02.3 — Multiplication & Division](/app-023-multiplication-division): arrays and multiplication facts. From [APP-02.4 — Fractions — Concepts & Operations](/app-024-fractions-concepts-operations): halves, fourths, and eighths on a number line, and adding like fractions. From [APP-02.6 — Decimals & Place Value](/app-026-decimals-place-value): multiplying by 10, 100, and 1,000.

> **Adult note:** Materials: a ruler marked in inches and centimeters, a measuring tape, square sticky notes or paper squares for area, and small cubes (sugar cubes or building blocks) with a small box for volume. Household items to measure make the lesson concrete.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Measure correctly with a ruler and explain how the size of the unit changes the number.
2. Change a measurement from a bigger unit to a smaller one in the same system.
3. Find area and perimeter of rectangles and volume of boxes, with the correct units.
4. Make and read line plots, bar graphs, and picture graphs.

## Core ideas

### Measuring length

Put the **zero mark** of the ruler (not the end of the plastic, which may be blank) at one end of the object. Read the mark at the other end. Units must be laid end to end with no gaps and no overlaps.

The size of the unit matters. A table might be 2 meters long or 200 centimeters long. The length is the same, but because a centimeter is much smaller than a meter, more of them are needed. **Smaller unit, bigger number.**

### Units and conversions

| Metric | Customary (U.S.) |
|---|---|
| 1 kilometer (km) = 1,000 meters (m) | 1 foot (ft) = 12 inches (in) |
| 1 meter = 100 centimeters (cm) | 1 yard (yd) = 3 feet |
| 1 centimeter = 10 millimeters (mm) | 1 mile (mi) = 5,280 feet |
| 1 kilogram (kg) = 1,000 grams (g) | 1 pound (lb) = 16 ounces (oz) |
| 1 liter (L) = 1,000 milliliters (mL) | 1 gallon = 4 quarts; 1 quart = 2 pints; 1 pint = 2 cups |

To change a bigger unit into a smaller one, **multiply** by how many small units are in one big unit. Example: 3 feet = 3 × 12 = 36 inches. A conversion table helps see the pattern:

| Feet | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inches | 12 | 24 | 36 | 48 |

Metric units are all related by 10, 100, or 1,000, which is why metric conversions only move digits (see [APP-02.6 — Decimals & Place Value](/app-026-decimals-place-value)).

### Perimeter and area

**Perimeter** is the distance around a shape. Add all the side lengths. A rectangle 6 cm by 4 cm has perimeter 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20 cm, or 2 × (6 + 4) = 20 cm.

**Area** is how many unit squares cover the inside. Tiling the same rectangle with 1-centimeter squares gives 4 rows of 6, which is an array: 4 × 6 = 24 **square centimeters**. That is why area of a rectangle = length × width.

Perimeter is a length (units like cm). Area is a count of squares (units like square cm).

### Volume

**Volume** is how many unit cubes fill a solid with no gaps. A box 4 cubes long, 3 cubes wide, and 2 cubes tall holds 4 × 3 = 12 cubes in each layer and 2 layers: 12 × 2 = 24 **cubic units**. So volume of a rectangular box = length × width × height. If a shape is made of two boxes that do not overlap, add their volumes.

### Line plots, bar graphs, and picture graphs

- A **line plot** puts an X above a number line for each measurement. It is good for measurements like lengths to the nearest $\frac{1}{4}$ inch.
- A **bar graph** uses bars whose heights show how many are in each category. The **scale** may count by 1s, 2s, 5s, or more.
- A **picture graph** uses a symbol for a set number of things. The **key** tells what each picture stands for, for example "each star = 2 votes."

Always read the scale or key before reading the graph.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Same perimeter, different area

*Illustrative example.* A learner has 12 meters of fence to make a rectangular pen. Which whole-number rectangles can be made, and which holds the most space?

1. Perimeter 12 means length + width = 6 (half of 12).
2. Possible whole-number sides: 1 by 5, 2 by 4, 3 by 3.
3. Areas: 1 × 5 = 5, 2 × 4 = 8, 3 × 3 = 9 square meters.
4. All three pens use exactly 12 meters of fence, but the areas differ. The 3 by 3 square holds the most.
5. Lesson: the same perimeter does not mean the same area.

### Example 2: Converting and comparing

*Illustrative example.* One ribbon is 2 yards long and another is 70 inches long. Which is longer, and by how much?

1. Change yards to feet: 2 × 3 = 6 feet.
2. Change feet to inches: 6 × 12 = 72 inches.
3. Compare in the same unit: 72 inches and 70 inches.
4. The 2-yard ribbon is longer by 72 − 70 = 2 inches.

### Example 3: Reading a line plot with fractions

*Illustrative data.* A class measured 8 pencils to the nearest $\frac{1}{4}$ inch: 5, $5\tfrac{1}{4}$, $5\tfrac{1}{4}$, $5\tfrac{1}{2}$, $5\tfrac{1}{2}$, $5\tfrac{1}{2}$, $6$, $6\tfrac{1}{4}$.

1. Draw a number line from 5 to $6\tfrac{1}{4}$ marked in fourths, and put one X above each measurement. $5\tfrac{1}{2}$ gets 3 Xs.
2. Most common length: $5\tfrac{1}{2}$ inches (the tallest stack).
3. Difference between the longest and shortest: $6\tfrac{1}{4} - 5 = 1\tfrac{1}{4}$ inches.
4. Total length if all the $5\tfrac{1}{4}$-inch pencils were laid end to end: $5\tfrac{1}{4} + 5\tfrac{1}{4} = 10\tfrac{2}{4} = 10\tfrac{1}{2}$ inches.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **Starting at 1 on the ruler.** If an object starts at the 1 mark and ends at 7, it is 6 units long, not 7. Count the spaces (units), not the marks.
- **"A bigger unit gives a bigger number."** It is the opposite: 1 meter is 100 centimeters. When changing to a smaller unit, the number gets bigger, so multiply.
- **Mixing up area and perimeter.** Perimeter goes around the edge (a fence); area covers the inside (a carpet). Example 1 shows they can change independently.
- **Leaving off or mixing up units.** 24 for the rectangle above means 24 square centimeters, not 24 centimeters. Volume is in cubic units.
- **Ignoring the graph's scale or key.** If each star means 2 votes, 4 stars mean 8 votes, not 4. A bar that reaches halfway between 10 and 20 on a scale of tens shows 15.

## Practice

1. A crayon starts at the 2 cm mark and ends at the 11 cm mark. How long is it?
2. Convert: 4 meters to centimeters; 3 pounds to ounces; 2 gallons to quarts.
3. Find the perimeter and area of a rectangle 9 feet by 5 feet.
4. A rectangle has area 36 square units. Give two different whole-number side lengths it could have and the perimeter of each.
5. A box is 5 cm long, 2 cm wide, and 3 cm tall. How many 1-cm cubes fill it?
6. *Illustrative data:* A picture graph key says each apple picture stands for 5 apples. Row A has 4 apple pictures and Row B has 6. How many more apples are in Row B?
7. *Illustrative data:* Plant heights (inches): $2\tfrac{1}{2}$, 3, 3, $3\tfrac{1}{2}$, 4. Make a line plot in halves. What is the difference between the tallest and shortest?

<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. 11 − 2 = 9 cm.
2. 400 cm; 48 ounces; 8 quarts.
3. Perimeter 2 × (9 + 5) = 28 feet. Area 9 × 5 = 45 square feet.
4. Any two of: 1 by 36 (perimeter 74), 2 by 18 (40), 3 by 12 (30), 4 by 9 (26), 6 by 6 (24).
5. 5 × 2 = 10 cubes per layer, 3 layers: 30 cubes (30 cubic centimeters).
6. Row A: 4 × 5 = 20. Row B: 6 × 5 = 30. Row B has 10 more apples.
7. Number line from $2\tfrac{1}{2}$ to 4 in halves with one X at $2\tfrac{1}{2}$, two at 3, one at $3\tfrac{1}{2}$, one at 4. Difference: $4 - 2\tfrac{1}{2} = 1\tfrac{1}{2}$ inches.

**Adult self-check:** Ask "What unit?" for every answer. For conversions to a smaller unit, the new number must be larger. For area, the learner should be able to point to the unit squares; for volume, to the layers of cubes.
