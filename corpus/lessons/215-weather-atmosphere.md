# 21.5 - Weather & Atmosphere

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time: temperature, humidity, wind, clouds, precipitation, and pressure. **Climate** is the typical weather of a place over a long period. Both come from the same physics: the Sun heats Earth unevenly, warm air rises and cools, water vapor condenses, and air flows from high pressure toward low pressure. This lesson describes the atmosphere's structure, then air masses, fronts, and storms, and finishes with the difference between weather and climate.

## Core ideas

### Composition

NOAA lists dry air as about **78.08% nitrogen**, **20.95% oxygen**, and **0.93% argon**; together with carbon dioxide, these four gases make up 99.998% of dry air. Real air also contains **water vapor**, from near 0% in dry desert winds to about 4% in humid tropical air. Carbon dioxide is a small fraction, but it traps heat (lesson 21.8).

### Layers of the atmosphere

NOAA's JetStream describes five layers, separated by boundaries called "pauses":

| Layer | Approximate height | Temperature trend | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Troposphere** | Surface to about 18–20 km at the equator, 9 km at 50° latitude, 6 km at the poles | Falls with height (about 17 °C average at the surface to about −51 °C at the tropopause) | Almost all weather happens here |
| **Stratosphere** | Top of troposphere to about 50 km | Rises with height | Contains the ozone layer; ozone formation releases heat; little water vapor |
| **Mesosphere** | About 50–85 km | Falls with height | Meteors burn up here |
| **Thermosphere** | About 85–600 km | Rises sharply (up to about 2,000 °C) | So few molecules that it would not feel hot |
| **Exosphere** | About 600–10,000 km | — | Atoms escape to space; many satellites orbit here |

Because the stratosphere is warmer on top, rising air stops at its base. That is why tall thunderstorm clouds flatten into **anvil** shapes at the tropopause.

### Air pressure

Air pressure is the force of air molecules striking a surface. Standard sea-level pressure is **1013.25 millibars (mb)**, which equals 1013.25 hectopascals (hPa) or 29.92 inches of mercury. Pressure drops with height because there are fewer molecules above you: NOAA notes that **half** of the atmosphere's molecules lie below about **5.6 km (18,000 ft)**. Warm air is less dense than cold air. On weather maps, **H** marks high pressure (usually sinking air and fair weather) and **L** marks low pressure (rising air, clouds, and precipitation are common).

**Rising air cools.** A rising parcel of air expands as the pressure around it drops, and it cools. Unsaturated air cools at the **dry adiabatic lapse rate** of about **10 °C per kilometer**. Once it cools enough for water vapor to condense, it forms a cloud and then cools more slowly, because condensation releases heat.

### Air masses and fronts

An **air mass** is a huge body of air with fairly uniform temperature and humidity, picked up from its **source region**.

| Code | Name | Source and character |
|---|---|---|
| **cA** | continental arctic | Over the Arctic or Antarctic; very cold, dry |
| **cP** | continental polar | High-latitude land; cold, dry |
| **mP** | maritime polar | High-latitude ocean; cool, moist |
| **cT** | continental tropical | Low-latitude deserts; hot, dry |
| **mT** | maritime tropical | Warm tropical ocean; warm, moist |

A **front** is the boundary where two air masses meet. Cold air is denser, so it pushes under warm air and forces it upward.

- **Cold front:** colder air replaces warmer air. The steep slope lifts air abruptly, often producing a **narrow band of showers and thunderstorms**, followed by cooler, drier air and a change in wind direction.
- **Warm front:** warmer air replaces colder air. The gentle slope lifts air gradually, giving **widespread layered clouds and steady precipitation** ahead of the front.
- **Stationary front:** the boundary barely moves; clouds and precipitation can linger for days.
- **Occluded front:** in a mature low-pressure system, the faster cold front catches up with the warm front and lifts the warm air off the ground. The storm then gradually weakens.

### Storms

**Thunderstorms** need three ingredients: **moisture**, **instability** (air that keeps rising once nudged upward, such as warm, moist air near the surface under cold, dry air), and a **lifting mechanism** (a front, uneven heating of the ground, or air pushed up a mountain).

**Tornadoes** are violently rotating columns of air, descending from a thunderstorm and in contact with the ground. Most come from **supercells**, thunderstorms with a persistent rotating updraft. Tornado strength is rated on the Enhanced Fujita (EF) scale from damage.

**Tropical cyclones** form over warm tropical oceans:

| Stage | Maximum sustained wind |
|---|---|
| Tropical depression | 38 mph (61 km/h) or less |
| Tropical storm (named) | 39–73 mph |
| Hurricane (typhoon in the NW Pacific) | 74 mph (119 km/h) or more |

The **Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale** rates hurricanes 1–5 by **wind speed only**: Category 1 is 74–95 mph, 2 is 96–110 mph, 3 is 111–129 mph, 4 is 130–156 mph, and 5 is 157 mph or higher. Categories 3–5 are **major hurricanes**. The National Hurricane Center stresses that the scale does not include **storm surge, rainfall flooding, or tornadoes**, which can be deadly at any category.

### Measuring and mapping weather

Common instruments: a **thermometer** (temperature), **barometer** (pressure), **anemometer** (wind speed), **wind vane** (wind direction), **hygrometer** (humidity), and **rain gauge** (precipitation). Weather balloons, radar, and satellites add measurements aloft. Surface weather maps show pressure in millibars, H and L centers, and front symbols.

### Weather versus climate

NOAA explains that the basic difference is **time**. Weather can change hour to hour. **Climate** is the average or typical weather over years or longer. U.S. **climate normals** are 30-year averages; the current set covers **1991–2020**.

The **Köppen-Geiger** system groups climates mainly by temperature and precipitation. NOAA's summary:

- **A — Tropical:** every month averages above 18 °C (64 °F), with heavy annual rain.
- **B — Dry:** potential evaporation and transpiration exceed precipitation.
- **C — Moist mid-latitude, mild winters:** warm, humid summers and mild winters.
- **D — Moist continental:** warmest month above 10 °C (50 °F) and coldest month below −3 °C (27 °F); severe winters.
- **E — Polar:** warmest month below 10 °C (50 °F).
- **H — Highland:** climates controlled by elevation.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A rising air parcel

Air at the ground is 25 °C and unsaturated. It is lifted 1.5 km and has not yet formed a cloud. What is its temperature?

1. Rate: about 10 °C cooler per kilometer for unsaturated air.
2. Cooling: $10\ \tfrac{^\circ\text{C}}{\text{km}} \times 1.5\ \text{km} = 15\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
3. New temperature: $25 - 15 = 10\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
4. Boundary: if the parcel reached saturation partway up, it would form a cloud and cool more slowly above that height, so this simple rate would overestimate the cooling.

### Example 2: How much air is below the summit?

Mount Everest rises about 8.85 km above sea level. Using NOAA's statement that half of the atmosphere's molecules are below about 5.6 km, what can you say about a climber at the summit?

1. The summit (8.85 km) is higher than 5.6 km.
2. Therefore **more than half** of the atmosphere's molecules are below the climber.
3. Fewer molecules overhead means lower pressure, and each breath holds fewer oxygen molecules.
4. Units check: sea-level pressure is 1013.25 mb = 1013.25 hPa = 101.325 kPa (because 1 kPa = 10 hPa), so the pressure at the summit must be less than half of 101.325 kPa, or under about 51 kPa.

### Example 3: Gases in a room

*Illustrative example.* A room holds 50,000 liters of dry air. About how many liters are nitrogen, oxygen, and argon?

1. Nitrogen: $0.7808 \times 50{,}000 = 39{,}040$ L.
2. Oxygen: $0.2095 \times 50{,}000 = 10{,}475$ L.
3. Argon: $0.0093 \times 50{,}000 = 465$ L.
4. Check: $39{,}040 + 10{,}475 + 465 = 49{,}980$ L. The remaining 20 L (0.04%) is mostly carbon dioxide and trace gases. ✓

### Example 4: Rating a hurricane

A hurricane has maximum sustained winds of 120 mph. Classify it and state what the category does **not** tell you.

1. Compare with the scale: 111–129 mph is **Category 3**.
2. Category 3 or higher is a **major hurricane**.
3. Convert for an international report: 120 mph × 1.609 ≈ 193 km/h, inside the Category 3 range of 178–208 km/h. ✓
4. Limit: the category says nothing about storm surge or rainfall. The National Hurricane Center notes that hurricanes of every category can produce deadly storm surge and rain-induced floods.

### Example 5: Reading a forecast

*Illustrative forecast.* "A cold front will pass through this afternoon. Expect a line of thunderstorms, then clearing skies, a wind shift, and much cooler, drier air tonight."

1. Cold front → colder, denser air is replacing warm air.
2. The steep frontal slope lifts warm, moist air quickly → a **narrow line** of thunderstorms.
3. Behind the front, the new air mass (likely cP in North America) is **cooler and drier** → clearing skies.
4. A change in wind direction marks the front's passage.

### Example 6: Weather or climate?

In 2015, Dallas/Fort Worth Airport recorded 62.61 inches of rain. NOAA lists the climate normal as 36.14 inches.

1. Ratio: $62.61 \div 36.14 \approx 1.73$, so the year had about **173%** of normal rainfall.
2. A single extraordinary year is **weather** (and a record).
3. The 36.14-inch normal is **climate**: a 30-year average.
4. To claim the climate itself is getting wetter, you would need a trend over decades, not one year.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The thermosphere is the hottest layer, so it would feel hot."** Its molecules have high energy, but there are so few of them that they could not warm your skin.
- **"Weather and climate are the same."** A cold week does not disprove a warming climate, and a hot week does not prove one. Climate uses long-term averages.
- **"Opening windows protects a house from a tornado."** NOAA says this does nothing to reduce damage; even the strongest tornadoes do not make houses "explode" from low pressure. Use the time to take shelter.
- **"A Category 1 hurricane is not dangerous."** The scale rates wind only. Storm surge, rain flooding, and tornadoes can be deadly at any category.
- **"High pressure always means heat."** High pressure usually means sinking air and clear skies, which can be very cold in winter.
- **"Lightning only hits metal objects."** NOAA notes that lightning can strike any object in its path, not just metal, so standing away from metal does not make you safe outdoors.

## Practice

1. In which layer does almost all weather occur? In which layer do meteors burn up?
2. Why does a thunderstorm cloud flatten at the top?
3. Unsaturated air at 18 °C rises 0.8 km. Estimate its new temperature.
4. Name the air mass: (a) forms over the Gulf of Mexico in summer, (b) forms over northern Canada in winter.
5. Describe the clouds and precipitation you would expect ahead of a warm front, and along a cold front.
6. A tropical cyclone has 65 mph winds. What is it called? At what wind speed would it become a hurricane?
7. You see lightning and hear thunder 20 seconds later. About how far away was the strike, in miles and kilometers?
8. A city's warmest month averages 22 °C and its coldest averages −10 °C, with precipitation in all seasons. Which Köppen group fits?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. The troposphere; the mesosphere.
2. Rising air stops at the tropopause, because the stratosphere above is warmer, so the cloud spreads out sideways into an anvil.
3. Cooling ≈ $10 \times 0.8 = 8\ ^\circ\text{C}$; new temperature ≈ $18 - 8 = 10\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
4. (a) maritime tropical (mT) (b) continental polar (cP), or continental arctic (cA) if it forms over the Arctic itself.
5. Warm front: widespread, layered clouds with steady, light-to-moderate precipitation over a broad area. Cold front: a narrow band of showers and thunderstorms, then clearing and cooler air.
6. A tropical storm (39–73 mph). It becomes a hurricane at 74 mph.
7. NOAA's rule: seconds ÷ 5 = miles, seconds ÷ 3 = km. $20 \div 5 = 4$ miles; $20 \div 3 \approx 6.7$ km. (Lightning that close means you should already be indoors.)
8. Group **D** (moist continental): warmest month above 10 °C and coldest below −3 °C.

**Self-check:** For temperature-with-height problems, multiply the rate by the height change before subtracting. For storm questions, separate the hazard (wind, surge, rain, lightning) from the rating, because one scale does not cover every hazard.

</details>

> **Safety note:** NOAA's lightning guidance: if you can hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike you, and there is no safe place outside. Go into a substantial building or a hard-topped vehicle with the windows up, and stay there at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. For tornadoes, Ready.gov advises sheltering in a safe room or storm shelter, or the next best option, a small interior, windowless room or basement on the lowest level of a sturdy building, covering your head and neck. Follow official warnings and evacuation orders for hurricanes.
