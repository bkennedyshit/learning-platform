# APP-05.7 — Political Participation & Public Opinion

**Political participation** is any action people take to influence government: voting, contacting officials, signing petitions, volunteering for a campaign, joining an interest group, peacefully protesting, serving on a jury, or running for office. **Public opinion** is the distribution of people's views on political questions, usually measured with surveys. This lesson connects the two: who participates, how elections convert participation into power, and how to read the polls and data that describe it.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Some of you are close to voting age, so it includes current eligibility rules. Those rules vary by state and can change, so always confirm them with your state or local election office or [vote.gov](https://vote.gov/) before an election. This lesson does not recommend any party, candidate, or position.

## Core ideas

### Who can vote: expansion over time

The original Constitution left voter qualifications largely to the states. Amendments and federal laws then limited how states could restrict the vote:

| Change | Ratified or signed | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Fifteenth Amendment | 1870 | Vote may not be denied "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude" |
| Seventeenth Amendment | 1913 | Voters, not state legislatures, elect senators |
| Nineteenth Amendment | 1920 | Vote may not be denied "on account of sex" |
| Twenty-third Amendment | 1961 | District of Columbia receives electors for president |
| Twenty-fourth Amendment | 1964 | No poll tax in federal elections |
| Voting Rights Act | 1965 | Outlawed discriminatory practices "including literacy tests" (National Archives) |
| Twenty-sixth Amendment | 1971 | Vote may not be denied to citizens 18 or older "on account of age" |

The National Archives notes that the Voting Rights Act was signed "95 years after" the Fifteenth Amendment was ratified, because poll taxes, literacy tests, intimidation, and violence had kept many Black Americans in the South from voting.

### Eligibility today (USAGov summary)

You can vote in federal, state, and local elections if you are a U.S. citizen, meet your state's residency requirements, are 18 on or before Election Day, and are registered by your state's deadline. Important details:

- "In almost every state, you can register to vote before you turn 18 if you will be 18 by Election Day. Some states allow 17-year-olds who will be 18 by Election Day to vote in primaries."
- "North Dakota does not require voter registration."
- Non-citizens, including permanent residents, cannot vote in federal or state elections; "some areas allow non-citizens to vote in local elections only."
- Rules for people with felony convictions "are different in each state."

### Turnout: what the numbers mean

The Census Bureau's Current Population Survey (CPS) estimates registration and voting after each federal election. For the 2024 presidential election, the Bureau reported that 73.6% (174 million people) of the **citizen voting-age population** was registered and 65.3% (154 million) voted. It also reported that 63.7% of men and 66.9% of women voted, and that turnout was 82.5% for people with an advanced degree, 77.2% for bachelor's degree holders, and 52.5% for high school graduates. About 39.6% of voters voted in person on Election Day, 30.7% in person before Election Day, and 29.0% by mail.

Two cautions from the Bureau itself: these are **survey estimates**, and they "may differ from those based on administrative data or exit polls due to factors such as survey nonresponse, vote misreporting and methodological issues." Always check what group a turnout rate is measured against: the citizen voting-age population, all adults, or registered voters give different percentages.

### Political parties

A **political party** is an organization that tries to win elections in order to control government. Parties recruit and nominate candidates, help voters choose by attaching a label to candidates, mobilize turnout, and organize Congress (the majority party chooses the Speaker and committee chairs, Lesson 5.3). The United States has had two major parties for most of its history; political scientists often connect this to single-member districts where the candidate with the most votes wins. A **realignment** is a lasting shift in which groups of voters support which party. Party coalitions change over time, so describe a coalition with the year your data comes from.

### Interest groups, lobbying, and media

- An **interest group** is an organization that tries to influence policy without running its own candidates: business associations, labor unions, advocacy groups, professional associations. Groups lobby, testify at hearings, file court briefs, mobilize members, and some form political action committees.
- **Lobbying** is regulated. The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, as amended in 2007, "requires all active registrants to file quarterly activity reports" with the Clerk of the House, and the reports are public.
- **Media** shape participation by choosing what to cover (**agenda-setting**), how to describe it (**framing**), and which voices to include. Evaluating sources, including checking who produced a claim and what evidence supports it, is part of informed participation.

### Measuring public opinion: polls and the margin of error

A poll interviews a **sample**, not everyone. Pew Research Center explains the **margin of sampling error**: "A margin of error of plus or minus 3 percentage points at the 95% confidence level means that if we fielded the same survey 100 times, we would expect the result to be within 3 percentage points of the true population value 95 of those times." Two further points from Pew:

- The margin for the **difference** between two candidates "is generally about twice that of the margin for an individual candidate."
- Sampling error "is only one of many possible sources of error." Question wording, which people are reachable, and who chooses to respond can also affect results.

### Elections and the Electoral College

The general election for president "is held every four years on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November." According to the National Archives:

- "The Electoral College consists of 538 electors. A majority of 270 electoral votes is required to elect the President."
- Each state has electors equal to its members of the House plus its two senators. The District of Columbia has 3 under the Twenty-third Amendment.
- "Most States have a 'winner-take-all' system," but "Maine and Nebraska each have a variation of 'proportional representation.'" Nebraska split its electoral votes in 2008, Maine in 2016, and both in 2020.
- If no candidate wins a majority, the House chooses the president from the top three candidates. Under the Twelfth Amendment, "the votes shall be taken by states, the representation from each state having one vote."

### Campaign finance

The Federal Election Commission (FEC) enforces federal campaign finance law.

- **Contribution limits.** For 2025–2026 federal elections, an individual may give a candidate committee up to 3,500 dollars **per election**. A primary, general, runoff, and special election "are each considered a separate election with a separate limit." An individual may give up to 5,000 dollars per year to a regular PAC.
- **Independent expenditures** are communications that expressly advocate the election or defeat of "a clearly identified candidate" and are "not made in consultation or cooperation with" the candidate or party. "Coordinated" means made "in cooperation, consultation or concert with, or at the request or suggestion of" a candidate or party.
- ***Buckley v. Valeo*** **(1976)** upheld limits on contributions to candidates but struck down limits on independent expenditures and on candidates' spending of their own money.
- ***Citizens United v. FEC*** **(2010)** held 5–4 that "corporate funding of independent political broadcasts in candidate elections cannot be limited," while upholding disclosure requirements (Oyez summary).
- **Super PACs** are "independent expenditure only political committees" that "may receive unlimited contributions from individuals, corporations, labor unions and other PACs" for independent spending (FEC). They may not coordinate with candidates.

Supporters of these decisions argue they protect political speech; critics argue they increase the influence of large donors. Both views appear in the Court's majority and dissenting opinions.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading a poll (Pew's illustrative example)

Poll A shows the Republican candidate at 48% and the Democratic candidate at 43%, with a margin of error of ±3 points for each.

1. **Each candidate's range:** 48% ± 3 means the true support is plausibly between 45% and 51%.
2. **The lead:** 48 − 43 = 5 points.
3. **Margin for the lead:** about twice the individual margin, so about ±6 points. The true lead is plausibly between −1 and +11.
4. **Conclusion:** because the range includes zero, the poll cannot rule out a tie or a small deficit. Pew notes the leader "would need to be ahead by 6 percentage points or more" to be confident the lead is not sampling error.
5. **Then ask about other errors:** who was surveyed (all adults, registered voters, or likely voters), how they were contacted, and how questions were worded.

### Example 2: Checking denominators in turnout data

1. The Census Bureau reports 154 million voters and 174 million registered people in 2024.
2. **Voters as a share of registered people:** 154 ÷ 174 ≈ 88.5%. This is not the same as the 65.3% headline figure.
3. **Find the headline denominator:** 154 million ÷ 0.653 ≈ 235.8 million, and 174 million ÷ 0.736 ≈ 236.4 million. Both point to a citizen voting-age population of about 236 million (the small difference comes from rounding).
4. **Conclusion:** "88% turnout" and "65% turnout" can describe the same election. A careful writer always names the denominator.

### Example 3: Electoral vote arithmetic

1. **Illustrative state** with 12 House seats: 12 + 2 = **14 electors**.
2. **Total:** 435 representatives + 100 senators + 3 for D.C. = 538.
3. **Majority:** more than half of 538 is 270 (538 ÷ 2 = 269, plus 1).
4. **A 269–269 tie** leaves no majority, so the House chooses, voting by state delegation with one vote per state. A majority of the states (26 of 50) is needed.
5. **Why a candidate can win the Electoral College without a national popular-vote majority:** in winner-take-all states, a candidate who wins a state by one vote and a candidate who wins it by a million votes get the same number of electors.

### Example 4: Classifying campaign money

| Illustrative action (2026 federal election) | Allowed? | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| A student's parent gives a House candidate 3,500 dollars for the primary and 3,500 dollars for the general election | Yes | Primary and general are separate elections with separate limits |
| The same parent gives 5,000 dollars for the general election alone | No | Exceeds the 3,500-dollar per-election limit |
| A corporation pays for TV ads urging a candidate's defeat, made without contact with any campaign | Yes, with disclosure | Independent expenditure; *Citizens United* |
| A super PAC plans an ad with the candidate's campaign manager | Not as an independent expenditure | Coordination makes it no longer independent |

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A margin of error covers every way a poll can be wrong."** It covers sampling error only; wording, coverage, and nonresponse can add more.
- **"If a candidate leads by more than the margin of error, the lead is certain."** The margin for a lead is about twice the margin for one candidate.
- **"Turnout is an exact count."** Census turnout figures are survey estimates; official vote totals come from election administrators, and the two can differ.
- **"You must be 18 to do anything related to voting."** Most states allow preregistration before 18, and some let 17-year-olds who will be 18 by Election Day vote in primaries. Check your state.
- **"Super PACs can give unlimited money to candidates."** Super PACs can *raise* unlimited sums, but only for independent spending; they cannot coordinate with or give unlimited amounts to candidates.
- **"In the Electoral College, everyone votes for electors the same way."** Maine and Nebraska allocate some electors by congressional district.
- **"Voting is the only way to participate."** Contacting officials, testifying at public hearings, petitioning, volunteering, and peaceful assembly are also participation, and many are open to people under 18.
- **Boundary: rules change.** Contribution limits are adjusted every election cycle, and state election laws change often. The figures in this lesson are for 2025–2026 and were checked in October 2026.
