# APP-06.2 — Physical Geography — Landforms & Bodies of Water

Look at any physical map and you see patterns: long chains of mountains, wide flat plains, rivers that branch like trees, and coastlines broken into bays and straits. Physical geographers describe these features and, more importantly, explain them. Each landform is the product of processes that push land up, wear it down, or build it back with sediment, usually over millions of years. This lesson gives you the vocabulary for landforms and bodies of water and a way of reasoning about how they formed.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its thinking skill is **explaining a landform by its process**: for any feature, ask whether it was built by tectonic forces, carved by erosion, or built up by deposition, and what evidence tells you which.

## Prerequisites

- [APP-06.1 — Map Skills & Spatial Thinking](/app-061-map-skills-spatial-thinking), especially map scale and contour lines.
- Helpful: [21.2 - Plate Tectonics](/212-plate-tectonics) (plate boundaries) and [21.3 - Rocks, Minerals & the Rock Cycle](/213-rocks-minerals-the-rock-cycle) (weathering, erosion, and sediment).
- A physical world map or atlas. Before you begin, find the Himalayas, the Nile delta, the Strait of Gibraltar, and Hudson Bay.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Define and identify the **four major landforms** (mountains, hills, plateaus, and plains) and common **minor landforms** such as valleys, canyons, basins, floodplains, and deltas.
2. Distinguish **oceans, seas, gulfs, bays, straits, lakes, rivers, and estuaries**, and describe how Earth's water is distributed.
3. Explain how **tectonic, erosional, and depositional** processes create landforms, using the Himalayas, the Grand Canyon, rift valleys, and river deltas as evidence.
4. Identify landforms from **contour patterns** and use rates and ages from scientific sources to reason about how fast landscapes change.

## Core ideas

### Landforms

A **landform** is a feature of Earth's surface that is part of the terrain. National Geographic's education encyclopedia names four major types: **mountains, hills, plateaus, and plains**. Minor landforms include buttes, canyons, valleys, and basins.

| Landform | Definition | Example and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Mountain** | A large, steep-sided rise in elevation, usually in a range | The Himalayas stretch about 2,900 km along the border between India and Tibet (USGS) |
| **Hill** | A rise in elevation that is lower and more rounded than a mountain | On a topographic map, a set of closed contour loops |
| **Plateau** | A flat, elevated landform that rises sharply above the surrounding area on at least one side | Plateaus cover about a third of Earth's land; the Tibetan Plateau, about 2.5 million km², is the largest |
| **Plain** | A broad area of relatively flat land | Plains cover more than a third of Earth's land; the Great Plains of central North America are mostly grassland |
| **Valley** | A low-lying area surrounded by higher land, carved by water or ice or formed by rifting (NPS) | Glacial valleys and river valleys |
| **Canyon** | An erosional valley with extremely steep, often nearly vertical sides (NPS) | The Grand Canyon in Arizona |
| **Floodplain** | The relatively flat surface beside a river, where floodwater spreads and deposits sediment (NPS) | Natural levees and oxbow lakes form on floodplains |
| **Delta** | Land built from sediment where a river enters a slower or standing body of water | The Nile, Mississippi, and Ganges–Brahmaputra deltas |

National Geographic distinguishes two kinds of plateau. A **dissected plateau** forms when the crust is pushed upward and rivers later cut valleys into it; the Colorado Plateau has been rising about 0.03 centimeter per year for more than 10 million years. A **volcanic plateau** forms from many lava flows that build up over time.

### Bodies of water

NOAA's National Ocean Service stresses that there is **only one global ocean**, covering about 71 percent of Earth's surface. It is divided into named regions: historically four (Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, and Arctic). Most countries, including the United States, now also recognize the **Southern Ocean**, which the U.S. Board on Geographic Names defines as extending from the coast of Antarctica to 60° S. Not all countries agree on its boundaries, so the International Hydrographic Organization has not yet ratified them. The average depth of the ocean is about 3,682 meters; its deepest known point, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, is about 10,935 meters deep.

| Body of water | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sea** | Generally, a part of the ocean partly surrounded by land; by that definition there are about 50 | The Mediterranean Sea |
| **Gulf** | A part of an ocean or sea that penetrates the land; usually more enclosed than a bay | The Gulf of Mexico |
| **Bay** | A body of water partly surrounded by land, usually smaller and less enclosed than a gulf | Hudson Bay, Canada |
| **Strait** | A narrow body of water connecting two larger bodies of water | The Strait of Gibraltar, the only link between the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean |
| **River** | A large body of fresh water flowing downhill by gravity | The Colorado River |
| **Lake** | A body of water surrounded by land | The Great Lakes of the United States and Canada |
| **Estuary** | Where rivers meet the sea; brackish water, a mix of fresh and salt water | The Chesapeake Bay |
| **Watershed (drainage basin)** | All the land drained by a main river and its tributaries, separated from neighbors by a high **divide** (NPS) | Rain falling on either side of a divide flows to different rivers |

Names do not always follow definitions. National Geographic points out that the Persian Gulf is much smaller than Hudson Bay, and that the Gulf of Mexico and Hudson Bay are both counted among the world's roughly 50 seas.

**Where Earth's water is.** According to the USGS Water Science School, the oceans hold about **96.5 percent** of all Earth's water. Of the small share that is fresh water, about **68.7 percent** is locked in ice caps and glaciers and about **30.1 percent** is groundwater. Rivers, the source of most of the fresh surface water people use, hold only about one ten-thousandth of one percent of all water.

### Three kinds of landform-building processes

1. **Tectonic processes** move and deform the crust. Plate collisions push up mountains; plates pulling apart form **rift valleys** such as the Great Rift Valley in Africa and the Rio Grande Rift in North America, which began forming 30 to 35 million years ago and is still widening slowly (NPS).
2. **Erosion** removes material. Running water, wind, and glacial ice wear land down and carve valleys and canyons. Glaciers erode by **abrasion** (scraping with rock frozen into the ice) and **plucking** (lifting rock away) (NPS).
3. **Deposition** builds land from sediment. When a river slows, it drops sediment: on its floodplain during floods (building natural levees) and at its mouth, where it builds a delta.

Most landscapes record all three. The Grand Canyon, for example, required uplift (tectonic) before the Colorado River could cut down into it (erosion), and the sediment it removed was carried away and deposited elsewhere.

### Case studies

**The Himalayas: a collision.** USGS explains that the Himalayas began to form 40 to 50 million years ago, when the landmasses of India and Eurasia collided. Both are continental crust of about the same density, so neither could sink beneath the other. The crust was instead thrust upward. The collision has not ended: the Himalayas continue to rise more than 1 centimeter per year, and the stresses are released by earthquakes.

**The Grand Canyon: uplift, then erosion.** The National Park Service (NPS) describes three stages. The rocks formed first: the oldest in the canyon are about 1.8 billion years old, and even the youngest layer at the rim, the Kaibab Formation, is about 270 million years old. Between about 70 and 30 million years ago, plate-tectonic forces uplifted the region, forming the high, relatively flat Colorado Plateau. Finally, beginning only 5 to 6 million years ago, the Colorado River began cutting downward. Most of that cutting happens during floods, when the river's flow can rise from an average of 12,000 to 15,000 cubic feet per second to as much as 300,000.

**Deltas: building new land.** National Geographic explains that a delta forms when a river drops sediment (silt) where it enters a slower body of water. Deltas are classified by the process that shapes them and by their form:

- The **Nile** forms an **arcuate** (fan-shaped) delta shaped by Mediterranean waves.
- The **Mississippi** forms a **bird-foot** delta with a few long distributary channels. Over at least the past 5,000 years, the river has switched course to build seven or eight distinct delta lobes.
- The **Ganges–Brahmaputra** delta in India and Bangladesh is the world's largest. Bangladesh sits almost entirely on it.

Deltas are fertile because floods spread nutrient-rich silt. When Egypt built the Aswan Dam in the 1960s, annual flooding of the Nile delta was reduced; Egypt now relies more on fertilizers and irrigation, and the delta is shrinking.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading landforms from contours

**Problem:** On a topographic map you see four areas. Identify the landform in each.

- (a) Closed contour loops, one inside another, with elevations increasing toward the center.
- (b) Contours far apart over a wide area, with almost no change in elevation.
- (c) A large area with widely spaced contours at high elevation, ending on one side in many tightly packed contours.
- (d) Contours that form a series of V shapes pointing in the same direction, with a blue stream line running through the points of the Vs.

1. **(a)** Closed loops rising to a center mean a high point: a **hill** (or a mountain peak, if the rise is large and steep).
2. **(b)** Widely spaced contours mean gentle slopes: a **plain**.
3. **(c)** High and flat on top, with a steep edge where the contours bunch together: a **plateau**.
4. **(d)** Contours bending into Vs along a stream mark a **valley**; the Vs point upstream, toward higher ground.
5. **Check:** in every case, the spacing of the contours tells you slope, and their shape tells you form.

### Example 2: Using a rate, and testing it

**Problem:** USGS reports that the Himalayas are rising more than 1 centimeter per year. If that rate continued for one million years, how much higher would they be? Why are they not that high?

1. **Convert units:** 1 cm/year × 1,000,000 years = 1,000,000 cm.
2. **Convert to kilometers:** 1,000,000 cm ÷ 100,000 cm/km = **10 km**. USGS gives the same figure: "a growth rate of 10 km in a million years."
3. **Compare with reality:** if the range had risen 10 km every million years since the collision began 40 to 50 million years ago, it would be hundreds of kilometers high (50 × 10 = 500 km). No mountain on Earth is remotely that tall, so something must be offsetting the uplift, or the rate has not been constant.
4. **Explain:** erosion removes rock as fast as uplift raises it in many places, and USGS notes that scientists believe the Eurasian Plate may now be stretching rather than thrusting up, causing some subsidence due to gravity.
5. **Lesson:** a present-day rate cannot simply be multiplied over long periods. Landforms reflect a balance between processes that build and processes that destroy.

### Example 3: Old rocks, young canyon

**Problem:** A classmate says, "The Grand Canyon is 1.8 billion years old because that is the age of its oldest rocks." Use the NPS figures to evaluate the claim.

1. **List the dates:** oldest rocks about 1.8 billion years; youngest rim rock (Kaibab) about 270 million years; uplift about 70 to 30 million years ago; river cutting began 5 to 6 million years ago.
2. **Compare:** 1,800 million ÷ 6 million = **300**. The oldest rocks are at least 300 times older than the canyon. Even the rim rock is about 270 ÷ 6 = **45** times older.
3. **Identify the error:** the age of the rocks is the age of the *material*. The age of the *landform* is when the canyon was carved. A canyon cannot be older than the river cutting it.
4. **Conclusion:** the canyon is about 5 to 6 million years old; it cuts through rocks that are far older.

### Example 4: Calculating the share of fresh water

**Problem:** USGS estimates that Earth has about 1,386 million km³ of water. Ice caps and glaciers hold about 24.06 million km³, which is 68.7 percent of all fresh water. What share of Earth's water is fresh? What share is in rivers (about 2,120 km³)?

1. **Find total fresh water:** if 24.06 million km³ is 68.7 percent of fresh water, total fresh water ≈ 24.06 ÷ 0.687 ≈ **35.0 million km³**.
2. **Find its share of all water:** 35.0 ÷ 1,386 ≈ 0.025, or about **2.5 percent**.
3. **Rivers:** 2,120 ÷ 1,386,000,000 ≈ 0.0000015, or about **0.00015 percent**. USGS rounds this to about 0.0002 percent, or one ten-thousandth of one percent.
4. **Interpret:** almost all water is salty; most fresh water is frozen or underground; the rivers people see are a tiny, constantly renewed share.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"There are five separate oceans."** There is one connected global ocean with five named regions. The boundaries are conventions, and the Southern Ocean's boundaries are not yet agreed internationally.
- **"A landform is as old as its rocks."** The Grand Canyon's rocks are hundreds of millions to billions of years old; the canyon is 5 to 6 million years old.
- **"Mountains are permanent, or they only grow."** The Himalayas are rising, but erosion and stretching offset uplift. Landforms change continually, mostly too slowly to notice in a lifetime.
- **"Rivers hold most of the world's fresh water."** About 68.7 percent of fresh water is ice and about 30.1 percent is groundwater; rivers hold a tiny fraction.
- **"A gulf is always bigger than a bay."** Usually, but not always: the Persian Gulf is much smaller than Hudson Bay. Place names are historical, not scientific.
- **"All deltas are triangles."** The word comes from the Greek letter Δ, but the Mississippi's delta is bird-foot shaped, and other deltas take other forms.
- **"Erosion only destroys."** Eroded sediment is deposited elsewhere, building floodplains and deltas that support farming and cities.
- **Boundary: dams change deltas.** Trapping sediment behind a dam, as at Aswan, can reduce flooding but starve the delta of the silt that built it.

2. Match each description to a body of water: (a) a narrow channel connecting two seas; (b) a mix of river water and seawater; (c) all land drained by one river system; (d) part of the ocean partly surrounded by land.
3. For each landform, name the main process (tectonic, erosional, or depositional) and give your evidence: (a) the Himalayas; (b) the Mississippi bird-foot delta; (c) a glacial valley; (d) the Rio Grande Rift.
4. On a map with a 20-meter contour interval, a hill's highest closed loop is labeled 340 m. The surrounding plain is at 200 m. How many contour lines would you cross walking from the plain to the top? (Count the 340-m line but not the 200-m line.)
5. National Geographic reports that the Colorado Plateau has been rising about 0.03 cm per year for more than 10 million years. Calculate the total uplift over 10 million years, in kilometers. Give one reason the plateau's surface today is not simply that much higher than when uplift began.

> **Note on sources and viewpoints:** This lesson draws on U.S. federal science agencies (USGS, NOAA's National Ocean Service, and the National Park Service) and National Geographic's education encyclopedia. Geologic ages and rates are scientific estimates with uncertainty and are revised as research continues; for example, sources give the start of Grand Canyon carving as "5 to 6 million years ago," not an exact date. Definitions such as "sea" and the boundaries of ocean regions are conventions, and countries do not all agree on them. Place names on maps often come from history and politics, so the name of a water body is not always a reliable guide to its type. Names can also differ between governments: this lesson uses "Gulf of Mexico," the name in its sources, while U.S. Executive Order 14172 (January 2025) directed U.S. federal agencies to use "Gulf of America" for the U.S. continental shelf area of the gulf.
