# 21.8 - Human Impact & Environmental Science

Humans are now a force that changes Earth's systems: we move carbon from rocks to the air, add nutrients to rivers, pump water out of aquifers, and reshape the land. Environmental science applies everything in this course (spheres, cycles, energy balance, oceans, and the atmosphere) to measure those changes and evaluate responses. This lesson focuses on evidence: what the measurements show, how to calculate with them, and how to tell evidence apart from opinion.

This lesson is educational. It explains what scientific agencies have measured and what international agreements state; it does not tell you which policies to support.

## Core ideas

### The greenhouse effect

Earth's surface absorbs sunlight and gives off heat as infrared radiation. Certain gases in the atmosphere absorb some of that heat and send part of it back toward the surface, which slows the loss of heat to space. NASA calls this greenhouse effect "essential to life on Earth" and names five key greenhouse gases: **carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide, methane, chlorofluorocarbons, and water vapor**. Adding more of these gases traps more heat, which shifts the energy balance described in lesson 21.1 toward warming.

### Where the extra carbon comes from

- NASA identifies **burning fossil fuels** (coal, oil, and natural gas) as the primary source of the increased carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Fossil fuels hold carbon that took millions of years to accumulate in the slow carbon cycle; burning them moves it into the fast cycle within a few centuries.
- **Clearing land**, especially forests, also releases stored carbon and removes plants that would take carbon dioxide out of the air.
- **Methane** comes from producing and transporting coal, natural gas, and oil, from livestock and other agriculture, and from decaying waste in landfills (EPA).
- The ocean absorbs part of the extra carbon dioxide. NOAA reports that this has already lowered surface-ocean pH by about 0.1 units, roughly a 30% increase in acidity.

### Lines of evidence

- **Direct carbon dioxide measurements.** Continuous measurements at Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii began in 1958; NOAA credits the early data to C. David Keeling, and the record is often called the Keeling Curve. The graph rises every year and also wiggles seasonally: Northern Hemisphere plants take up carbon dioxide in spring and summer and release it as they decay in fall and winter.
- **Ice cores.** Air bubbles trapped in ancient ice show that during the ice-age cycles of roughly the past million years, carbon dioxide stayed at or below about 300 parts per million (ppm). Before the Industrial Revolution (mid-1700s) it was 280 ppm or less.
- **Temperature.** NOAA reports that Earth's average surface temperature has risen about 0.06 °C (0.11 °F) per decade since 1850, and about 0.20 °C (0.36 °F) per decade since 1982. NOAA's analysis found 2024 to be the warmest year since global records began in 1850.
- **Sea level.** NOAA reports that global sea level rose about 1.4 mm per year through most of the 20th century and about 3.6 mm per year from 2006 to 2015, and that in 2023 it was 101.4 mm above its 1993 level. Rising water comes from melting land ice and from seawater expanding as it warms.
- **Projections depend on choices.** NOAA's projections for U.S. sea level in 2100 range from about 0.6 m above 2000 levels if emissions are significantly reduced to about 2.2 m on a pathway with high emissions and rapid ice-sheet collapse.

### Pollution in the spheres

- **Ground-level ozone (smog).** High in the stratosphere, ozone shields life from ultraviolet light. Near the ground, EPA describes ozone as a harmful pollutant and the main ingredient of smog. It is not emitted directly; it forms when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds from vehicles, power plants, and industry react in **sunlight**, so it is worst on hot, sunny days.
- **Acid rain.** EPA explains that sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, mostly from burning fossil fuels, react in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids that fall as wet or dry deposition. Normal rain has a pH of about **5.6** (slightly acidic from dissolved carbon dioxide); acid rain usually has a pH of **4.2–4.4**. It can harm soils, forests, streams, and lakes.
- **Nutrient pollution and eutrophication.** EPA describes excess **nitrogen and phosphorus** (from sources such as agriculture and stormwater) causing algae to grow faster than ecosystems can handle. NOAA explains the chain: algae bloom and block sunlight, plants die, bacteria decompose the dead material and use up oxygen, and fish that cannot swim away become unhealthy or die. Low-oxygen areas are called "dead zones."
- **Metals in soil and water.** EPA notes that metals such as arsenic, cadmium, chromium, copper, lead, mercury, nickel, selenium, and zinc can be toxic in water, and that human activities redistribute and concentrate metals in places that are not naturally rich in them.

### Resources

- **Renewable** energy sources, according to EIA, are constantly replenished by nature; EIA lists five major ones: solar, geothermal, wind, biomass, and hydropower. They are limited by availability (solar power only when the Sun shines, for example).
- **Nonrenewable** sources (petroleum, hydrocarbon gas liquids, natural gas, coal, and nuclear energy) are limited to what can be mined or extracted and take a very long time to form. Coal, oil, and natural gas are **fossil fuels**.
- **Groundwater** is renewable only if it is not pumped faster than it is recharged. USGS describes long-term groundwater depletion in many areas of the United States from sustained pumping.

### Responses: mitigation and adaptation

NASA defines two kinds of response:

- **Mitigation** reduces climate change by cutting the flow of heat-trapping gases into the atmosphere (for example, using less fossil fuel for electricity, heat, and transport) or by enhancing "sinks" that store carbon (oceans, forests, soil).
- **Adaptation** adjusts to actual or expected climate change to reduce risks, such as preparing for higher seas or more intense extreme weather.

### The Paris Agreement

According to the United Nations, the Paris Agreement was adopted at the COP21 conference on 12 December 2015 and entered into force on 4 November 2016. Its central goal is to hold the increase in global average temperature to **well below 2 °C** above pre-industrial levels while pursuing efforts to limit it to **1.5 °C**. Countries submit national climate plans, called **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)**, on a five-year cycle, and a "global stocktake" reviews collective progress; the first concluded in 2023. Which countries participate can change over time, so check the UN climate secretariat (UNFCCC) for current status.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: How fast is carbon dioxide rising?

NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory lists these Mauna Loa annual means: 1960: 316.91 ppm; 1970: 325.68 ppm; 2015: 401.01 ppm; 2025: 427.35 ppm. Compare the average rates of increase.

1. 1960s rate (slope): $\dfrac{325.68 - 316.91}{1970 - 1960} = \dfrac{8.77}{10} \approx 0.88$ ppm per year.
2. 2015–2025 rate: $\dfrac{427.35 - 401.01}{10} = \dfrac{26.34}{10} \approx 2.63$ ppm per year.
3. Compare: $2.63 \div 0.88 \approx 3$, so carbon dioxide is rising about **three times faster** per year than in the 1960s.
4. Interpretation: a straight line would have the same slope everywhere; this record curves upward, which means the rise is accelerating.

### Example 2: Compared with pre-industrial air

How much higher is the 2025 Mauna Loa annual mean than a pre-industrial level of 280 ppm?

1. Difference: $427.35 - 280 = 147.35$ ppm.
2. Percent increase: $\dfrac{147.35}{280} \times 100\% \approx 53\%$.
3. Check against NOAA's statement that carbon dioxide is now about 50% higher than before the Industrial Revolution. NOAA's statement used the 2024 global average (422.8 ppm), and $\dfrac{422.8 - 280}{280} \approx 51\%$, so the two agree once you note the different year and station. ✓
4. Context: ice cores show values no higher than about 300 ppm for roughly the past million years.

### Example 3: Volcanoes or people?

NASA reports that volcanoes emit about 130–380 million metric tons of carbon dioxide per year. NOAA cites an estimate of 37.4 billion tons of carbon dioxide from fossil fuels in 2024. Compare them.

1. Convert to the same unit: 37.4 billion = 37,400 million tons.
2. Ratio using the high volcanic estimate: $37{,}400 \div 380 \approx 98$.
3. Ratio using the low estimate: $37{,}400 \div 130 \approx 288$.
4. Conclusion: fossil-fuel emissions are roughly **100 to 290 times** larger than volcanic emissions, consistent with NASA's "100–300 times" comparison made with earlier emission figures.

### Example 4: How much more acidic is acid rain?

pH is a logarithmic scale: each step of 1 pH unit means a **10-fold** change in hydrogen-ion concentration. Compare acid rain at pH 4.3 with normal rain at pH 5.6.

1. Difference: $5.6 - 4.3 = 1.3$ pH units.
2. Factor: $10^{1.3} \approx 20$.
3. Conclusion: rain at pH 4.3 has about **20 times** the hydrogen-ion concentration of normal rain.
4. Common error: saying it is "1.3 times" as acidic ignores that the pH scale is logarithmic.

### Example 5: Sea level over a decade

At 1.4 mm/yr (most of the 20th century) and 3.6 mm/yr (2006–2015), how much would sea level rise in 10 years?

1. Slower rate: $1.4 \times 10 = 14$ mm.
2. Faster rate: $3.6 \times 10 = 36$ mm.
3. Ratio: $3.6 \div 1.4 \approx 2.6$, matching NOAA's description of the rate having "more than doubled."
4. Boundary: these are global averages. NOAA notes that the local rate along many U.S. coastlines is greater than the global average because of land processes such as subsidence, erosion, and oil and groundwater pumping.

### Example 6: Mitigation or adaptation?

1. A city replaces coal-fired power with solar and wind → **mitigation** (cuts emissions).
2. A coastal town raises a seawall and updates flood maps → **adaptation** (reduces risk from rising seas).
3. A region replants forests on cleared land → **mitigation** (enhances a carbon sink).
4. Farmers switch to crop varieties that tolerate heat and drought → **adaptation**.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The greenhouse effect is bad."** The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth habitable. The concern is the *added* warming from extra greenhouse gases.
- **"Volcanoes release more carbon dioxide than people."** Measured volcanic emissions are roughly 100–300 times smaller than human emissions.
- **"A cold winter disproves global warming."** One season is weather; climate trends are measured over decades of global data (lesson 21.5).
- **"Ozone is always harmful" or "ozone is always protective."** EPA describes stratospheric ozone as "good" because it shields living things from ultraviolet radiation, and ground-level ozone as "bad" because it is a smog pollutant that harms health. Where the ozone is matters.
- **"Correlation alone proves the cause."** A graph of carbon dioxide and temperature rising together is not proof by itself. The scientific case rests on a known physical mechanism (gases that absorb infrared radiation), on measurements of where the carbon comes from, and on several independent lines of evidence. See [17.2 - Exploring Data (2-Variable)](/172-exploring-data-2-variable) for why correlation is not causation.
- **"Renewable means unlimited and always available."** EIA describes renewable sources as constantly replenished but limited by availability; solar panels produce no power at night, and hydropower depends on river flow.
- **Boundary:** some sources used here are archived. NOAA's Climate.gov pages were archived in June 2025; their figures describe data through 2024, and newer values may differ.

## Practice

2. Mauna Loa's annual mean was 315.98 ppm in 1959 and 427.35 ppm in 2025. Find the average rate of increase per year over that span.
3. Why does the Mauna Loa curve rise and fall a little within each year?
4. Lake A has pH 6.0 and Lake B has pH 4.0. How many times more acidic (in hydrogen-ion concentration) is Lake B?
5. Explain, step by step, how fertilizer runoff can lead to a fish kill.
6. Classify as renewable or nonrenewable: coal, geothermal, natural gas, wind, uranium for nuclear power, biomass.
7. Classify as mitigation or adaptation: (a) planting shade trees and opening cooling centers for heat waves, (b) capturing methane from a landfill.
8. State the two temperature goals of the Paris Agreement and what an NDC is.
