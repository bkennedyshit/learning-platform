# APP-06.6 — Political Geography

Every map of the world is covered with lines that do not exist on the ground. You cannot see the border between the United States and Canada from space, yet it decides which laws apply, which language is on the road signs, and who can live where. Political geography studies how people divide the Earth's surface into territories, why boundaries are where they are, and how states cooperate and compete.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its thinking skill is **asking who drew the line, when, and why**. It is a nonpartisan lesson: it explains concepts and evidence about states, borders, and alliances and does not take sides in present-day disputes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Define **state, nation, nation-state, multinational state, and stateless nation**, and apply the four **Montevideo Convention** criteria for statehood.
2. Classify political **boundaries** as physical, geometric, or cultural, and explain how land and **maritime** boundaries are defined, maintained, and disputed, including the 12-nautical-mile territorial sea and 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone.
3. Explain **centripetal and centrifugal forces** and the **colonial legacy** visible in present-day borders.
4. Compare **international organizations** such as the UN, NATO, and the EU by membership, purpose, and how much sovereignty members share, and explain why counts of the world's countries differ.

## Core ideas

### States, nations, and nation-states

These words are used loosely in everyday speech, but geographers distinguish them carefully. The definitions below follow the open textbook *Introduction to Human Geography* (Dorrell and Henderson).

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **State** | A country: a territory whose government has **sovereignty**, control of its own affairs within its boundaries. (Not the same as the 50 U.S. states) | Brazil, Kenya, Japan |
| **Nation** | A group of people with a shared cultural and historical identity | A people who share a language and history |
| **Nation-state** | A state whose territory largely matches one nation | Dorrell and Henderson give Japan, Finland, and Egypt as examples |
| **Multinational state** | A state containing more than one nation; more common than nation-states, and growing more common with international migration | Many states today |
| **Stateless nation** | A nation without a state of its own | The Kurds, who live in Iraq, Turkey, Syria, and Iran |

**What makes a state?** The 1933 Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States, Article 1, lists four qualifications: "(a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) government; and (d) capacity to enter into relations with the other states." Article 2 adds that a federal state "shall constitute a sole person in the eyes of international law," however its internal powers are divided.

**Unitary and federal states.** In a **unitary** state, the national government holds most power and local governments act on its behalf. In a **federal** state, such as the United States, power is constitutionally divided between the national government and regional governments.

**How many states are there?** It depends on whom you ask. The United Nations has **193 member states**; the most recent, South Sudan, joined in 2011. The UN also has non-member observer states. Dorrell and Henderson give a figure of 196. Counts differ because some territories are recognized as states by some governments but not others, and because "country" can mean different things in different contexts. The number also changes over time through conquest, independence, or **devolution**, the transfer of power from a central government to regions.

### Forces that hold states together or pull them apart

Dorrell and Henderson describe two kinds of forces:

- **Centripetal forces** bind a state together: for example, nationalism, economic prosperity, and strong, ethical security forces.
- **Centrifugal forces** pull a state apart: for example, wars, ineffective or corrupt governments, and market failure.

Shared institutions, transport networks, and national symbols usually act centripetally. Deep regional inequality or a group that feels excluded can act centrifugally. The same factor, such as religion or language, can be centripetal in one state and centrifugal in another.

### Boundaries

A boundary is really an invisible vertical plane: it divides the airspace above and the ground below as well as the surface. Dorrell and Henderson describe three common types:

| Type | Basis | Examples (Dorrell and Henderson unless noted) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Natural features such as rivers, lakes, and mountains | The Rio Grande (U.S.–Mexico); the Pyrenees (Spain–France); the Andes (Chile–Argentina) |
| **Geometric** | Straight lines, often along latitude or longitude | The U.S.–Canada boundary along 49° N; Indonesia–Papua New Guinea along the 141st meridian |
| **Cultural (ethnic)** | Drawn to follow a cultural trait such as religion or language | The India–Pakistan boundary, drawn between a predominantly Hindu and a predominantly Muslim state |

**Physical boundaries move.** Rivers shift course gradually or suddenly, so a river boundary needs rules and maintenance. The U.S.–Mexico **International Boundary and Water Commission**, under treaties between the two countries, manages issues such as the distribution of the waters of the Rio Grande and the Colorado River.

**Boundaries are made in stages.** The U.S.–Canada boundary shows how. According to the International Boundary Commission, the 1783 Treaty of Paris first described a boundary between the United States and British North America. The **Convention of 1818** extended it along the **49th parallel** from the Lake of the Woods to the Rocky Mountains, and the **Oregon Treaty of 1846** extended the 49th parallel to the Strait of Georgia. A permanent commission, created by a 1925 treaty, maintains the line today. The border is **8,891 km** (5,525 miles) long.

**Maritime boundaries.** The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) sets two key distances, both measured from a state's coastal **baselines**:

- **Territorial sea:** up to **12 nautical miles** (Article 3), where the coastal state is sovereign.
- **Exclusive economic zone (EEZ):** up to **200 nautical miles** (Article 57), where the coastal state has rights over resources such as fish, oil, and gas.

Where neighbors' zones overlap, they must negotiate a boundary or seek a settlement.

### The colonial legacy

Many of today's borders were drawn by outside powers. OpenStax's *World History, Volume 2* reports that Europeans controlled about **10 percent** of Africa at the start of the Second Industrial Revolution and about **90 percent** by the end of the nineteenth century. At the **Berlin Conference** of 1884–1885, "without input from Africans," European nations allotted parts of the continent to one another. Dorrell and Henderson note that almost **50 percent** of Africa's boundaries are geometric and that at least **177 ethnic groups** are split between two or more states. Most of these colonial boundaries became the borders of independent states.

### International organizations and sovereignty

Dorrell and Henderson call alliances of states **supranational organizations**, because members give up some sovereign power for shared benefits. They differ in how much.

| Organization | Members (official source) | Purpose | Sovereignty shared |
|---|---|---|---|
| **United Nations** (1945) | 193 member states | International peace and security, cooperation, and human rights (UN Charter, Article 1) | Limited: the Charter is based on "the sovereign equality of all its Members" (Article 2) |
| **NATO** (founded April 4, 1949, by 12 countries) | 32 member countries; 20 joined in 10 rounds of enlargement, most recently in 2023 and 2024 | Collective defense and security | Decisions are made by consensus among all members |
| **European Union** | 27 member countries | Economic and political union, including a single market; many members share the euro | Extensive: members pool authority over trade and many laws |

Regional organizations elsewhere, such as the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) and the African Union, also coordinate trade, security, and policy among neighboring states.

### Names on the map

Place names, or **toponyms**, are political choices too. For example, U.S. Executive Order 14172 (January 20, 2025) directed U.S. federal agencies to use "Gulf of America" for the U.S. continental-shelf area of the Gulf of Mexico and "Mount McKinley" for the peak named Denali in 2015, while the surrounding park keeps the name Denali National Park and Preserve. Other governments and organizations may use different names for the same place. A careful map reader notes whose naming convention a map follows.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Applying the Montevideo criteria

**Problem:** A hypothetical territory, "Islandia," has 80,000 permanent residents, an elected government that controls its whole island, clearly mapped coasts, and embassies in no other country because no state has agreed to deal with it. Does it meet the Montevideo criteria?

1. **Permanent population:** yes, 80,000 residents.
2. **Defined territory:** yes, its island's coasts are mapped.
3. **Government:** yes, it controls the island.
4. **Capacity to enter into relations with other states:** it has the capacity in principle, but in practice no state deals with it.
5. **Conclusion:** Islandia meets the first three criteria clearly. Whether it is a state in practice depends heavily on **recognition** by other states, which the Montevideo list does not settle. This is why counts of countries differ.

### Example 2: Classifying and explaining boundaries

**Problem:** Classify each boundary and give one advantage and one problem of that type: (a) the Pyrenees between Spain and France; (b) the 49th parallel between the United States and Canada; (c) the Rio Grande between the United States and Mexico.

1. **(a) Physical (mountain):** advantage, it is easy to identify and few people live along high ridges; problem, ridgelines and watersheds can be hard to locate exactly.
2. **(b) Geometric:** advantage, it is easy to define on a map; problem, a straight line ignores landforms and communities, and it must be surveyed and marked on the ground.
3. **(c) Physical (river):** advantage, it is visible; problem, rivers shift, so the boundary needs treaties and a commission to manage changes and water sharing.
4. **General point:** every boundary type is a human decision about where to draw a line, even when the line follows a natural feature.

### Example 3: Converting maritime zones

**Problem:** A coastal state claims the full zones allowed by UNCLOS. How far from its baseline do its territorial sea and EEZ extend, in kilometers? Two states face each other across a strait 300 km wide. Can both claim full EEZs?

1. **Territorial sea:** 12 × 1.852 = **22.2 km**.
2. **EEZ:** 200 × 1.852 = **370.4 km**.
3. **Overlap test:** two full EEZs would need 2 × 370.4 = 740.8 km. The strait is only 300 km wide, so the claims **overlap**.
4. **Resolution:** UNCLOS Article 74 says that the EEZ boundary between states "with opposite or adjacent coasts shall be effected by agreement on the basis of international law" to reach "an equitable solution." The states must negotiate, or seek a settlement through other procedures. UNCLOS sets maximum distances, not automatic lines between neighbors.

### Example 4: Reading a colonial boundary

**Problem:** A student sees a map of Africa with many straight-line borders and says, "Straight borders mean those countries were empty land." Evaluate the claim.

1. **Evidence:** about half of Africa's boundaries are geometric (Dorrell and Henderson); the Berlin Conference divided the continent "without input from Africans" (OpenStax).
2. **Reasoning:** straight lines usually show that boundaries were drawn on maps by distant negotiators, often along lines of latitude, longitude, or between surveyed points, not that no one lived there.
3. **Further evidence:** at least 177 ethnic groups are divided by these boundaries, which shows the lines crossed populated regions.
4. **Conclusion:** the claim confuses the *shape* of a boundary with the *population* of the land. Straight borders are evidence about who drew the line and how, not about who lived there.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"State," "nation," and "country" mean the same thing.** A state is a political territory; a nation is a people. Many nations have no state, and most states contain several nations.
- **"Nation-states are purely one nation."** Even the usual examples, such as Japan, have minority communities. "Nation-state" describes a close match, not perfect uniformity.
- **"There is an official count of the world's countries."** The UN has 193 member states, but other counts include territories recognized by some governments and not others.
- **"Natural boundaries are permanent."** Rivers shift course, and mountain ridgelines can be hard to pin down; both require treaties and maintenance.
- **"A country owns the ocean out to 200 nautical miles."** Full sovereignty extends only 12 nautical miles. The EEZ gives rights over resources, not full sovereignty, and overlapping claims must be negotiated.
- **"Joining an international organization means giving up all independence."** Organizations differ: the UN is based on sovereign equality, NATO decides by consensus, and the EU pools authority in many areas.
- **Boundary: names and borders can be disputed.** Some borders and place names are contested between governments. A map shows one party's convention, so check who made it.
