# APP-06.3 — Climate & Biomes

Why is the Sahara a desert while the Amazon basin, at a similar distance from the equator, is a rainforest? Why can you find snow on mountains in the tropics? Why do California, the Mediterranean coast, and southern Australia, half a world apart, have such similar plants? The answers come from climate: the long-term pattern of temperature and precipitation in a place. Climate, in turn, largely determines which **biome**, or large community of plants and animals, can live there.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its thinking skill is **classifying with explicit criteria**: you will use the same thresholds scientists use to sort climates, and then check whether the result matches the vegetation actually found there.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Distinguish **weather** from **climate** and explain how climate normals are calculated.
2. Explain how **latitude, elevation, nearness to water, ocean currents, prevailing winds and pressure belts, and mountains** shape a place's climate.
3. **Classify** a place's climate with the Köppen–Geiger criteria from its monthly temperature and precipitation data.
4. Describe the **eight major terrestrial biomes** and connect each to its climate, and summarize documented effects of **climate change** on where species and biomes occur.

## Core ideas

### Weather and climate

NASA explains the difference this way: **weather** refers to local conditions over short periods, "from minutes to hours, to days to weeks," such as rain, snow, clouds, and wind. **Climate** refers to longer-term averages and "can be thought of as the weather averaged over several decades."

In the United States, NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) publishes official **climate normals**: 30-year averages of temperature and precipitation for about 15,000 stations. Normals are recalculated every 10 years, following World Meteorological Organization requirements. The current set covers **1991–2020**. NCEI reports that most of the U.S. was warmer, and the eastern two-thirds wetter, in 1991–2020 than in the previous normals period (1981–2010).

### What controls climate

| Factor | How it works | Evidence or example |
|---|---|---|
| **Latitude** | Near the equator the sun is high year-round; toward the poles sunlight arrives at a lower angle and seasons become extreme | Tropical forests stay between about 20 °C and 34 °C; the Arctic tundra averages about −34 °C in winter (OpenStax) |
| **Elevation** | Air cools as it rises. NOAA's National Weather Service gives the **dry adiabatic lapse rate**: rising unsaturated air cools about **9.8 °C per kilometer**. Saturated air cools more slowly, at a rate that varies | In the troposphere, average temperature falls from around 17 °C near the surface to about −51 °C at its top (NOAA) |
| **Pressure belts and prevailing winds** | Three circulation cells in each hemisphere create bands of **high pressure near 30° N and S** and at the poles, and **low pressure near the equator and 50°–60° N and S**. High pressure brings fair, dry weather; low pressure brings rain and storms | NOAA points to the many deserts along 30° N and S, and the wet regions between 50° and 60° (such as the British Isles and Alaska); westerly winds blow between about 35° and 60° |
| **Nearness to water** | Water warms and cools more slowly than land, so coasts have milder seasons than continental interiors | Compare a coastal city's monthly temperatures with an inland city at the same latitude |
| **Ocean currents** | Currents move heat. NOAA calls the Gulf Stream "the main conveyor of heat from south to north in the Atlantic"; it flows north along Florida and then northeast across the ocean at an average of about 6.4 km/h | Warm currents tend to warm and moisten nearby coasts; cold currents tend to cool them |
| **Mountains (rain shadow)** | Moist air forced up a mountain cools, forms clouds, and drops rain or snow on the **windward** side. Descending air on the **leeward** side warms and dries | National Geographic: Death Valley lies in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada |

### The Köppen–Geiger climate classification

A widely used system for sorting climates was devised by Wladimir Köppen and later revised with Rudolf Geiger. Beck and colleagues, who published new global maps of it in the journal *Scientific Data* (2018), describe it as five main classes and 30 sub-types, based on thresholds of monthly temperature and precipitation. They explain that it aims to map biomes empirically, treating vegetation as "crystallized, visible climate."

The five main classes and their criteria (from Beck et al., Table 2):

| Class | Name | Rule (check B first) |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | Arid | Mean annual precipitation (MAP) < 10 × P-threshold. **BW** (desert) if MAP < 5 × P-threshold, otherwise **BS** (steppe). Add **h** (hot) if mean annual temperature (MAT) ≥ 18 °C, or **k** (cold) if below |
| **A** | Tropical | Not B, and the coldest month ≥ 18 °C. **Af** (rainforest) if the driest month has ≥ 60 mm |
| **C** | Temperate | Not B; warmest month > 10 °C; coldest month between 0 °C and 18 °C |
| **D** | Cold | Not B; warmest month > 10 °C; coldest month ≤ 0 °C |
| **E** | Polar | Not B; warmest month ≤ 10 °C. **ET** (tundra) if the warmest month > 0 °C, **EF** (frost) otherwise |

**P-threshold** (in mm) depends on when rain falls: 2 × MAT if more than 70 percent of precipitation falls in winter; 2 × MAT + 28 if more than 70 percent falls in summer; otherwise 2 × MAT + 14. The logic: warmer places lose more water to evaporation, so they need more rain to avoid being dry.

For C and D climates, a second letter describes the dry season: **s** (dry summer: the driest summer month has less than 40 mm and less than one-third of the wettest winter month), **w** (dry winter: the driest winter month has less than one-tenth of the wettest summer month), or **f** (no dry season). A third letter describes summer heat: **a** if the warmest month ≥ 22 °C, **b** if not and at least four months average above 10 °C.

### The eight major terrestrial biomes

A **biome** is a large region with a characteristic community of plants and animals. The free textbook *Biology 2e* from OpenStax (Rice University) identifies eight major terrestrial biomes, "each distinguished by characteristic temperatures and amount of precipitation." The same biome can appear on different continents with similar climates.

| Biome | Climate (OpenStax) | Typical vegetation | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tropical wet forest** (rainforest) | Stable temperatures of 20–34 °C; 125–660 cm of rain a year | Broad-leaved evergreen trees in layers; 100–300 tree species in one hectare of Amazon forest | Near the equator |
| **Savanna** | Hot (about 24–29 °C average) with an extensive dry season | Grasses with scattered trees; plants resprout after fire | Africa, South America, northern Australia |
| **Subtropical desert** | Less than 30 cm a year, sometimes as little as 2 cm; very hot days and cold nights | Annual plants, deep roots, water-storing stems, tiny or no leaves | Between 15° and 30° N and S |
| **Chaparral** | 65–75 cm a year, mostly in winter; very dry summers | Shrubs adapted to periodic fire | California, the Mediterranean, southern Australia |
| **Temperate grassland** | 25–75 cm a year; hot summers, cold winters | Dense grasses; few trees except along rivers; natural fires | Prairies of North America; steppes of Eurasia |
| **Temperate forest** | −30 to 30 °C; 75–150 cm spread through the year | Deciduous trees that drop their leaves in fall | Eastern North America, Western Europe, East Asia, Chile, New Zealand |
| **Boreal forest** (taiga) | Cold, dry winters; short, cool, wet summers; 40–100 cm, mostly as snow | Evergreen conifers such as pine, spruce, and fir | Canada, Alaska, Russia, northern Europe |
| **Arctic tundra** | Winter average about −34 °C; summer 3–12 °C; growing season about 10–12 weeks | Low plants and lichens; permafrost below | North of the boreal forest |

### Climate change and biomes

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) states that global surface temperature in 2011–2020 was about **1.1 °C** above 1850–1900, and that human activities, principally greenhouse-gas emissions, have "unequivocally caused" this warming. Its Working Group II report finds that "approximately half of the species assessed globally have shifted polewards or, on land, also to higher elevations" (very high confidence). Beck and colleagues also mapped projected Köppen–Geiger classes for 2071–2100 under a high-emissions scenario, showing that climate zones themselves are expected to shift. Note the language: scientists attach confidence levels to findings and describe projections as scenarios, not certainties.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Classifying a humid temperate climate

**Data (a hypothetical station):** MAT 15 °C; MAP 1,200 mm, spread fairly evenly through the year; coldest month 4 °C; warmest month 26 °C; driest summer month 80 mm; driest winter month 70 mm; wettest summer month 130 mm.

1. **Check B first.** Rain is not concentrated in one season, so P-threshold = 2 × 15 + 14 = **44 mm**. Arid if MAP < 10 × 44 = 440 mm. MAP is 1,200 mm, so **not B**.
2. **Main class.** Warmest month 26 °C > 10 °C, and the coldest month 4 °C is between 0 °C and 18 °C: **C (temperate)**.
3. **Dry season.** Dry summer (s) needs the driest summer month < 40 mm; it is 80 mm, so not s. Dry winter (w) needs the driest winter month < 130 ÷ 10 = 13 mm; it is 70 mm, so not w. Therefore **f**.
4. **Summer heat.** Warmest month 26 °C ≥ 22 °C: **a**.
5. **Result: Cfa** (humid subtropical). **Check against vegetation:** OpenStax places temperate forests where precipitation is 75–150 cm spread through the year; 1,200 mm (120 cm) fits.

### Example 2: Classifying a hot desert

**Data (a hypothetical station):** MAT 24 °C; MAP 180 mm, with more than 70 percent falling in summer.

1. **P-threshold:** summer-concentrated rain, so 2 × 24 + 28 = **76 mm**.
2. **Arid test:** 10 × 76 = 760 mm. MAP 180 < 760, so the climate is **B**.
3. **Desert or steppe:** 5 × 76 = 380 mm. 180 < 380, so **BW** (desert).
4. **Hot or cold:** MAT 24 °C ≥ 18 °C, so **h**.
5. **Result: BWh** (hot desert). **Check:** OpenStax's subtropical deserts have less than 30 cm (300 mm) a year; 180 mm fits. NOAA's high-pressure belt near 30° explains why such deserts are common there.

### Example 3: A rain shadow, step by step

**Problem:** Air at 20 °C at sea level is pushed up the windward side of a 3-km mountain range. It cools at the dry rate (9.8 °C per km) for the first 1 km, where it becomes saturated and clouds form. For this example, **assume** saturated air cools about 6 °C per km (the real moist rate varies). After the summit, the air descends 3 km on the leeward side, warming at the dry rate because it has lost its moisture as rain and snow. What is its temperature at the leeward base?

1. **First 1 km (dry):** 20 − 9.8 = **10.2 °C**.
2. **Next 2 km (saturated):** 2 × 6 = 12 °C of cooling; 10.2 − 12 = **−1.8 °C** at the summit. Clouds drop precipitation on the windward slope.
3. **Descent of 3 km (dry):** 3 × 9.8 = 29.4 °C of warming; −1.8 + 29.4 = **27.6 °C**.
4. **Interpret:** the air reaches the leeward base about 7.6 °C warmer than it started and much drier. This is the rain-shadow effect National Geographic describes: wet and cool on one side, warm and dry on the other.

### Example 4: Matching a climate to a biome

**Problem:** A region has a coldest-month average below 0 °C, a warmest month of about 16 °C, short wet summers, and 60 cm of precipitation, mostly as snow. Predict its biome and explain.

1. **Climate class:** warmest month > 10 °C and coldest ≤ 0 °C, so a **D (cold)** climate.
2. **Compare with the biome table:** 40–100 cm, mostly snow, cold winters and short cool summers: **boreal forest (taiga)**.
3. **Explain the vegetation:** OpenStax notes that evergreen conifers can photosynthesize earlier in spring and keep their nitrogen-rich needles, an advantage in short seasons and poor soils.
4. **Check the alternative:** tundra is ruled out because its warmest month is too cool for trees (3–12 °C summers and permafrost).

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A cold week disproves a warming climate."** A week is weather. Climate is a multi-decade average, which is why NCEI uses 30-year normals.
- **"Deserts are always hot."** Deserts are defined by dryness. OpenStax notes that cold deserts can drop below −30 °C, and Köppen's BWk class is a cold desert.
- **"Places at the same latitude have the same climate."** Elevation, ocean currents, distance from the sea, and mountains can make places at the same latitude very different.
- **"The tropics are rainy everywhere."** Savannas have long dry seasons, and subtropical deserts lie between 15° and 30°, under high pressure.
- **"Biome boundaries are sharp lines."** Biomes grade into each other, and soils, fire, and human land use also shape vegetation. A climate class predicts *likely* vegetation, not exact boundaries.
- **"The Köppen system is a law of nature."** It is a human classification with chosen thresholds. Beck and colleagues note that maps of it can be wrong where weather stations are sparse or mountains create sharp gradients.
- **Boundary: the moist lapse rate is not a constant.** The National Weather Service gives a fixed dry rate (9.8 °C per km) but says the saturated rate depends on temperature and pressure. Example 3 uses an assumed value.
