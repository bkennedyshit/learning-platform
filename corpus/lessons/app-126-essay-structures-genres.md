# APP-12.6 — Essay Structures & Genres

A **genre** is a kind of writing that readers recognize by its purpose and conventions: an argument tries to persuade, an analysis explains how something works or what it means, an expository essay informs, a comparison shows a relationship between two or more things, and a literature review maps what researchers have said about a question. **Structure** is the order in which you present your ideas. Choosing the right structure for the genre and the audience is a big part of what makes an essay easy to follow.

The five-paragraph essay is a useful starting pattern, but Purdue OWL calls it "by no means the only formula." This lesson shows several older and more flexible structures and when to use each. It is written for high school students moving into longer research essays.

[APP-12.2 — Thesis & Argument Construction](/app-122-thesis-argument-construction) (claims, Toulmin, rebuttals) and [APP-12.5 — Citation & Documentation](/app-125-citation-documentation) (citing several sources in one paragraph). ## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Identify the **genre** a prompt calls for from its verbs and purpose.
2. Outline an argument with the **classical arrangement**, and explain when a **Rogerian** structure fits better.
3. Organize a comparison **subject by subject** or **point by point**.
4. Write a **synthesis** paragraph for a literature review.

## Core ideas

### Reading the prompt for genre

| Prompt language | Likely genre | The thesis does this |
|---|---|---|
| *argue, take a position, persuade, should* | argumentative | makes a debatable claim and justifies it with evidence |
| *analyze, examine, how does, interpret* | analytical | breaks a text or issue into parts and evaluates how they work |
| *explain, describe, inform, report* | expository | states what the essay will explain and how |
| *compare, contrast, similarities, differences* | comparative | makes a claim about the relationship between the subjects |
| *review the research, what do scholars say* | literature review | states what the research shows as a whole and where it disagrees or has gaps |

Purdue OWL's thesis guide describes the first three: an analytical paper "breaks down an issue or an idea into its component parts, evaluates the issue or idea, and presents this breakdown and evaluation"; an expository paper "explains something"; an argumentative paper "makes a claim about a topic and justifies this claim with specific evidence." Many real assignments mix genres: an argument often includes analysis, and an analysis usually has an arguable thesis about meaning.

### Audience and purpose shape structure

NCTE notes that different purposes and genres create different relationships between writers and readers, reflected in "degrees of formality in language, as well as assumptions about what knowledge and experience are already shared, and what needs to be explained." A school board, a science teacher, and a general newspaper reader need different amounts of background and different evidence. Ask: *What does this reader already know? What will they doubt?*

### The classical arrangement

Ancient Greek and Roman teachers of rhetoric taught a standard order for arguments. Purdue OWL summarizes its parts:

| Part | What it does | In a modern essay |
|---|---|---|
| **Exordium** | the introduction or hook; gains interest and sets the tone | opening paragraph |
| **Narratio** | the context or background of the topic | background paragraph or the middle of the introduction |
| **Propositio and partitio** | the claim and an outline of the argument | thesis and preview |
| **Confirmatio** | positive proofs supporting the claim | body paragraphs with evidence |
| **Refutatio** | addresses opposing views | counterargument and rebuttal paragraph(s) |
| **Peroratio** | the conclusion and call to action | conclusion |

Traditionally the refutation follows the confirmation, but writers often move it earlier when readers already hold the opposing view, so that the objection is cleared away before the proof; when readers are undecided, ending the body with your strongest proof can work better.

### Rogerian argument

Purdue OWL describes **Rogerian argument**, adapted for writing by Young, Becker, and Pike from the psychotherapist Carl Rogers, as aiming "to establish a middle ground between parties with opposing viewpoints or goals." A Rogerian essay typically introduces the problem, states the opposing position fairly, shows when that position is valid, presents the writer's position and when it is valid, and explains how the other side would benefit. The OWL notes that the form is less common in academic writing and may not work when an opponent argues in bad faith or is "demonstrably wrong." Use it when your audience is resistant and the goal is a workable agreement.

### Comparative structures

The UNC Writing Center describes two main ways to organize a comparison:

- **Subject by subject:** say everything about subject A, then everything about subject B. This suits short papers and **lens** comparisons, where one subject is used to understand another.
- **Point by point:** discuss one point of comparison at a time, covering all subjects within each point. This keeps the comparison visible throughout and suits longer papers.

Either way, the thesis must say what the comparison *shows*, not just that similarities and differences exist.

### The literature review

Purdue OWL defines a literature review as a document or section that "collects key sources on a topic and discusses those sources in conversation with each other (also called synthesis)." It usually has an introduction (topic, focus, and what will be covered), a body that summarizes, synthesizes, analyzes, and evaluates sources, and a conclusion that states the key findings and connects them to your research question. Common organizations are **chronological**, **thematic**, **methodological** (grouping studies by research approach), and **theoretical**.

A quick self-test from the OWL: look at your in-text citations. If each paragraph cites only one source, you are probably summarizing, not synthesizing.

### Introductions and conclusions

Whatever the genre, the introduction orients the reader (topic, context, and thesis) and the conclusion does more than repeat: it explains what the argument adds up to, why it matters, or what should happen next. Avoid opening with a dictionary definition or a sweeping claim about "all of human history"; open with something specific to your question.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Outlining a classical argument

**Thesis (from Lesson 12.2):** *Ninth-grade English classes should teach lateral reading as part of the research unit.*

**Audience:** the school's English department, who control the curriculum and worry about time.

1. **Exordium:** a short, specific scenario: a student cites a polished website in a research paper without realizing who funds it. (Label it as a hypothetical scenario.)
2. **Narratio:** what students currently learn about evaluating sources (for example, a checklist like CRAAP) and why online information is hard to judge.
3. **Propositio and partitio:** the thesis plus a preview: lateral reading addresses a skill gap, fits inside the existing unit, and supports the department's research goals.
4. **Confirmatio:** (a) Wineburg and McGrew found that historians and students were misled by logos and domain names while fact checkers who read laterally reached better conclusions faster; (b) the four SIFT moves can be practiced on sources students already use; (c) lateral reading supports the research paper's evaluation requirements.
5. **Refutatio:** *Objection:* the unit is already full. *Response:* SIFT practice can replace part of the existing checklist lesson rather than add days. *Objection:* one study does not prove a classroom lesson works. *Response:* concede the point, and propose a pilot with one section to gather evidence.
6. **Peroratio:** call to action: pilot the lesson next semester and compare students' source choices with the previous year's.

Reasoning: the department's main worry (time) is answered directly, and the second rebuttal uses a **concession**, which keeps the claim within what the evidence supports.

### Example 2: Reorganizing a comparison

**Thesis:** *CRAAP and SIFT both ask who is behind information, but CRAAP mainly examines a source's own features, while SIFT begins by leaving the source to see what others say, which makes SIFT faster for unfamiliar websites and CRAAP more thorough for a source you have already decided to use.*

**Subject-by-subject outline (draft):**

1. Intro and thesis
2. CRAAP: the five criteria, its checklist format, its questions about authority and purpose
3. SIFT: the four moves, lateral reading
4. Conclusion

Problem: paragraph 3 has to remind the reader of paragraph 2 to make any comparison, and the thesis's real claim (when each method is better) appears only in the conclusion.

**Point-by-point revision:**

1. Intro and thesis
2. **Who is behind it:** CRAAP's Authority questions vs. SIFT's "Investigate the source"
3. **Where you look:** CRAAP's questions mostly about the page itself (including whether the URL reveals anything) vs. SIFT's lateral reading; note that CRAAP's Accuracy criterion also asks whether you can verify information in another source
4. **When each works best:** speed with unfamiliar sites vs. depth with chosen sources
5. Conclusion: use SIFT to decide whether a source deserves your time, then CRAAP to judge it closely

Reasoning: each body paragraph now makes a comparison, and paragraph 3 includes a fair complication so the comparison does not oversimplify CRAAP.

### Example 3: From summary to synthesis

The three sources below are **invented for this practice**, not real studies.

- **Ortiz (2021):** surveyed 300 high school students; most said they check whether a website "looks professional."
- **Bell and Kim (2022):** observed 40 students evaluating sites; students rarely left the page to investigate it.
- **Nakamura (2023):** after a four-week lateral-reading unit in two schools, students more often identified who funded a site.

**Summary-only paragraph (weak):**

> Ortiz (2021) surveyed 300 students about how they judge websites. Bell and Kim (2022) observed 40 students evaluating sites. Nakamura (2023) studied a lateral-reading unit.

**Synthesis paragraph (stronger):**

> Research on students' source evaluation points to a consistent gap between what students attend to and what reveals credibility. Students report judging sites by whether they look professional (Ortiz, 2021), and observation confirms that they rarely leave a page to investigate it (Bell & Kim, 2022). Together these findings suggest that the problem is not carelessness but a habit of reading vertically. Evidence that the habit can change is more limited: Nakamura (2023) found that students who completed a lateral-reading unit more often identified a site's funder, but the study included only two schools. The research therefore supports teaching lateral reading while leaving open how well the effect lasts or transfers.

Reasoning: the synthesis opens with a claim about the research as a whole, groups sources by what they show together, explains the connection in the writer's own words, evaluates a limitation, and ends with what remains unknown. Three citations appear in one paragraph, which the OWL identifies as a sign of synthesis.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Every essay has five paragraphs."** Five paragraphs fits a short argument with three reasons. Longer and more complex topics need more sections.
- **"The counterargument always goes at the end, right before the conclusion."** That is one common placement, not a rule. Place the refutation where your audience needs it, earlier if they already hold the opposing view.
- **"A comparison essay just lists similarities and differences."** The thesis must say what the comparison reveals.
- **"A literature review is a list of summaries."** It synthesizes: it groups sources, compares their findings, and evaluates them.
- **"Rogerian argument means having no position."** You still hold and argue for a position; you present it in a way that shows understanding of the other side and seeks common ground.
- **"Expository essays have no thesis."** They still need a controlling idea that tells the reader what will be explained and how.
- **Boundary: discipline conventions.** In the sciences, literature reviews tend to report study designs and results, while in the humanities they include more overt argument and interpretation (Purdue OWL). Check examples from your field (Lesson 12.7).
