# APP-01.6 — Reading Comprehension — Informational Text

**Informational text** (nonfiction) explains facts about the real world: how butterflies grow, why storms form, how a bill becomes a law. Science books, news articles, encyclopedias, and instructions are informational texts. They are read differently from stories. Instead of following a character through a plot, you follow the author's **organization**: how the facts are arranged and how they connect.

Readers who can spot that organization understand and remember more, because they know what kind of information to expect next. This lesson teaches five common structures, how to find a main idea, how to summarize, and how to use the features that nonfiction books add to help you.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 4-5. It is written for the student.

## Prerequisites

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Name the **structure** of an informational paragraph and explain how its ideas are organized.
2. State the **main idea** of a paragraph in one sentence and list its supporting details.
3. Write a **summary** that keeps the main ideas and drops minor details, and explain how it differs from a **retelling**.
4. Use **text features** to find information and explain the **author's purpose**.

## Core ideas

### Five text structures

| Structure | What the author does | Common clue words |
|---|---|---|
| Description | Tells what something is like or what it is made of | *for example*, *such as*, *looks like*, *is made of* |
| Sequence (chronological) | Tells events or steps in time order | *first*, *next*, *then*, *after*, *finally*, dates |
| Compare and contrast | Shows how two things are alike and different | *both*, *alike*, *same as*, *but*, *however*, *unlike* |
| Cause and effect | Shows how one event leads to another | *because*, *so*, *therefore*, *as a result* |
| Problem and solution | Describes a problem and how it was or could be solved | *problem*, *solved*, *so that*, *one answer is* |

Clue words help, but they are not proof. Some paragraphs use no clue words, and *so* or *but* can appear in any structure. Decide by asking: **How are the ideas connected?**

### Main idea and supporting details

The **topic** is what a paragraph is about in a word or two (*monarch butterflies*). The **main idea** is the most important point the author makes about the topic, in one sentence. **Supporting details** are facts, numbers, and examples that prove or explain the main idea. Sometimes the main idea is stated in the first sentence. Sometimes you have to put it together yourself from the details.

### Summarizing versus retelling

- A **retelling** repeats the information in order, with most of the details.
- A **summary** is shorter. It keeps only the main ideas, in your own words, and leaves out examples and minor numbers.

One way to build a summary is to write a one-sentence **gist** for each section: *who or what is this about*, plus *the most important thing the author wants you to know about it*. Then put the gist sentences together.

### Author's purpose

Many classrooms use **PIE**: an author writes to **P**ersuade (convince you), **I**nform (teach you facts), or **E**ntertain (tell a story or make you laugh). Writers also **describe** and **explain**. A single text can have more than one purpose. A funny science article can inform and entertain at the same time.

### Text features

Nonfiction often includes **headings** (tell what a section is about), **bold** or *italic* words (key vocabulary, often in a **glossary**), **captions** (explain a picture), **diagrams**, **tables** and **graphs** (show numbers), a **table of contents** (at the front), and an **index** (at the back, listing pages for each topic). Previewing these features before reading tells you what the text is about and how it is organized.

## Worked examples

The two monarch paragraphs below were written for this lesson using facts from the U.S. Forest Service page "Monarch Butterfly Biology" (see Sources).

> **Four Stages of Life**
>
> A monarch butterfly goes through four stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult. First, a female lays her eggs; during her life she lays from 100 to 300. About four days later, each egg hatches into a larva, or caterpillar. A new caterpillar is less than 1 centimeter long, but it grows to about 5 centimeters. Next, the caterpillar becomes a pupa, also called a chrysalis. In as few as five days, the adult butterfly comes out. Going through all four stages is called **complete metamorphosis**.
>
> **Two Kinds of Monarchs**
>
> Summer monarchs and the last monarchs of the year live very different lives. Summer monarchs live only 2 to 5 weeks. During that time they mate and lay the eggs that become the next generation. The last generation of the year does not lay eggs right away. Instead, these butterflies fly 2,000 to 3,000 miles to Mexico, where they spend the winter. Some of them live as long as 9 months.

### Example 1: Finding the structure

1. Paragraph 1, "Four Stages of Life": the clue words are *First*, *About four days later*, *Next*, and *In as few as five days*. The events go in time order from egg to adult. **Structure: sequence.**
2. Paragraph 2, "Two Kinds of Monarchs": the first sentence says the two groups "live very different lives." The paragraph then gives one fact about summer monarchs and a matching fact about the last generation (how long they live; what they do). The word *Instead* signals a difference. **Structure: compare and contrast.**
3. Check against the "How are the ideas connected?" question. Paragraph 1 connects ideas by **time**. Paragraph 2 connects ideas by **difference**.

### Example 2: Main idea and details

Paragraph 1:

1. Topic: the monarch's life cycle.
2. Main idea: *A monarch goes through four stages, egg, larva, pupa, and adult, which together are called complete metamorphosis.*
3. Supporting details: 100 to 300 eggs; hatching after about four days; the caterpillar grows from under 1 cm to about 5 cm; the pupa becomes an adult in as few as five days.

Reasoning: the numbers are interesting, but they do not *state* the big point. They support it. The main idea is the sentence you would keep if you could keep only one.

### Example 3: Retelling versus summary

**Retelling** (too long for a summary): *A monarch has four stages. A female lays 100 to 300 eggs. They hatch in about four days into caterpillars less than 1 cm long that grow to about 5 cm. Then the caterpillar becomes a pupa, and in as few as five days an adult comes out. Summer monarchs live 2 to 5 weeks and lay eggs, but the last generation flies 2,000 to 3,000 miles to Mexico and can live 9 months.*

**Summary:**

1. Gist of section 1: *Monarchs grow through four stages, from egg to adult.*
2. Gist of section 2: *Most monarchs live only a few weeks, but the last generation of the year migrates to Mexico and lives much longer.*
3. Combined summary: *Monarch butterflies grow through four stages, from egg to adult. Most live only a few weeks, but the last generation of the year migrates to Mexico for the winter and lives much longer.*

Reasoning: the summary is about half as long, uses new wording, and drops the exact numbers, which are details.

### Example 4: Using a text feature

*Illustrative table built from the same source.*

| Monarch group | How long it lives | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Summer generation | 2 to 5 weeks | mates and lays eggs |
| Last generation of the year | up to about 9 months | migrates to Mexico for the winter |

Question: *About how many times longer can a migrating monarch live than a summer monarch that lives 5 weeks?*

1. Find the row values: summer, up to 5 weeks; migrating, up to about 9 months.
2. Convert to the same unit. 9 months is about 39 weeks (using about 4.3 weeks per month: 9 × 4.3 ≈ 39).
3. Compare: 39 ÷ 5 ≈ 8.
4. Answer: roughly **8 times** longer. The table made the comparison faster than rereading the paragraph.

Author's purpose for the passage: mainly to **inform**. It gives facts, with no attempt to convince you of an opinion.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The main idea is always the first sentence."** Often it is, but sometimes it comes last, or is never stated and must be built from the details.
- **"A summary is just a shorter retelling."** A summary leaves out details on purpose and uses your own words. Copying sentences and deleting a few is not summarizing.
- **"If I see the word *because*, it must be cause and effect."** A sequence paragraph might use *because* once. Look at how the whole paragraph is organized, not one word.
- **"The most interesting detail is the main idea."** "Some live as long as 9 months" is exciting, but it supports the bigger point about the two kinds of monarchs.
- **"Informational texts have only one purpose."** A text can inform and persuade at once, for example an article with facts about pollution that ends by asking readers to recycle.
- **"Text features are decoration I can skip."** Headings, captions, and tables often contain information that is not in the paragraphs.

## Practice

Passages 1-4 are illustrative passages written for practice.

1. Name the structure: *Our school had a problem. The lunch line was so long that some students had only ten minutes to eat. The student council suggested opening a second line, and now everyone has time to finish lunch.*
2. Name the structure: *Because the road was covered in ice, the buses could not drive safely. As a result, school started two hours late.*
3. Name the structure: *To plant a seed, first fill a cup with soil. Next, push the seed in about as deep as your fingertip. Then water it. Finally, set the cup in a sunny window.*
4. Name the structure: *Both of the town's libraries are open on Saturdays and lend books for free. However, the Main Street library has a computer lab, while the Park Road library has a garden where you can read outside.*
5. Write the main idea of passage 1 in one sentence.
6. Which sentence is a summary of passage 3, and which is a retelling? (a) *The passage gives the steps for planting a seed in a cup.* (b) *Fill a cup with soil, push in a seed as deep as your fingertip, water it, and set it in a window.*
7. What is the author's purpose in passage 3? What about a poster that says, *Join the garden club, the best club in school!*?
8. You want to find the page about *migration* in a nonfiction book about butterflies. Which text feature do you use, and where is it in the book?

<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. Problem and solution: the problem is the long line; the solution is a second line.
2. Cause and effect: the icy road (cause) led to the late start (effect). Clue words *because* and *as a result*.
3. Sequence: steps in order, with *first*, *next*, *then*, *finally*.
4. Compare and contrast: *both* shows a similarity and *however* introduces differences.
5. Example: *The student council solved the long lunch line problem by opening a second line.* Your sentence should name the problem and the solution.
6. (a) is a summary: short, in new words, with no details. (b) is a retelling: it repeats each step.
7. Passage 3: to inform (it teaches how to do something). The poster: to persuade (it wants you to join, and "the best club" is an opinion).
8. The index, at the back of the book. It lists topics in alphabetical order with page numbers. (The table of contents at the front lists chapters, which might also help.)

**Self-check for summaries:** Count the words. A good summary of a paragraph is usually one or two sentences. Then underline any phrase copied word for word from the original; if more than a few words are underlined, rewrite it in your own words.

> **Adult note:** Before reading a nonfiction book together, spend a minute previewing it: read the headings aloud, look at the pictures and captions, and ask, "What do you think we will learn?" After each section, ask the learner to say its gist in one sentence.
