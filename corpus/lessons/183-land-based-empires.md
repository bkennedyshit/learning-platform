# 18.3 - Land-Based Empires

Between about 1450 and 1750, a handful of very large land empires ruled most of the people living between the Balkans and the Pacific. The Ottomans captured Constantinople and governed southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa. The Safavids made Iran a Shia Muslim state. The Mughals ruled most of northern India. The Manchus founded the Qing dynasty and ruled China. Each of these empires governed millions of people who spoke different languages and practiced different religions, and each had to answer the same question: how do a small ruling group and its ruler hold a huge, diverse territory together?

This lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its historical-thinking skill is **comparison**: identifying meaningful similarities and differences between cases and explaining why they exist.

## Prerequisites

- [18.2 - Networks of Exchange](/182-networks-of-exchange), especially the Mongol khanates and the Ming dynasty's tribute system.
- The difference between **Sunni** and **Shia** Islam at a basic level: the two main branches of Islam, which divided early over who should lead the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad.
- Helpful but optional: [APP-12.2 — Thesis & Argument Construction](/app-122-thesis-argument-construction), which practices writing a claim you can defend.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Describe how the **Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Qing** empires were founded, citing key dates such as 1453, 1501, 1526, and 1644.
2. Compare how these empires governed **diverse populations**, using specific policies such as the millet system, the devshirme, Akbar's abolition of the jizya, and the Qing queue order.
3. Evaluate the **"gunpowder empires"** thesis by weighing the role of firearms against other explanations for imperial power.
4. **Source** a foreign observer's account of an empire, identifying the author's purpose and how it shapes the evidence.

## Core ideas

### Four empires at a glance

| Empire | Founding moment | Ruling group and religion | High point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ottoman** | Ottoman armies took **Constantinople** in **1453** and made it their capital (Istanbul) | Turkish Sunni Muslim dynasty ruling Muslims, Christians, and Jews | Under **Suleiman I** in the sixteenth century, the empire threatened Vienna, reached the Persian Gulf, and controlled Mecca and Medina |
| **Safavid** | In July **1501**, Ismail entered Tabriz and declared himself **shah** of all Iran | Made **Shia** Islam the state religion "at a time when Iran's population was mostly Sunni" | **Abbas I** reformed the army and civil service and built the capital **Isfahan** |
| **Mughal** | In **1526**, **Babur**, a descendant of Timur, used artillery to defeat the much larger army of the Delhi Sultanate | Muslim dynasty ruling a mostly Hindu population | **Akbar** expanded the empire; **Shah Jahan** built the **Taj Mahal** (begun 1631) |
| **Qing** | Rebels seized Beijing in April **1644**; the **Manchus**, from northeast of the Great Wall, then took control of China | Manchu minority ruling a Han Chinese majority | Emperors such as **Kangxi** adopted Chinese bureaucracy and toured the country |

Russia also expanded in this period, eastward across Siberia, under rulers who took the title *tsar*. This lesson focuses on the four empires above.

### Governing diversity: four strategies

Every empire mixed **accommodation** (letting groups keep their customs) with **control** (forcing obedience and extracting taxes and soldiers).

**Ottoman: the millet system and the devshirme.** Christians, Jews, and other non-Muslims were *dhimmis*, protected non-Muslim subjects; in the Balkans they were the large majority. Under the **millet system**, each religious community "had their own courts to settle their affairs": Orthodox Christians under the Orthodox patriarch in Istanbul, Armenian Christians, and Jews formed the first three millets. At the same time, under the **devshirme** ("gathering"), Ottoman officials took Christian boys, usually aged eight to ten, from villages in the Balkans and Caucasus. OpenStax describes this as "the enslavement of youth for state and military service." The boys converted to Islam and trained for the sultan's service; most became **Janissaries**, an elite corps of soldiers paid directly by the sultan, and a small number with "exceptional intellect" went to the palace school to become administrators.

**Safavid: religious unity.** Rather than accommodating religious difference, the early Safavids made Twelver Shia Islam the religion of the state. Court historians gave Ismail's family a descent from the Prophet's family, a genealogy OpenStax calls "most likely invented," and Ismail recruited the Turkish tribal warriors called **Qizilbash**. This religious identity divided Safavid Iran from the Sunni Ottomans, and the two empires fought repeatedly; the Ottomans laid waste to Tabriz in 1514.

**Mughal: incorporation, then reversal.** **Akbar** let local rulers keep their lands if they submitted, married daughters of both Muslim and Hindu rulers, and brought Hindus into his administration. In **1568** he abolished the **jizya**, a tax on non-Muslims; he allowed new Hindu temples to be built; and he built a hall for religious debates that came to include scholars of many faiths, including Portuguese Jesuits. A century later, **Aurangzeb** (who took the throne in 1658) ended the debates, at times ordered the destruction of temples of Hindus who resisted him, and eventually imposed a tax on all non-Muslims again.

**Qing: adopt the system, mark the subjects.** The Manchus kept China's existing bureaucracy and appointed Manchu and Han Chinese co-administrators to lead the six main ministries, while Manchus held supervisory positions. They also demanded a visible sign of loyalty: all men had to wear the Manchu hairstyle, with the front of the head shaved and the back hair braided into a **queue**. "After 1645, men who did not wear their hair in this fashion were subject to execution."

### Gunpowder and the "gunpowder empires" thesis

Gunpowder was invented in China and reached the Islamic world in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, possibly through the Mongols. The Ottomans "famously used artillery in the siege of Constantinople in 1453," Janissaries carried early firearms, and Babur used artillery at the battle of 1526.

In **1974**, historians **Marshall G. S. Hodgson** and **William H. McNeill** argued that adopting gunpowder weapons explained the rise of the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires, and they called them **"gunpowder empires."** OpenStax notes that "today that theory is largely disputed, but the name has remained." What these empires shared, it argues, was a view of "the entire state as a military force," in which the central government coordinated political, economic, and cultural resources to strengthen the army. That organization is what let them pay for and adopt new weapons quickly. Example 2 works through this argument.

### China and the tribute system

The Ming and Qing dynasties managed foreign relations through **tribute**: foreign rulers sent gifts and acknowledged the emperor's superiority, and in return received gifts and permission to trade. In 1793, near the end of this period, the Qianlong emperor rejected a British request for a permanent ambassador and expanded trade, writing to King George III, "we possess all things. I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country's manufactures." The same letter acknowledged that tea, silk, and porcelain were "absolute necessities to European nations," which is why trade was permitted at Canton.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A structured comparison of the Ottomans and Mughals

**Question:** How did the Ottoman and Mughal empires govern non-Muslim majorities or large minorities?

1. **Similarity:** both were Muslim dynasties that allowed non-Muslims a degree of self-rule. Ottoman millets ran their own courts; under Akbar, sharia applied only to Muslims and Hindus served in government.
2. **Difference 1 (policy tools):** the Ottomans used the devshirme to recruit Christian boys into the state; the Mughals under Akbar recruited Hindu elites through marriage alliances and appointments.
3. **Difference 2 (change over time):** Mughal policy changed sharply between Akbar (who abolished the jizya in 1568) and Aurangzeb (who later imposed a tax on all non-Muslims).
4. **Explain the pattern:** both dynasties needed the cooperation of very large non-Muslim populations to collect taxes and keep order, which encouraged accommodation; but each ruler's religious priorities and military needs also shaped policy.
5. **Claim:** Both empires combined accommodation with control, but the Mughal approach depended more on individual emperors, while the Ottoman millet system was a lasting institution.

### Example 2: Evaluating the "gunpowder empires" thesis

**Thesis (Hodgson and McNeill, 1974):** gunpowder weapons explain the rise of the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires.

| Evidence that supports it | Evidence that complicates it |
|---|---|
| Ottoman artillery at Constantinople, 1453 | Gunpowder was available to many states, including their enemies, so weapons alone cannot explain why these states succeeded |
| Janissaries armed with firearms | The Safavids rose in 1501 largely through religious leadership and Qizilbash cavalry |
| Babur's artillery defeated a much larger army in 1526 | Long-term rule depended on taxation, bureaucracy, and alliances such as Akbar's marriages and appointments |

1. **Identify the causal claim:** weapons caused empire.
2. **Test it against the cases:** firearms mattered in key battles, but other factors explain how the empires grew and lasted.
3. **Consider the revised view:** the empires' strength came from organizing the whole state around the military, which made it possible to pay for and adopt guns quickly.
4. **Conclusion:** gunpowder was a **contributing cause** and a **tool** of these empires, not a sufficient explanation. This is why historians kept the label but dropped the strong version of the theory.

### Example 3: Sourcing a foreign ambassador's praise of the Ottomans

Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq was the Holy Roman Emperor's ambassador at the Ottoman court from 1555 to 1562. He wrote:

> "No distinction is attached to birth among the Turks; the deference to be paid to a man is measured by the position he holds in the public service. ... Those who receive the highest offices from the Sultan are for the most part the sons of shepherds or herdsmen."

1. **Author and audience:** a European diplomat writing letters for readers at home, in a Europe where noble birth largely decided rank.
2. **Purpose:** the Fordham Sourcebook's introduction explains that Busbecq "was trying to bring about reform at home," so "he did not dwell on the very real problems with Ottoman government." He praised Ottoman merit to criticize his own society.
3. **Corroborate and complicate:** the devshirme did let some boys of humble birth rise to high office, which supports Busbecq. But the system took those boys from their families by force and enslaved them, a cost he does not discuss.
4. **Conclusion:** Busbecq is useful evidence that Ottoman officials could rise by service rather than birth. His purpose means we should not treat his letters as a balanced description of Ottoman society.

### Example 4: What a hairstyle can tell a historian

**Evidence:** after 1645, Qing law required all Chinese men to shave the front of the head and wear a braided queue, on pain of execution.

1. **What it shows:** the Manchu rulers demanded a visible, daily sign of submission from every man.
2. **Why a minority would do this:** the Manchus were far outnumbered by Han Chinese. A universal rule made loyalty easy to check and refusal easy to punish.
3. **Compare to other policies:** at the same time, the Qing kept the Chinese bureaucracy, shared ministries with Han officials, and embraced Chinese culture.
4. **Claim:** Qing rule combined **accommodation** in government with **coercion** in symbols, a pattern similar to other empires in this lesson.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Islamic empires were all the same."** The Ottomans and Mughals were Sunni dynasties; the Safavids made Shia Islam the state religion, and they fought the Ottomans repeatedly.
- **"Toleration meant equality."** Ottoman dhimmis and Mughal Hindus could worship and, in many matters, keep their own laws, but they faced limits: Mughal non-Muslims paid the jizya before 1568 and again under Aurangzeb, and the Ottoman devshirme enslaved Christian boys.
- **"Akbar's policies describe the whole Mughal period."** Aurangzeb reversed several of them a century later. Empires change over time; always give dates.
- **"Gunpowder created these empires."** Historians now largely dispute that strong claim. Firearms mattered, but so did taxation, bureaucracy, religion, and alliances.
- **"The Qing were 'Chinese' rulers like the Ming."** The Qing were Manchus, a minority from northeast of the Great Wall, who adopted Chinese institutions while keeping a separate Manchu identity.
- **"The Qianlong letter proves China was ignorant or closed."** The emperor wrote from within a tribute system that had organized China's foreign relations for centuries, and the same letter allowed trade at Canton. It shows a policy choice and a worldview, not a lack of knowledge.
- **Boundary: the 1793 letter is from after 1750.** It is used here because it states the tribute system's logic clearly; remember that it comes from the end of the period.
