# APP-04.5 — Forces & Motion — Newtons Laws

In [APP-04.1](/app-041-forces-motion-foundations) you learned that balanced forces leave motion unchanged and unbalanced forces change it. Isaac Newton turned those observations into three laws that let us **predict** motion with numbers. This lesson states the three laws, introduces free-body diagrams and the equation $F_{\text{net}} = ma$, separates mass from weight, and previews projectile motion.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 7-8.

- [APP-04.1 — Forces & Motion — Foundations](/app-041-forces-motion-foundations): forces as arrows, balanced and unbalanced forces, friction, gravity.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. State Newton's three laws and match each to an example.
2. Draw a free-body diagram, find the net force, and use $F_{\text{net}} = ma$.
3. Calculate weight from mass and explain how mass and weight differ.
4. Explain why action-reaction forces do not cancel, and why a projectile's horizontal and vertical motions are independent.

## Core ideas

### Describing motion with numbers

- **Speed** = distance ÷ time, in meters per second (m/s).
- **Velocity** is speed **with a direction**, such as 3 m/s east.
- **Acceleration** is how fast velocity changes: $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$, in meters per second per second (m/s²). Speeding up, slowing down, and turning are all accelerations, because each changes the velocity.

### Newton's first law (inertia)

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, **unless a net external force acts on it**. The tendency to keep doing what it is doing is called **inertia**. More mass means more inertia: a loaded shopping cart is harder to start and harder to stop than an empty one.

### Free-body diagrams and net force

A **free-body diagram** shows one object as a dot or box, with an arrow for every force acting **on** it. The **net force** is the sum of all forces, with direction included. Along one line, choose one direction as positive (for example, right = +) and the other as negative.

Common forces:

- **Weight** ($w$): the pull of gravity, straight down.
- **Normal force** ($N$): a surface pushing back, perpendicular to the surface.
- **Friction** ($f$): acts along a surface, opposing sliding (or attempted sliding).
- **Applied force** and **tension** (the pull of a rope or string).

### Newton's second law

The acceleration of an object is in the direction of the net force, is larger when the net force is larger, and is smaller when the mass is larger:

$$F_{\text{net}} = ma$$

Force is measured in **newtons** (N). One newton is the net force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s², so $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2$.

### Mass and weight

**Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter in an object and measures its inertia. It is the same everywhere. **Weight** (N) is the gravitational force on it, and it depends on where you are. Near Earth's surface:

$$w = mg, \quad g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$$

An astronaut has the same mass on the Moon as on Earth, but a smaller weight, because the Moon's gravity is weaker.

### Newton's third law

When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts a force on A that is **equal in strength and opposite in direction**. Forces always come in pairs. The two forces in a pair act on **different objects**, so they never cancel each other. A swimmer pushes backward on the wall; the wall pushes forward on the swimmer, and the swimmer accelerates away.

### A first look at projectile motion

A **projectile** is an object moving only under gravity after it is launched, such as a thrown ball. If air resistance is small enough to ignore, its **horizontal** and **vertical** motions are independent. Horizontally, no force acts, so its horizontal velocity stays constant (first law). Vertically, gravity accelerates it downward at about 9.8 m/s² (second law). The two combine into a curved path.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Net force and acceleration of a sled

*Illustrative example.* A 20 kg sled is pulled to the right with 60 N. Friction on the snow is 20 N to the left. Find the acceleration.

1. Free-body diagram, horizontal: 60 N right, 20 N left. (Vertically, weight and normal force balance, so there is no vertical acceleration.)
2. Take right as positive: $F_{\text{net}} = +60\ \text{N} - 20\ \text{N} = +40\ \text{N}$.
3. Solve $F_{\text{net}} = ma$ for $a$: $a = \dfrac{40\ \text{N}}{20\ \text{kg}} = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$ to the right.
4. Units check: $\text{N/kg} = \dfrac{\text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s}^2}{\text{kg}} = \text{m/s}^2$.
5. Reasonableness: if the pull dropped to 20 N, the net force would be 0 and the sled would move at a constant velocity, not stop instantly (first law).

### Example 2: Weight of a student

A student has a mass of 50 kg. What is their weight on Earth?

1. $w = mg = 50\ \text{kg} \times 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
2. $w = 490\ \text{N}$, directed downward.
3. Check: a 1 kg bag of rice weighs about 9.8 N, so 50 kg should weigh 50 times as much: $50 \times 9.8 = 490$. ✓
4. Boundary: a bathroom scale labeled in kilograms is really measuring force and converting to kilograms using Earth's $g$. On the Moon it would read the wrong mass.

### Example 3: A book at rest and the force pairs

A 2 kg book rests on a table.

1. Forces **on the book**: weight $w = 2 \times 9.8 = 19.6\ \text{N}$ down, and normal force from the table up.
2. The book is not accelerating, so $F_{\text{net}} = 0$ and $N = 19.6\ \text{N}$ up. These two forces are balanced, but they are **not** a third-law pair: both act on the book.
3. Third-law pairs: (a) Earth pulls the book down 19.6 N; the book pulls Earth up 19.6 N. (b) The table pushes up on the book; the book pushes down on the table.
4. Lesson: "balanced" (same object) and "action-reaction" (different objects) are different ideas.

### Example 4: Rearranging $F = ma$

*Illustrative example.* A cart accelerates at 3 m/s² when the net force on it is 12 N. What is its mass, and what net force would give it 5 m/s²?

1. $m = \dfrac{F_{\text{net}}}{a} = \dfrac{12\ \text{N}}{3\ \text{m/s}^2} = 4\ \text{kg}$.
2. New force: $F_{\text{net}} = ma = 4\ \text{kg} \times 5\ \text{m/s}^2 = 20\ \text{N}$.
3. Check the proportion: acceleration grew by a factor of $5/3$, so force should too: $12 \times 5/3 = 20$. ✓

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A force is needed to keep something moving."** A force is needed to **change** motion. Things slow down on Earth because friction and air resistance are forces.
- **"Action and reaction cancel, so nothing can move."** They act on different objects. Only forces on the **same** object add together to make its net force.
- **"Mass and weight are the same."** Mass is in kilograms and does not change from place to place. Weight is a force in newtons and depends on gravity.
- **"Heavier objects fall faster."** Ignoring air resistance, all objects near Earth's surface fall with the same acceleration, about 9.8 m/s². A heavier object has more weight but also more inertia. Air resistance is what makes a feather fall slowly.
- **"Zero velocity means zero acceleration."** A ball thrown straight up is momentarily at rest at the top, but gravity is still accelerating it downward at 9.8 m/s².
- **"Friction always works against motion."** Friction opposes **sliding** between surfaces. Static friction between your shoes and the floor pushes you forward when you walk.

## Practice

1. Which law best explains each? (a) Passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly. (b) A rocket pushes exhaust down and rises. (c) The same push accelerates an empty cart more than a full one.
2. *Illustrative example:* A 5 kg box is pushed right with 30 N while friction pushes left with 10 N. Find the net force and acceleration.
3. What is the weight on Earth of a 70 kg person? Of a 0.5 kg ball?
4. *Illustrative example:* A 1,000 kg car accelerates at 2 m/s². What net force acts on it?
5. *Illustrative example:* A net force of 45 N gives a wagon an acceleration of 1.5 m/s². What is the wagon's mass?
6. A child on skates pushes a wall with 40 N. What force does the wall exert on the child, and in which direction?
7. A ball rolls off a table at the same instant another ball is dropped from the same height. Ignoring air resistance, which lands first? Why?
8. Draw a free-body diagram for a lamp hanging still from a cord. Which forces act, and how do they compare?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. (a) first law (inertia) (b) third law (c) second law
2. $F_{\text{net}} = 30 - 10 = 20\ \text{N}$ to the right; $a = 20 \div 5 = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$ to the right.
3. $70 \times 9.8 = 686\ \text{N}$; $0.5 \times 9.8 = 4.9\ \text{N}$.
4. $F_{\text{net}} = 1000 \times 2 = 2000\ \text{N}$.
5. $m = 45 \div 1.5 = 30\ \text{kg}$.
6. 40 N, directed away from the wall (opposite to the child's push). That is why the child rolls backward.
7. They land at the same time. Their vertical motions are identical (both start with zero vertical velocity and accelerate down at 9.8 m/s²); the rolling ball's horizontal motion does not affect its fall.
8. Weight down and tension in the cord up. They are equal in size because the lamp is not accelerating.

**Self-check:** In every numeric answer, write the unit and the direction. If an acceleration comes out in N or a force in kg, the formula was rearranged incorrectly. For question 1, name the evidence (a change in motion, a pair of objects, or a comparison of masses) that points to each law.

</details>

> **Safety note:** If you test the falling-balls question, use soft balls from table height, keep the landing area clear, and do not stand on furniture. Never test inertia with a moving vehicle or bicycle stops.
