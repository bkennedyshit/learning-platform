# APP-02.3 — Multiplication & Division

Multiplication is a fast way to find the total of equal groups. Division undoes multiplication: it splits a total into equal groups. In this lesson the learner builds both with objects and arrays, learns tricks for finding facts they do not know yet, and learns what to do with a remainder.

The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-02.2 — Addition & Subtraction](/app-022-addition-subtraction): adding several numbers quickly, especially doubles and tens. From [APP-02.1 — Number Sense & Counting](/app-021-number-sense-counting): skip counting by 2s, 5s, and 10s.

> **Adult note:** Materials: about 40 counters, a few small cups or paper plates for "groups," square grid paper for arrays, and optionally an egg carton (2 rows of 6 is a ready-made array).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Show a fact such as 4 × 3 as 4 groups of 3 and as an array with 4 rows of 3.
2. Solve a division story by sharing or by grouping, and say which question the story is asking.
3. Work out an unknown fact from known facts by turning an array or breaking it into two parts.
4. Interpret a remainder in a story and explain why a number cannot be divided by 0.

## Core ideas

### Equal groups and arrays

4 × 3 means **4 groups of 3**. If there are 4 bags with 3 apples in each, there are 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12 apples, so 4 × 3 = 12. The numbers being multiplied are **factors**; the answer is the **product**.

An **array** puts the objects in rows and columns. An array with 4 rows of 3 dots shows 4 × 3. Turn the paper sideways and it becomes 3 rows of 4: 3 × 4. Nothing was added or taken away, so 4 × 3 = 3 × 4. This is the **commutative property** of multiplication (the "turn-around" fact).

### Breaking facts apart

An array can be cut into two smaller arrays. A 6 × 7 array is the same as a 6 × 5 array next to a 6 × 2 array:

6 × 7 = 6 × 5 + 6 × 2 = 30 + 12 = 42

This is the **distributive property**. It lets the learner build hard facts from easy ones, such as fives and twos.

Some patterns that help with facts up to 10 × 10:

- × 2 is a double: 2 × 8 = 8 + 8 = 16.
- × 5 is half of × 10: 10 × 7 = 70, so 5 × 7 = 35.
- × 9 is × 10 minus one group: 9 × 6 = 60 − 6 = 54.
- × 1 leaves a number unchanged: 1 × 8 = 8. × 0 gives 0: 0 groups of anything is nothing.

### Two meanings of division

12 ÷ 3 can ask two different questions:

- **Sharing (how many in each group?)** 12 cookies shared equally by 3 children. Deal them out one at a time to 3 plates. Each child gets 4.
- **Grouping (how many groups?)** 12 cookies put in bags of 3. Keep filling bags of 3 until the cookies run out. You make 4 bags.

Both give 12 ÷ 3 = 4, but the 4 means different things: cookies per child, or number of bags.

### Fact families

Multiplication and division facts come in families. From the array of 3 rows of 4:

3 × 4 = 12, 4 × 3 = 12, 12 ÷ 3 = 4, 12 ÷ 4 = 3

So to find 28 ÷ 4, ask "4 times what is 28?" Because 4 × 7 = 28, the answer is 7.

### Remainders and zero

Sometimes the objects do not split evenly. 14 ÷ 4: 3 groups of 4 use 12, and 2 are left over. We write 14 ÷ 4 = 3 R 2 ("3 remainder 2"). In a story, the remainder has to be interpreted (see Example 3).

Dividing by 0 has no answer. 6 ÷ 0 would ask "0 times what makes 6?" Every number times 0 is 0, never 6, so there is no answer. On the other hand, 0 ÷ 6 = 0: sharing 0 cookies among 6 children gives each child 0.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: 6 × 7 from facts you know

1. Draw a 6 × 7 array on grid paper: 6 rows, 7 squares in each row.
2. Draw a line after the 5th column. The left part is 6 rows of 5; the right part is 6 rows of 2.
3. Left: 6 × 5 = 30 (count by fives six times: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30).
4. Right: 6 × 2 = 12 (double 6).
5. Add the parts: 30 + 12 = 42. So 6 × 7 = 42.
6. Check with a different split: 6 × 7 = 3 × 7 + 3 × 7 = 21 + 21 = 42. Same answer.

### Example 2: Sharing or grouping?

*Illustrative stories.*

- (a) "15 stickers are shared equally among 3 friends. How many does each friend get?"
- (b) "15 stickers are put on pages, 3 per page. How many pages are used?"

1. In (a) the number of groups (3 friends) is known. Deal the stickers out one by one to 3 piles until all 15 are gone. Each pile has 5. Answer: 5 stickers each.
2. In (b) the size of each group (3 per page) is known. Make groups of 3 until the stickers run out: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, which is 5 groups. Answer: 5 pages.
3. Both stories are 15 ÷ 3 = 5, and both check with 3 × 5 = 15.

### Example 3: What to do with the remainder

*Illustrative story.* "23 children are going on a trip. Each car holds 4 children. How many cars are needed?"

1. This is grouping: groups of 4. 4 × 5 = 20 and 4 × 6 = 24, so 23 ÷ 4 is 5 with 3 left over: 5 R 3.
2. Five cars carry 20 children. The 3 children left over still need a ride.
3. So the answer to the story is **6 cars**, not 5 and not "5 R 3."
4. If the question were "How many cars can be filled completely?" the answer would be 5. The same division gives different answers depending on the question.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **Reading 3 × 4 as 3 + 4.** Build it: 3 groups of 4 counters is 12, while 3 counters and 4 counters is 7.
- **"Multiplying always makes a number bigger."** That is true for whole numbers greater than 1, but 1 × 8 = 8 and 0 × 8 = 0. Later, with fractions, multiplying can make a number smaller ([APP-02.5 — Fractions — Advanced Operations](/app-025-fractions-advanced-operations)).
- **"Division works in either order."** 12 ÷ 3 = 4, but 3 ÷ 12 is not 4. Sharing 12 cookies among 3 children is not the same as sharing 3 cookies among 12.
- **Mixing up 0 ÷ 6 and 6 ÷ 0.** 0 ÷ 6 = 0; 6 ÷ 0 has no answer.
- **Unequal groups.** Division and multiplication only describe groups that are all the same size. Three plates with 5, 4, and 6 crackers are not "3 × 5."

## Practice

1. Draw an array for 5 × 4. Then turn it to show the turn-around fact. Write both equations.
2. Use breaking apart to find 7 × 8. Show the two smaller facts you used.
3. Use the × 9 pattern to find 9 × 7.
4. *Illustrative story:* 24 crayons are shared equally into 6 boxes. How many crayons are in each box? Is this sharing or grouping?
5. *Illustrative story:* 24 crayons are packed 8 to a box. How many boxes? Is this sharing or grouping?
6. Write the whole fact family for 3, 6, and 18.
7. *Illustrative story:* 17 muffins are put on plates of 5. How many plates are completely full, and how many muffins are left over?
8. What is 0 ÷ 9? What is 9 ÷ 0? Explain.

<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. 5 rows of 4 and 4 rows of 5: 5 × 4 = 20 and 4 × 5 = 20.
2. One way: 7 × 8 = 7 × 5 + 7 × 3 = 35 + 21 = 56. Another: 7 × 8 = 7 × 4 + 7 × 4 = 28 + 28 = 56.
3. 9 × 7 = 10 × 7 − 7 = 70 − 7 = 63.
4. 4 crayons in each box. Sharing: the number of groups (6 boxes) is known.
5. 3 boxes. Grouping: the size of each group (8) is known.
6. 3 × 6 = 18, 6 × 3 = 18, 18 ÷ 3 = 6, 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
7. 17 ÷ 5 = 3 R 2. Three plates are completely full and 2 muffins are left over.
8. 0 ÷ 9 = 0 (nothing to share means each group gets 0). 9 ÷ 0 has no answer, because no number times 0 equals 9.

**Adult self-check:** Every division answer can be checked by multiplying back: quotient × divisor, plus any remainder, must equal the starting number (for question 7: 3 × 5 + 2 = 17). A remainder must always be smaller than the number you divided by.
