# APP-04.1 — Forces & Motion — Foundations

A **force** is a push or a pull. You push a door open, pull a wagon, and kick a ball. Forces are how things start moving, stop, speed up, slow down, or change direction. This lesson is the first one in physical science. It uses toys, ramps, and your own body to explore forces and motion.

The course plan places this lesson in grades K-2. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

None in this course. It helps to be able to count, to compare "more" and "less," and to measure with a ruler or with blocks, as in [APP-02.7 — Measurement & Data](/app-027-measurement-data).

> **Adult note:** Gather a toy car or ball, a board or stiff book to use as a ramp, a few books to raise it, a towel, masking tape, and a strong toy magnet with a paper clip. A phone timer or counting aloud ("one-one-thousand") works for time.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Say that a force is a push or a pull with a strength (how hard) and a direction (which way), and name contact and non-contact forces.
2. Describe motion with words for position, direction, and speed.
3. Draw arrows to show balanced and unbalanced forces and predict what will happen.
4. Explain that a rolling ball stops because a force (friction) acts on it, and that gravity pulls things down toward Earth.

## Core ideas

### Pushes and pulls

Every force has two parts:

- **Strength:** a gentle push or a hard push.
- **Direction:** toward you, away from you, up, down, left, or right.

We draw a force as an **arrow**. The arrow points the way the force acts. A longer arrow means a stronger force.

### Contact and non-contact forces

Some forces only act when things touch. These are **contact forces**. Your hand pushing a swing and your shoe kicking a ball are contact forces. **Friction** is a contact force too. It acts where two surfaces rub, and it works against sliding. A ball rolls farther on a smooth floor than on a thick rug because the rug causes more friction.

Some forces act without touching. These are **non-contact forces**:

- **Gravity** pulls things toward Earth. That is why a dropped spoon falls down and never up.
- **Magnetism** lets a magnet pull a paper clip from a short distance away, before they touch.

### Describing motion

To describe how something moves, tell:

- **Position:** where it is. "The car is next to the chair."
- **Direction:** which way it goes. "It rolls toward the door."
- **Speed:** how fast it goes. A fast car covers more distance in the same time than a slow car.

### Balanced and unbalanced forces

When two people pull a rope equally hard in opposite directions, the rope does not move. The forces are **balanced**: the arrows are the same length and point opposite ways. When one side pulls harder, the forces are **unbalanced**, and the rope moves toward the stronger pull.

- Balanced forces: no change. A still object stays still. A moving object keeps going the same way at the same speed.
- Unbalanced forces: a change. The object starts, stops, speeds up, slows down, or turns.

### Things keep doing what they are doing

A ball at rest stays at rest until a force moves it. A rolling ball would keep rolling if nothing pushed against it. Real balls do stop, but that is because forces act on them: friction from the floor and air pushing back. Later this idea gets a name, Newton's first law, in [APP-04.5 — Forces & Motion — Newtons Laws](/app-045-forces-motion-newtons-laws).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A bigger push on a toy car

*Activity to try.* Put a toy car on a smooth floor. Give it a gentle push, then a harder push in the same direction.

1. **Predict:** which push will send the car farther? Say why.
2. **Think about the force:** the harder push is a longer arrow. More force for the same car makes a bigger change in its motion, so it should leave your hand faster.
3. **Measure:** mark the start with tape. After each push, mark where the car stops. Count how many footsteps or blocks from the start to each mark.
4. **Explain the stop:** the car stopped both times because friction and air pushed against its motion. Nothing "ran out" inside the car.
5. **Check fairness:** use the same car, the same floor, and push in the same direction, so the only thing you changed was how hard you pushed.

> **Adult note:** Results vary with the toy and the floor. If a harder push did not go farther, look for an unfair test (a different direction, or the car hit something) rather than telling the learner what they "should" have seen.

### Example 2: Reading a force arrow drawing

A drawing shows a box. One arrow pushes right and is 4 squares long. Another arrow pushes left and is 4 squares long.

1. Compare lengths: 4 and 4 are equal, so the pushes are equally strong.
2. Compare directions: one points right, one points left. They are opposite.
3. Equal and opposite means **balanced**.
4. Prediction: if the box was still, it stays still.
5. Now the right arrow grows to 6 squares. 6 is more than 4, so the forces are **unbalanced** and the box starts to move to the right, toward the stronger push.

### Example 3: Which toy is faster?

*Illustrative example.* Two toy cars roll for 3 seconds. The red car goes 8 blocks. The blue car goes 5 blocks.

1. The time is the same for both cars: 3 seconds.
2. Compare the distances: 8 blocks is more than 5 blocks.
3. In the same time, the red car went farther, so the red car was faster.
4. Boundary: if the times were different, you could not compare this way. A car that rolls 8 blocks in 10 seconds is slower than one that rolls 5 blocks in 3 seconds.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A moving thing needs a push all the time, or it stops."** Things stop because a force such as friction acts against them. On ice, which has little friction, a puck slides a long way after one push.
- **"If something is not moving, no forces act on it."** A book on a table has gravity pulling it down and the table pushing it up. The forces are balanced, so it stays still.
- **"Heavy things always fall faster."** Drop a sheet of paper and a crumpled ball made from the same kind of sheet. They weigh the same, but the flat sheet drifts down slowly because air pushes against its large flat side. Air, not weight alone, makes the difference here.
- **"Gravity only pulls on things that are falling."** Gravity pulls on you right now. The chair or floor pushes up just as hard, so you do not fall.
- **"Magnets pull on everything."** A magnet pulls a steel paper clip but not a plastic spoon, a wooden block, or an aluminum can. Test and sort objects to see.

## Practice

1. Is opening a drawer a push or a pull? Is closing it a push or a pull?
2. Sort these into contact or non-contact forces: kicking a ball, a magnet lifting a paper clip, an apple falling from a tree, pulling a wagon.
3. Draw a box with a 3-square arrow pointing left and a 3-square arrow pointing right. Balanced or unbalanced? What does the box do?
4. A ball rolls across a rug and stops. What force made it stop?
5. *Illustrative example:* In 5 seconds, a snail moves 2 centimeters and a ladybug moves 9 centimeters. Which is faster? How do you know?
6. You lift your backpack straight up. Which way is your force? Which way does gravity pull on the backpack?
7. Why does a ball roll farther on a smooth floor than on grass?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. Opening a drawer is a pull. Closing it is a push.
2. Contact: kicking a ball, pulling a wagon. Non-contact: the magnet and the paper clip (it pulls before touching), and the falling apple (gravity).
3. Balanced. The arrows are the same length and point opposite ways. If the box was still, it stays still.
4. Friction between the ball and the rug (and some push from the air).
5. The ladybug. In the same 5 seconds it went farther, 9 centimeters compared with 2.
6. Your force points up. Gravity pulls down.
7. Grass causes more friction than a smooth floor, so it slows the ball faster.

**Adult self-check:** Ask the learner to draw arrows for answers 3, 4, and 6. Check that the arrow points the way the force acts, not the way the object happens to be moving, and that a stronger force gets a longer arrow.

</details>

> **Safety note:** Roll cars and balls along the floor, not toward people or pets, and keep the ramp low. Use a large toy magnet. Small magnets and small parts can be swallowed, so keep them away from young children.
