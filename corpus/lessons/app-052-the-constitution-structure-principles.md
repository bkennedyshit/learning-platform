# APP-05.2 — The Constitution — Structure & Principles

The **Constitution of the United States** is the written plan of the national government. It creates three branches, lists what the national government may and may not do, sets the relationship between the national government and the states, and explains how the document itself can be changed. Signed on September 17, 1787, and in effect since 1789, it has been amended 27 times.

This lesson, for high school students (grades 9–12), is a guided tour of the document's structure and the principles built into it. Have the text open while you read: the [National Archives transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript) is free and searchable. When this lesson quotes the Constitution, the spelling and capitalization are the original's.

## Prerequisites

- [APP-05.1 — Foundations of American Democracy](/app-051-foundations-of-american-democracy): popular sovereignty, separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism, and why the Articles of Confederation were replaced.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Identify the subject of each of the **seven articles** and locate a given clause in the correct article.
2. Classify a power as **enumerated, implied, reserved, concurrent, or denied**, and support the classification with text.
3. Trace the four routes in **Article V** for amending the Constitution and calculate what each step requires.
4. Explain how ***McCulloch v. Maryland*** (1819) used the **Necessary and Proper Clause** and the **Supremacy Clause**.

## Core ideas

### The Preamble and the seven articles

The Preamble begins "We the People of the United States" and lists the purposes of the government, such as to "establish Justice" and "secure the Blessings of Liberty." It states goals; it does not itself grant powers.

| Article | Subject | Clauses to know |
|---|---|---|
| I | Legislative branch (Congress) | House and Senate qualifications; "All Bills for raising Revenue shall originate in the House"; the veto and override (Section 7); enumerated powers (Section 8); limits on Congress (Section 9) and on states (Section 10) |
| II | Executive branch (president) | Election through electors; qualifications; commander in chief; treaties and appointments with the Senate; "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" |
| III | Judicial branch | "one supreme Court" and lower courts Congress creates; judges serve "during good Behaviour"; jurisdiction; treason defined |
| IV | States and their relations | Full Faith and Credit; Privileges and Immunities; admission of new states; guarantee of a "Republican Form of Government" |
| V | Amendment process | Two ways to propose, two ways to ratify; equal Senate suffrage cannot be removed from a state without its consent |
| VI | Debts, supremacy, oaths | The Supremacy Clause; officials' oath to support the Constitution; "no religious Test shall ever be required" for federal office |
| VII | Ratification | Approval by conventions of nine states puts the Constitution into effect among them |

The National Constitution Center labels the articles the same way: Legislative Branch; Executive Branch; Judicial Branch; States, Citizenship, New States; Amendment Process; Debts, Supremacy, Oaths, Religious Tests; Ratification.

### Principles built into the structure

- **Popular sovereignty:** the people are the source of authority ("We the People").
- **Limited government:** the national government has the powers the Constitution grants; Article I, Section 9 forbids some actions outright, such as "No Bill of Attainder or ex post facto Law shall be passed."
- **Separation of powers:** making, carrying out, and interpreting law are assigned to different branches in Articles I, II, and III.
- **Checks and balances:** the president may veto bills; Congress may override; the Senate confirms appointments and approves treaties; the House impeaches and the Senate tries impeachments; judges serve for good behavior so they are independent of the other branches.
- **Federalism:** power is divided between the national government and the states.
- **Judicial review:** the power of courts to strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution. It is not written in the text; the Supreme Court established it in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), covered in Lesson 5.5.
- **Individual rights:** protected mainly by the amendments, beginning with the Bill of Rights.

### Kinds of power in a federal system

| Kind | Definition | Textual basis | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enumerated (expressed) | Listed national powers | Article I, Section 8 | Lay and collect taxes; borrow money; regulate commerce "among the several States"; coin money; declare war |
| Implied | Powers that are reasonable means of carrying out enumerated powers | The Necessary and Proper (elastic) Clause: "To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers" | Chartering a national bank (*McCulloch*) |
| Reserved | Powers left to the states or the people | Tenth Amendment: "The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people" | Setting most criminal and traffic law, licensing professions, organizing public schools |
| Concurrent | Powers both levels may use | Not listed in one place; follows from powers granted to Congress without being denied to states | Taxing, borrowing, setting up courts, making and enforcing laws |
| Denied (prohibited) | Powers neither level, or one level, may use | Article I, Sections 9 and 10; many amendments | Congress may not pass ex post facto laws; no state may "enter into any Treaty" or "coin Money" |

Three clauses manage relations among levels and states:

- **Supremacy Clause** (Article VI): "This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties ... shall be the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby." Note the condition "made in Pursuance thereof": a federal law has to be within national power to be supreme.
- **Full Faith and Credit** (Article IV, Section 1): "Full Faith and Credit shall be given in each State to the public Acts, Records, and judicial Proceedings of every other State." A court judgment from one state can generally be enforced in another.
- **Privileges and Immunities** (Article IV, Section 2): "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States." A state cannot generally treat visitors from other states as second-class citizens for fundamental purposes.

### Amending the Constitution (Article V)

| Step | Route A | Route B |
|---|---|---|
| Propose | Two-thirds of both the House and the Senate | A convention called by Congress when two-thirds of state legislatures (34 of 50) apply |
| Ratify | Legislatures of three-fourths of the states (38 of 50) | Conventions in three-fourths of the states (38 of 50); Congress chooses the method |

According to the National Archives, "None of the 27 amendments to the Constitution have been proposed by constitutional convention." Congress proposes an amendment by joint resolution, and "since the President does not have a constitutional role in the amendment process," the resolution does not go to the president. Only the Twenty-first Amendment, which repealed Prohibition, was ratified by state conventions; its text required ratification "by conventions in the several States."

### The 27 amendments at a glance

| Amendments | Ratified | Main subject |
|---|---|---|
| 1–10 (Bill of Rights) | 1791 | Speech, religion, press, assembly, petition; arms; quartering; searches; due process and self-incrimination; trial rights; jury in civil cases; bail and punishment; unenumerated rights (9th); reserved powers (10th) |
| 11–12 | 1795, 1804 | Limits on suits against states in federal court; separate electoral votes for president and vice president |
| 13–15 | 1865, 1868, 1870 | Abolition of slavery; citizenship, due process, and equal protection; voting rights regardless of race |
| 16–19 | 1913–1920 | Federal income tax; direct election of senators; Prohibition; women's suffrage |
| 20–21 | 1933 | New start dates for terms; repeal of Prohibition |
| 22–27 | 1951–1992 | Presidential two-term limit; electors for D.C.; no poll taxes in federal elections; presidential disability and succession; voting age 18; timing of congressional pay changes |

The 1st, 4th, 5th, 6th, and 14th Amendments come up most often in court cases about individual rights; Lesson 5.6 covers them in depth.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Classifying powers

For each action, find the text that authorizes or forbids it.

| Action | Classification | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Congress sets a tariff on imported steel | Enumerated | Article I, Section 8 lists the power to lay "Duties" and to regulate commerce "with foreign Nations." |
| A state raises its sales tax | Concurrent | Taxing is granted to Congress but not denied to states; both levels tax. |
| A state sets requirements for a cosmetology license | Reserved | Not delegated to the national government or denied to states, so the Tenth Amendment leaves it with the states. |
| Congress creates a federal agency to carry out its commerce laws | Implied | No clause says "create agencies," but the Necessary and Proper Clause allows laws that carry out enumerated powers. |
| A state prints its own currency | Denied to states | Article I, Section 10: "No State shall ... coin Money; emit Bills of Credit." |
| Congress passes a law punishing an act that was legal when it was done | Denied to Congress | Article I, Section 9 bans ex post facto laws. |

**Reasoning pattern:** start by searching the text for a grant; if there is none, look for a denial; if neither applies to the states, the power is reserved.

### Example 2: Counting votes for an amendment

1. **House:** if all 435 members vote, two-thirds is 290 (435 × 2/3 = 290).
2. **Senate:** if all 100 senators vote, two-thirds is 66.67, so 67 votes are needed; a vote cannot be split, so round up.
3. **Ratification:** three-fourths of 50 states is 37.5, so 38 states are required. The National Archives states the same number.
4. **Blocking:** if 38 are needed, then 13 states that refuse to ratify can stop an amendment (50 − 38 + 1 = 13).

**Check with history:** the Twenty-sixth Amendment passed Congress on March 23, 1971, and was ratified on July 1, 1971, 100 days later. The Twenty-seventh Amendment was proposed on September 25, 1789, and ratified on May 7, 1992, more than 202 years later.

### Example 3: Reasoning through *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819)

**Facts (National Archives and Oyez):** In 1816 Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland passed a law in 1818 taxing banks not chartered by the state. James McCulloch, the cashier of the Baltimore branch of the national bank, refused to pay.

1. **Question one: may Congress charter a bank?** The word "bank" does not appear in Article I, Section 8. But Congress has enumerated powers to tax, borrow, and regulate commerce. Chief Justice John Marshall read "necessary" in the Necessary and Proper Clause to mean, in Oyez's summary, "appropriate and legitimate" rather than "absolutely indispensable." A bank is an appropriate means to carry out financial powers. **Conclusion:** yes, as an implied power.
2. **Question two: may Maryland tax the national bank?** The bank is a lawful exercise of national power, so laws establishing it are "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution and are supreme. A state tax on it would let a state control an instrument of the national government. **Conclusion:** no.
3. **Result:** a unanimous Court ruled for McCulloch. The case is still the foundational reading of implied powers.

**Why it matters for analysis:** the Court answered two questions in order: first whether the national government had the power, then whether the state's action conflicted with it. Use the same order when you analyze a federal-state conflict.

### Example 4: An illustrative supremacy question

**Illustrative scenario:** Congress, using its power to regulate interstate commerce, sets a national safety standard for a product sold across state lines. A state passes a law allowing sale of a version that fails the national standard.

1. Is the federal law within an enumerated or implied power? The product moves "among the several States," so the commerce power is the likely basis.
2. If the federal law is valid, the Supremacy Clause makes it controlling, and judges "in every State shall be bound thereby."
3. Does the state law actually conflict? Here, yes: obeying the state law means violating the federal standard.
4. Conclusion: the state provision likely cannot be enforced to the extent it conflicts. If the federal law were outside Congress's powers, step 2 would fail and the state law could stand.

Real cases add complications, such as whether Congress intended to displace all state regulation in a field. The order of reasoning still applies.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Constitution lists everything the government may do."** It lists enumerated powers and then adds the Necessary and Proper Clause, which supports implied powers.
- **"The Tenth Amendment means states win any conflict."** The Tenth Amendment reserves only powers "not delegated to the United States." When a national law is a valid use of a delegated power, the Supremacy Clause makes it controlling.
- **"The Supremacy Clause means any federal action beats any state action."** Only laws "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution are supreme. An unconstitutional federal law is not.
- **"The president signs amendments."** The president has no constitutional role in proposing or ratifying amendments.
- **"Amendments can only come from Congress."** Article V also allows a convention called at the request of two-thirds of state legislatures. It has never been used.
- **"The Bill of Rights was part of the original Constitution."** The original 1787 text had a few rights protections, such as the ban on ex post facto laws, but the Bill of Rights was added as amendments ratified in 1791.
- **"The Preamble gives the government power to promote the general welfare."** The Preamble states purposes. Powers come from the articles and amendments.
- **Boundary: the text is not the whole story.** Courts interpret broad phrases such as "commerce among the several States" and "due process of law." Knowing the text is necessary, but you also need the cases, which later lessons cover.
