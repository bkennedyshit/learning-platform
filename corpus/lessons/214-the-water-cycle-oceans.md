# 21.4 - The Water Cycle & Oceans

Water is always moving: it evaporates from the ocean, travels as vapor, falls as rain or snow, runs into rivers, soaks into the ground, and eventually returns to the sea. The ocean is the largest reservoir in this cycle and also a giant, slowly moving system of its own, with mountains and trenches on its floor and currents that carry heat around the planet. This lesson follows water through the cycle, measures where it is stored, and explains how the ocean is structured and how it circulates.

## Core ideas

### The water cycle

| Process | What happens | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| **Evaporation** | Liquid water becomes water vapor | Solar energy |
| **Transpiration** | Plants release water vapor through their leaves (together with evaporation: **evapotranspiration**) | Solar energy |
| **Condensation** | Vapor cools and forms droplets (clouds, fog, dew) | Cooling of rising air |
| **Precipitation** | Rain, snow, sleet, or hail falls | Gravity |
| **Runoff** | Water flows over the land into streams and rivers | Gravity |
| **Infiltration** | Water soaks into the soil | Gravity |
| **Percolation** | Water keeps moving down through soil and rock to reach groundwater (recharge) | Gravity |

Two forces run the cycle: the **Sun** supplies the energy that lifts water into the atmosphere, and **gravity** brings it back down and pulls it downhill.

### Where Earth's water is

The USGS water-distribution table (from Igor Shiklomanov's estimates) gives these values for the total of about 332.5 million cubic miles of water:

| Reservoir | Percent of all water | Percent of freshwater |
|---|---|---|
| Oceans, seas, and bays | 96.54% | — (saline) |
| Ice caps, glaciers, and permanent snow | 1.74% | 68.7% |
| Fresh groundwater | 0.76% | 30.1% |
| Fresh lakes | 0.007% | 0.26% |
| Atmosphere | 0.001% | 0.04% |
| Rivers | 0.0002% | 0.006% |

USGS summarizes: only about **2.5%** of Earth's water is fresh, and almost all of that is locked in ice or underground. Rivers and lakes, which supply most of the water people use, hold only about 1/150 of one percent of the total.

### Groundwater and aquifers

Below the surface, water fills the small spaces (pores and cracks) between grains of sediment and rock. The **zone of saturation** is the region where these spaces are completely full; its top surface is the **water table**. Above it is the **unsaturated zone**, which still holds some water. USGS calls a water-bearing rock or sediment that readily transmits water to wells and springs an **aquifer**.

- **Porosity** is how much open space a material has; **permeability** is how easily water flows through it. Sand and gravel are usually permeable; dense granite yields little water.
- **Recharge** is water added to an aquifer by precipitation that infiltrates and percolates down. Recharge rates differ from aquifer to aquifer.
- USGS warns that pumping too much water too fast draws down the water in the aquifer. Long-term water-level decline from sustained pumping is called **groundwater depletion**. In the United States, groundwater is the drinking-water source for about half of the population.

### The ocean floor

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Continental shelf** | Gently sloping, shallow edge of a continent, typically less than about 200 m deep |
| **Continental slope** | Steeper drop from about 200 m to several thousand meters over a few hundred kilometers |
| **Continental rise** | A gentler apron of sediment at the base of the slope |
| **Abyssal plain** | Very flat deep-ocean floor, about 4,000–6,000 m deep, covered with sediment |
| **Mid-ocean ridge** | Underwater volcanic mountain chain more than 50,000 km long where new crust forms (lesson 21.2) |
| **Trench** | Deep, narrow canyon at a subduction zone; the Challenger Deep is nearly 11,000 m deep |

### Seawater properties

- **Salinity** is the amount of dissolved salt. Seawater averages about **35 parts per thousand (ppt)**, meaning about 35 g of salt per kilogram (or roughly per liter) of seawater, and usually ranges from 33 to 37 ppt. Sodium and chloride make up over 90% of the dissolved ions. Salt comes mainly from rocks weathered on land: rivers carry the dissolved ions to the sea. Hydrothermal vents at ridges add more.
- Salinity is **higher** where evaporation is strong (the Red Sea and Persian Gulf reach about 40 ppt) and **lower** near the equator (heavy rain) and near the poles (melting ice).
- **Temperature** is highest in the sunlit surface layer. Below it, the **thermocline** is a layer where temperature drops rapidly with depth. Deep water stays near 4 °C or colder.
- **Density** of seawater **increases** when salinity increases and when temperature decreases. Cold, salty water sinks below warm or fresher water, so the ocean is **stratified** (layered).

### Ocean currents

- **Surface currents** are driven by **wind**. NOAA describes winds driving currents in the upper 100 meters of the ocean, and Earle describes the major surface currents as involving the upper few hundred meters. Because Earth rotates, moving water and air are deflected to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere (the **Coriolis effect**), so large surface currents form circular **gyres**. Warm currents such as the **Gulf Stream** flow poleward along the east coasts of continents; cold currents such as the California Current flow toward the equator along west coasts.
- **Deep currents** are driven by **density** differences, controlled by temperature (*thermo*) and salinity (*haline*): **thermohaline circulation**. In polar seas, sea ice forms and leaves its salt behind, so the water below becomes saltier, denser, and sinks. Surface water flows in to replace it. This drives the **global conveyor belt**, a slow loop through the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans. NOAA estimates that once water sinks in the North Atlantic, it takes about 1,000–1,200 years to return to the upper ocean.
- Currents move heat. NOAA notes that the Gulf Stream helps give Norway and the British Isles winters about 10 °C (18 °F) warmer than other regions at the same latitude.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: How much water is fresh?

Use the USGS numbers to check the claim that about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh.

1. Ice, glaciers, and permanent snow hold 5,773,000 cubic miles, which is 68.7% of all freshwater.
2. Total freshwater $\approx 5{,}773{,}000 \div 0.687 \approx 8{,}400{,}000$ cubic miles.
3. Fraction of all water: $8{,}400{,}000 \div 332{,}500{,}000 \approx 0.025$, or **2.5%**. ✓
4. Fresh surface water: lakes (21,830) + rivers (509) = 22,339 cubic miles. As a fraction of all water: $22{,}339 \div 332{,}500{,}000 \approx 0.0067\%$, which matches "1/150 of one percent" ($1 \div 150 \approx 0.0067$). ✓

### Example 2: A 1,000-liter model of Earth's water

*Illustrative scale model.* If all of Earth's water were 1,000 liters, how much would be in each reservoir?

1. Oceans: $0.9654 \times 1{,}000 = 965.4$ L.
2. Ice, glaciers, and snow: $0.0174 \times 1{,}000 = 17.4$ L.
3. Fresh groundwater: $0.0076 \times 1{,}000 = 7.6$ L.
4. Fresh lakes and rivers: about $0.0067\% \times 1{,}000\ \text{L} = 0.067$ L, or about **67 mL**, less than half a cup.
5. Interpretation: the water most people use every day is a tiny fraction of the total, which is why groundwater and surface water are managed carefully.

### Example 3: Following a raindrop

*Illustrative scenario.* Rain falls on a hillside farm. Name each process.

1. Rain falling from a cloud → **precipitation**.
2. Some water flows downhill across a plowed field into a stream → **runoff**.
3. Some soaks into the soil → **infiltration**; part of it moves deeper to the water table → **percolation** (aquifer **recharge**).
4. Corn plants take up soil water and release vapor from their leaves → **transpiration**.
5. A puddle dries in the afternoon Sun → **evaporation**.
6. That evening, dew forms on the grass → **condensation**.

### Example 4: Salt left behind

Seawater has a salinity of 35 ppt. How much salt is in 2 kg of seawater, and what happens to salinity if half the water evaporates?

1. 35 ppt means 35 g of salt per 1,000 g of seawater.
2. Salt in 2 kg: $35 \times 2 = 70$ g. The rest, $2{,}000 - 70 = 1{,}930$ g, is water.
3. If half the water evaporates, $1{,}930 \div 2 = 965$ g of water remains, and the salt stays behind.
4. New salinity: $\dfrac{70}{965 + 70} \times 1{,}000 = \dfrac{70}{1{,}035} \times 1{,}000 \approx 67.6$ ppt.
5. Interpretation: evaporation raises salinity, which is why the Red Sea, with strong evaporation and little river inflow, is saltier than the open ocean.

### Example 5: Which water sinks?

Rank these water samples from most dense to least dense: (A) cold, salty polar water; (B) warm, salty tropical water; (C) cold water freshened by melting ice.

1. Density rises with **salinity** and with **lower temperature**.
2. A is both cold and salty → densest.
3. B is salty but warm; C is cold but fresh. Without measured values you cannot rank B and C for certain, which is an honest limit of the rule.
4. A sinks beneath both, which is what starts deep thermohaline flow in polar seas.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Groundwater flows in underground lakes and rivers."** Most groundwater fills tiny pores and cracks in sediment and rock, like water in a sponge; large open caves with streams exist only in some rock types such as limestone.
- **"Clouds are water vapor."** Water vapor is an invisible gas. Clouds are tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals that formed by condensation.
- **"Most of Earth's freshwater is in lakes and rivers."** About 68.7% is ice and 30.1% is groundwater; lakes and rivers hold well under 1% of freshwater.
- **"The ocean floor is flat and featureless."** It has the longest mountain range on Earth and trenches deeper than Everest is tall. At the same time, profiles of the sea floor usually exaggerate the vertical scale, and the abyssal plains really are among the flattest surfaces on Earth.
- **"Ocean currents are all caused by wind."** Wind drives the surface currents, but the deep ocean moves because of density differences, and tides create tidal currents near shore.
- **"Sea ice is salty frozen seawater."** NOAA explains that ice does not take salt into its crystal structure; the salt is pushed into the water below, which is part of why polar water becomes dense and sinks.

## Practice

1. Put these in an order that makes a complete loop, starting from the ocean: precipitation, evaporation, runoff, condensation.
2. Which two forces drive the water cycle, and which processes does each power?
3. Using the USGS table, what percent of all water is fresh groundwater? How many times more freshwater is stored as ice than as fresh groundwater?
4. A town's well level has dropped every year for 20 years, even in wet years. What is the most likely cause, and what is this called?
5. Name the ocean-floor feature: (a) a flat, sediment-covered plain about 5,000 m deep, (b) a narrow canyon nearly 11,000 m deep, (c) shallow sea floor about 100 m deep next to a continent.
6. What mass of salt is in 500 g of seawater at 35 ppt?
7. Explain why water sinks when sea ice forms in polar seas.
8. Why are winters in the British Isles milder than in other places at the same latitude?

1. Evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff (back to the ocean).
2. The Sun powers evaporation and transpiration (lifting water into the air); gravity powers precipitation, runoff, infiltration, and percolation.
3. 0.76%. Ratio: $68.7 \div 30.1 \approx 2.3$ times more freshwater is stored as ice (or $1.74 \div 0.76 \approx 2.3$ using percents of all water).
4. Pumping faster than the aquifer is recharged; this long-term decline is groundwater depletion.
5. (a) abyssal plain (b) trench (c) continental shelf.

> **Safety note:** If you model the water cycle in a jar or bag, use warm tap water rather than boiling water and let an adult handle any hot water. Do not drink from streams, lakes, or "model" water. At the beach, NOAA advises swimming near a lifeguard and knowing how to swim before entering surf, because rip currents can pull even a waist-deep wader into deeper water.
