# APP-12.3 — Evidence, Analysis & Integration

**Evidence** is information you use to persuade a reader that a claim is true or reasonable: a quotation from a novel, a statistic from a survey, a result from an experiment, the judgment of an expert, a historical document. Information becomes evidence only when you connect it to a claim. The UNC Writing Center asks, "Does evidence speak for itself?" and answers, "Absolutely not." After you present evidence, you must explain why and how it supports your argument. That explanation is **analysis**, and it is usually what separates a strong paper from a list of facts.

This lesson covers choosing evidence, judging whether a source is trustworthy enough to use, weaving evidence into your own sentences, and explaining it. It is written for high school students and builds directly on the claims you learned to write in Lesson 12.2.

## Prerequisites

[APP-12.2 — Thesis & Argument Construction](/app-122-thesis-argument-construction): you should be able to write a debatable claim and recognize the grounds and warrant in an argument. You will also use percentages; if you need a refresher, see [13.3 — Fractions, Decimals & Percent](/133-fractions-decimals-percent).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Identify common **types of evidence** and explain what each can and cannot show.
2. Choose evidence that fits a claim, an audience, and a discipline, and judge a source with the **CRAAP** questions.
3. Integrate evidence with **context, attribution, explanation, and citation**, choosing among quotation, paraphrase, and summary.
4. Write **analysis** that explains how evidence supports a claim, and avoid plagiarism and overclaiming.

## Core ideas

### Types of evidence

| Type | Example | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Statistical / quantitative** | survey results, measurements, rates | shows scale and patterns | depends on who was measured and how; correlation is not causation |
| **Textual** | a quotation from a novel, speech, or document | direct proof of what a text says | needs interpretation; one line can be taken out of context |
| **Expert testimony** | a scientist's or historian's published judgment | brings specialized knowledge | only as strong as the expert's relevance and evidence; experts disagree |
| **Examples and cases** | a specific school's policy and its results | concrete and memorable | one case may not represent others |
| **Anecdote / personal experience** | a story from your own life | engaging; useful for illustrating | rarely enough to prove a general claim |
| **Firsthand observation or data you collect** | your interview notes, your lab measurements | original evidence | must be collected and reported carefully |

Disciplines value different kinds of evidence. UNC notes that a chemistry paper might rely on graphs and quantitative data, while an English paper relies on passages from the text and discussion of characters and symbols. Look at the kinds of evidence your course readings use.

**Primary and secondary.** UNC also points out that in research, "primary" means "first" or "original," not "most important." **Primary sources** are original materials, such as documents, photographs, interviews, data, or the literary work you are analyzing. **Secondary sources** present information that someone else has already processed or interpreted. Lesson 12.4 explores both, plus tertiary sources.

### Is the source trustworthy enough to use?

The **CRAAP Test**, created by the Meriam Library at California State University, Chico, is a list of questions grouped under five criteria:

- **Currency:** When was it published or updated? Does your topic need current information?
- **Relevance:** Does it answer your question? Is it written at an appropriate level for your needs?
- **Authority:** Who is the author or publisher? What are their credentials or affiliations?
- **Accuracy:** Where does the information come from? Is it supported by evidence? Was it reviewed? Can you verify it elsewhere?
- **Purpose:** Is it meant to inform, teach, sell, entertain, or persuade? Is it fact, opinion, or propaganda?

The library itself notes that "different criteria will be more or less important depending on your situation or need." A 150-year-old letter fails "currency" but may be exactly the primary source a history paper needs. Lesson 12.4 adds **lateral reading**, a faster way to check an unfamiliar website.

### Quote, paraphrase, or summarize

| Method | Use it when | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| **Quotation** | the exact wording matters: you will analyze the language, or the phrasing is memorable or authoritative | use quotation marks, quote only what you need, and cite |
| **Paraphrase** | the idea matters more than the wording, and you want to restate one passage in your own words and structure | change both words and sentence structure; still cite |
| **Summary** | you need the main point of a longer passage or whole source | much shorter than the original; still cite |

**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own. **Patchwriting** is a common form: copying a sentence and swapping a few synonyms or rearranging its parts. The UNC Writing Center is explicit that paraphrasing "does NOT mean changing a word or two in someone else's sentence, changing the sentence structure while maintaining the original words, or changing a few words to synonyms."

**Common knowledge.** Facts that are widely known and easily verified, such as the year a famous event happened, usually need no citation. UNC suggests two questions for students: *Did I know this before I took this course?* and *Did this idea come from my own brain?* If the answer to either is no, cite it.

### The quotation sandwich

The UNC Writing Center describes each quotation as the filling in a sandwich: "messy to eat without some bread on either side." Its four guidelines:

1. **Provide context.** Tell the reader when, where, and under what circumstances the words were written or spoken.
2. **Attribute the quotation.** Name who is speaking, using a **signal verb** such as *argues*, *notes*, *observes*, *suggests*, *points out*, *proposes*, or *complains*. Choose verbs carefully: *argues* signals a claim, *notes* signals a neutral observation, and *admits* implies reluctance.
3. **Explain the significance.** Say why the quotation matters for your point.
4. **Cite it.** Every quotation and paraphrase needs a citation (Lesson 12.5).

Many teachers use a short memory aid for evidence paragraphs, such as **ACE** (Assert, Cite, Explain) or **CER** (Claim, Evidence, Reasoning). They all say the same thing: a claim, then evidence, then your explanation of the link.

### Analysis: answering "so how does this prove it?"

Analysis explains the connection between the evidence and the claim, the warrant from Lesson 12.2. Useful moves include:

- **Zooming in** on a word or detail: *The word "because" makes misery the cause, not just a companion, of the creature's malice.*
- **Explaining implications:** *If even trained historians were fooled, the problem is not a lack of general knowledge.*
- **Considering limits:** *The survey includes only students in one grade, so it may not represent the whole school.*
- **Connecting back** to the thesis in the last sentence of the paragraph.

A rough rule of thumb for body paragraphs: your own analysis should take up at least as much space as the quoted or paraphrased material.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Building a quotation sandwich from a literary text

**Claim:** In *Frankenstein*, the creature presents his violence as a consequence of rejection.

1. **Context:** In Chapter 17, the creature confronts Victor and demands that he create a companion; Victor refuses.
2. **Attribution with a signal verb:** *The creature argues...*
3. **Quotation, trimmed to what the analysis needs:** "I am malicious because I am miserable."
4. **Explanation:** focus on the word *because*, which makes misery the cause of malice, and on the rhetorical question that follows.
5. **Citation (MLA style):** the Project Gutenberg edition has no fixed page numbers, so cite the chapter.

**Result:**

> When Victor refuses to create a companion, the creature does not deny his crimes; instead, he explains them. "I am malicious because I am miserable," he argues, and immediately asks, "Am I not shunned and hated by all mankind?" (Shelley, ch. 17). The word *because* turns misery from a feeling into a cause, and the question shifts responsibility outward, toward the people who rejected him. Shelley gives the creature an argument, not just a grievance, which forces the reader to weigh Victor's guilt against the creature's.

Reasoning: the paragraph has context (first clause), attribution (*he argues*), two short quotations, a citation, and three sentences of analysis that each do something different: zoom in on a word, explain the question's effect, and connect to the novel's larger tension.

### Example 2: Quotation, patchwriting, paraphrase, and summary

**Original sentence** (from the published abstract of Wineburg and McGrew's 2019 study): "Historians and students often fell victim to easily manipulated features of websites, such as official-looking logos and domain names."

1. **Quotation:** *Wineburg and McGrew (2019) report that "historians and students often fell victim to easily manipulated features of websites, such as official-looking logos and domain names."* Exact words, quotation marks, citation. Acceptable. (These examples use APA author-date citations; a final draft would also give a page or section locator for the quotation, as Lesson 12.5 explains.)
2. **Patchwriting (plagiarism):** *Historians and students frequently fell prey to easily faked features of sites, like official-seeming logos and domain names (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019).* The structure and most words are the original's with synonyms swapped. Not acceptable, even with a citation.
3. **Paraphrase:** *In one study, both the historians and the college students tended to trust a website because it looked official, judging it by surface details like its logo or web address (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019).* New structure and wording, same meaning, cited. Acceptable.
4. **Summary of the whole abstract:** *Wineburg and McGrew (2019) found that professional fact checkers judged websites more accurately and quickly than historians or students because they left the site to check what other sources said about it.* Main point only, cited.

Check the paraphrase against the original: it keeps "historians and students," "often" (as "tended to"), and the logo and domain-name examples, and it adds nothing the source does not say.

### Example 3: Using statistics without overclaiming

**Illustrative data, not a real study:** a student surveys 40 classmates in one ninth-grade homeroom. 30 say they study better with music on.

1. **Calculate:** 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75, so **75%** of respondents said this.
2. **Ask what the number measures:** it measures what students *say*, not how well they actually study.
3. **Ask who was measured:** one homeroom of 40 students, not a random sample of the school. See [17.3 — Sampling & Experimental Design](/173-sampling-experimental-design) for why that matters.
4. **Ask what it cannot show:** whether music *causes* better studying. Students who prefer music might differ in other ways. See [17.2 — Exploring Data (2-Variable)](/172-exploring-data-2-variable) on correlation and causation.

**Overclaiming:** *Music makes students study better, as 75% of students agree.*

**Accurate:** *In one homeroom survey, 30 of 40 students (75%) said they study better with music on; the result shows a common preference in that group, not that music improves studying.*

### Example 4: Revising a "dropped quotation" paragraph

**Draft (illustrative):**

> Roosevelt's speech was important. "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself." This shows he was a good leader.

Problems: no context, no attribution, no citation, and the analysis ("good leader") is vague.

**Revision:**

> Roosevelt delivered his first inaugural address on March 4, 1933, to a country in economic crisis. Rather than deny the danger, he redefined it: "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself--nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance." By naming fear as the enemy, Roosevelt turned public panic into something the nation could resist, which prepared listeners to accept the bold action his speech goes on to propose.

The revision adds context, embeds the quotation in the writer's own sentence, keeps the speech's original punctuation, and explains a specific effect. In a final draft, the writer would add a citation for the source of the speech text.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"More quotations make a stronger paper."** Quotations without analysis read as a collage. Quote only what you will discuss, and paraphrase the rest.
- **"If I cite it, I can copy the wording."** Without quotation marks, copied or patchwritten wording is plagiarism even with a citation.
- **"Paraphrases and summaries don't need citations."** They do. The idea still belongs to the source.
- **"Statistics are objective, so they prove my point."** Numbers depend on who was measured, how, and what was asked. Report what the data actually measured, and do not turn correlation into causation.
- **"Personal stories don't count as evidence."** Anecdotes can illustrate a point and are central in some genres, such as personal essays. They rarely prove a general claim by themselves.
- **"A source from a .edu or .gov site is automatically reliable."** Domain names are one clue, not a verdict. Wineburg and McGrew found that skilled readers were misled by official-looking domain names. Evaluate the content and the source's reputation.
- **Boundary: block quotations.** Long quotations are set off as indented blocks under MLA, APA, and Chicago rules, which differ in length thresholds; check your style guide and use them sparingly.
- **Boundary: altering quotations.** You may shorten a quotation with an ellipsis (...) or clarify with square brackets, but you must not change its meaning.
