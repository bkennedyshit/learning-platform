# APP-12.1 — The Writing Process

The **writing process** is the set of activities writers use to get from an assignment to a finished text: understanding the task, generating ideas (often called **prewriting** or **invention**), **drafting**, getting **feedback**, **revising**, **editing**, and **proofreading**. The process is **recursive**, which means writers loop back. A writer who discovers halfway through a draft that the evidence points somewhere unexpected goes back to planning and rewrites the thesis. The National Council of Teachers of English (NCTE) puts it directly: to say that writing is a process "is decidedly not to say that it should or can be turned into a formulaic set of steps," and experienced writers shift between operations depending on audience, purpose, genre, and deadlines.

This lesson opens the Research Writing & Academic Literacy course. The course plan places it in high school (grades 9-12) and early college, and it is written for the student. Every later lesson, from thesis writing to citation, happens inside this process.

## Prerequisites

You should be able to write a paragraph with a topic sentence, supporting details, and a concluding sentence, and you should know the basic parts of an essay (introduction, body paragraphs, conclusion). If either feels shaky, review [APP-01.8 — Writing — Essays & Research](/app-018-writing-essays-research) for the basics or [38.4 — Paragraph Construction & The Essay Structure](/384-paragraph-construction-the-essay-structure) for a more advanced treatment.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Turn a prompt into a focused question and use at least two **prewriting** strategies to generate and narrow ideas.
2. Draft from a **working thesis** without stopping to fix surface errors, and change the thesis when the draft shows it no longer fits.
3. Tell **revision** apart from **editing** and **proofreading**, and use a **reverse outline** to diagnose a draft.
4. Give peer feedback that **describes, evaluates, and suggests**, and turn feedback into a revision plan.

## Core ideas

### The activities of writing

| Activity | What you are doing | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding the task | Reading the prompt for its verb, audience, length, and source requirements | What is the question I must answer? Who is reading? |
| Prewriting (invention) | Generating and narrowing ideas before committing to an argument | What do I already know? What do I need to find out? |
| Drafting | Getting a full version on the page, guided by a working thesis | What is my point, and what supports it? |
| Feedback | Learning how a real reader experiences the draft | Where did the reader get lost, convinced, or bored? |
| Revising | Rethinking the thesis, structure, evidence, and fit to the audience | Does every paragraph serve the thesis? Is anything missing? |
| Editing | Improving sentences: clarity, word choice, concision, flow | Is each sentence as clear and direct as it can be? |
| Proofreading | Fixing errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation, and format | Is anything wrong that would distract a reader? |

The table reads top to bottom, but real writing does not. You might draft one paragraph, realize you need more research, return to prewriting, and then revise the thesis before you have drafted the conclusion. That is normal, not a sign of failure.

### Start with the prompt

Before generating ideas, find the task inside the assignment. Verbs such as *analyze*, *compare*, *evaluate*, and *argue* signal that you need a thesis, a claim a reader could question, not just a report of information. The University of North Carolina Writing Center suggests rephrasing prompts that are not written as questions: "Discuss the effect of X on Y" becomes "What is the effect of X on Y?" Your thesis should answer that question.

### Prewriting strategies

These four techniques come from the UNC Writing Center's handout on brainstorming. Try more than one; different topics respond to different methods.

- **Freewriting.** Write without stopping for a set time or a set length. Do not judge quality or fix spelling. If you are stuck, write that you are stuck. The point is to get past your internal critic.
- **Clustering (mapping, webbing).** Write terms and phrases anywhere on a page, then circle related ideas and draw lines between them. The clusters that form suggest possible focuses.
- **Journalistic questions.** Answer *Who? What? When? Where? Why? How?* about your topic. Gaps in your answers show what you need to research.
- **Cubing.** Look at the topic from six sides: *describe* it, *compare* it, *associate* it, *analyze* it, *apply* it, and *argue for and against* it.

A **scratch outline** (a short list of the points you expect to make, in order) is also a planning tool. It is a plan, not a contract; change it when the draft teaches you something.

### Drafting with a working thesis

A **working thesis** is your best current answer to the prompt's question. It guides the draft, and you expect to revise it. While drafting, keep moving: if you cannot remember a date or are unsure of a word, leave a bracketed note such as *[check date]* and keep going, then resolve every note before you submit. Stopping to perfect each sentence slows the drafting of ideas, and those sentences may be cut during revision anyway.

### Revision, editing, and proofreading are different jobs

The UNC Writing Center defines revision as literally "to see again": "reconsidering your arguments, reviewing your evidence, refining your purpose, reorganizing your presentation, reviving stale prose." Fixing commas and spelling is **proofreading**. Rewording sentences is **editing**, which UNC calls a part of revision but a final step, because polishing sentences you later delete wastes time.

Work from large to small:

1. **Global revision:** thesis, organization, evidence, audience, and purpose.
2. **Editing:** paragraph transitions, sentence clarity, word choice, and concision.
3. **Proofreading:** grammar, spelling, punctuation, citation format.

Purdue OWL recommends setting a draft aside at least twice during revision, once while reorganizing and once while polishing, so you return with fresh eyes. Reading aloud helps you hear unclear sentences.

### Reverse outlining

A **reverse outline** is an outline made *from* a draft instead of before it. Purdue OWL describes two steps: in the left margin, write the topic of each paragraph in as few words as possible; in the right margin, write how that paragraph advances the overall argument. If you cannot state either in about 5-10 words, the paragraph probably needs work. Reading the margin notes from top to bottom shows repeated points, missing steps, and paragraphs that drift from the thesis.

### Peer review

According to Purdue OWL, the point of peer review "isn't to grade a peer's work, but to offer insight about audience reactions." Reviewers often fall back on commenting on grammar because they are not sure what else to say. Better reviewers read the whole draft once before commenting, ask about the writer's intended audience and plans, explain what prompted each comment, and follow a **describe, evaluate, suggest** pattern (a framework the OWL credits to the writing researchers at Eli Review):

- **Describe** what you understand the passage to be saying or doing.
- **Evaluate** how well it works against the assignment or rubric.
- **Suggest** a specific next step.

As a writer, you decide which feedback to use. Look for patterns: if two readers stumble in the same place, revise there.

## Worked examples

A What Works Clearinghouse practice guide for grades 6-12 recommends teaching writing strategies through a **model, practice, reflect** cycle. The examples below model each strategy; the practice section asks you to try it; the self-check asks you to reflect.

### Example 1: From prompt to working thesis

**Prompt:** *Should students be allowed to retake unit tests? Write an argument for your school's teachers.*

1. **Find the task.** The verb is *argue*, the audience is teachers, and the question is *Should students be allowed to retake unit tests?* The answer must be a position teachers could disagree with.
2. **Freewrite for five minutes** (illustrative student notes, shortened): *retakes feel fair when you were sick... but some people would not study the first time... my math teacher makes you do corrections first... what is the test for, a grade or learning?...*
3. **Journalistic questions** reveal gaps: *Who* retakes? *When*? Is the retake the same test? *Why* do teachers worry? The writer does not know teachers' concerns yet, so that becomes a research and interview task.
4. **Narrow.** The freewrite returns twice to "corrections first," which is more specific than "retakes are good."
5. **Working thesis:** *Teachers should allow one retake per unit test for students who first complete corrections, because the policy keeps the focus on learning while discouraging students from skipping preparation.*

Reasoning: the thesis answers the question, takes a position, names a condition (corrections first), and previews two reasons. It is "working" because the writer still needs to learn teachers' objections; Lesson 12.2 shows how to test and strengthen it.

### Example 2: Diagnosing a draft with a reverse outline

An illustrative five-paragraph draft argues the working thesis above. The writer's reverse outline:

| Paragraph | Left margin: topic | Right margin: how it advances the thesis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Intro, thesis | States position and two reasons |
| 2 | Retakes help sick students | Shows fairness |
| 3 | Students who were absent deserve a chance | Shows fairness (again) |
| 4 | My friend's bad test day | ? |
| 5 | Conclusion | Restates thesis |

Diagnosis and revision decisions:

1. Paragraphs 2 and 3 make the same point. **Decision:** merge them into one paragraph on fairness.
2. Paragraph 4 has no clear right-margin note, and a single story is weak support. **Decision:** cut it or use it briefly as an example inside the fairness paragraph.
3. The thesis promised a second reason, that requiring corrections discourages skipping preparation, but no paragraph develops it. **Decision:** add a paragraph explaining how corrections make a retake take work.
4. Teachers are the audience, and their likely objections (extra grading time, students not studying the first time) are never addressed. **Decision:** add a counterargument paragraph.

Notice that every decision is global. None involves commas. Those come later, after the structure is right.

### Example 3: Revising, then editing, then proofreading one paragraph

**Illustrative draft paragraph:**

> Retakes are good for alot of reasons. Students who are sick can do better, it is not fair to give someone a bad grade for a bad day. Also tests are for learning. In conclusion retakes help.

**Global revision.** The topic sentence is vague ("good for a lot of reasons"), the paragraph mixes two reasons, and "tests are for learning" is stated but never explained. The writer refocuses the paragraph on one reason and explains the link to the thesis:

> A corrections-first retake policy keeps unit tests focused on learning. When students must explain why each missed answer was wrong before retaking, the retake measures what they understand after studying their mistakes, not just whether they had a good morning. A student who skips the corrections does not earn the second attempt, so the policy rewards effort instead of luck.

**Editing.** "Not just whether they had a good morning" is informal for an audience of teachers; the writer changes it to "not just how they performed on a single day."

**Proofreading.** The original's "alot" and the comma splice ("Students who are sick can do better, it is not fair...") disappeared during revision, which shows why proofreading last saves effort.

### Example 4: Turning a vague comment into useful feedback

**Vague comment:** *Good job! Fix your grammar in paragraph 3.*

**Describe, evaluate, suggest:**

> *Describe:* Paragraph 3 says retakes are fair because students can be sick on test day. *Evaluate:* The reason is clear, but it overlaps with paragraph 2, and the assignment asks for reasons teachers will find convincing; fairness to sick students is already handled by most make-up policies. *Suggest:* Could you merge 2 and 3 and use the space to answer the teacher concern about extra grading?

The second comment tells the writer what the reader understood, why it matters for this audience, and what to try next.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Good writers get it right on the first try."** Experienced writers revise heavily. NCTE describes writers developing and refining process skills "throughout their writing lives."
- **"The writing process is five fixed steps in order."** The activities are real, but writers loop between them. A rigid sequence is exactly what NCTE warns against.
- **"Revision means fixing commas and spelling."** That is proofreading. Revision changes ideas, structure, and evidence. UNC compares proofreading a draft with weak ideas to "putting a band-aid on a bullet wound."
- **"Once I outline, I must follow the outline."** An outline is a plan. If drafting reveals a better structure, revise the outline.
- **"Peer review is for catching typos."** Peers are most useful as readers who show you where meaning breaks down. Typos are the easiest problems to find on your own.
- **"My freewrite is my first draft."** Freewriting produces raw material. A draft has a working thesis and an intended order, even if both will change.
- **Boundary: timed writing.** On an in-class or exam essay you cannot set a draft aside for a day. The process still applies, compressed: spend a few minutes understanding the prompt and jotting a scratch outline, draft, and save a few minutes to reread for missing words and unclear sentences.
- **Boundary: different genres need different processes.** NCTE notes that a quick email and a carefully redrafted legal contract involve very different processes. Match the effort to the stakes.

## Practice

1. Label each activity **R** (revising), **E** (editing), or **P** (proofreading): (a) moving the counterargument from the end to the middle; (b) changing "utilize" to "use"; (c) fixing "their" to "there"; (d) replacing a weak example with stronger evidence; (e) combining two choppy sentences; (f) correcting a hanging indent on the Works Cited page.
2. Rephrase each prompt as a question your thesis must answer: (a) *Discuss the role of public libraries in a digital age.* (b) *Analyze how the author of a speech you have read builds trust with the audience.*
3. Use **cubing** on the topic *school start times*: write one sentence for each of the six sides (describe, compare, associate, analyze, apply, argue for and against). Then circle the sentence that could become a thesis.
4. Here is a reverse outline of an illustrative draft arguing that *our town should add protected bike lanes downtown*: P1 intro and thesis (safety and local business); P2 bike lanes and safety; P3 history of bicycles; P4 safety again, with a new example; P5 conclusion. Identify two problems and a revision decision for each.
5. Rewrite this peer comment using describe, evaluate, suggest: *Your intro is boring.*
6. **Extended task.** Choose a draft you have written recently, or freewrite for ten minutes on a prompt your teacher assigns and turn it into a rough draft of at least 300 words.
