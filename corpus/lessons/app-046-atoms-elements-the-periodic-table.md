# APP-04.6 — Atoms, Elements & the Periodic Table

Everything in [APP-04.4 — Matter — Properties & States](/app-044-matter-properties-states) is built from **atoms**. There are only about a hundred kinds of atom, called **elements**, yet they combine to make every substance you have ever touched. This lesson explains what is inside an atom, how the number of protons decides which element it is, how the periodic table is organized, and how to read chemical symbols and formulas.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 7-8.

## Prerequisites

- [APP-04.4 — Matter — Properties & States](/app-044-matter-properties-states): elements, compounds, and mixtures; matter is made of particles.
- The idea that opposite electric charges attract and like charges repel (studied in detail in [APP-04.8 — Electricity & Magnetism](/app-048-electricity-magnetism)).- Subtraction and reading a table.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Describe the three particles in an atom and where each is found.
2. Calculate protons, neutrons, and electrons from the atomic number and mass number.
3. Find periods, groups, metals, nonmetals, and metalloids on the periodic table and predict valence electrons for main-group elements.
4. Count the atoms in a chemical formula.

## Core ideas

### Inside an atom

| Particle | Charge | Where it is | Mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | Nucleus (center) | About 1 atomic mass unit (u) |
| Neutron | 0 | Nucleus | About 1 u |
| Electron | −1 | Around the nucleus | About 0.00055 u (roughly 1,800 electrons equal one proton) |

The **nucleus** is tiny but holds almost all of the atom's mass. The electrons take up almost all of its **volume**. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons, so the charges balance and the net charge is zero.

### Atomic number, mass number, and isotopes

- The **atomic number** ($Z$) is the number of protons. It is the identity of the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon, and every atom with 8 protons is oxygen.
- The **mass number** ($A$) is the number of protons plus neutrons.
- Number of neutrons = $A - Z$.
- **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same $Z$) with different numbers of neutrons (different $A$). Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon.
- The decimal "atomic mass" printed on most periodic tables (carbon's is about 12.01) is an **average** over the element's natural isotopes. It is not the mass number of one atom.

### The periodic table

The periodic table lists the elements in order of **atomic number**. Each box usually shows the atomic number, the symbol, the name, and the average atomic mass.

- The seven horizontal rows are **periods**.
- The 18 vertical columns are **groups**, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties. For example, lithium, sodium, and potassium (group 1) are all shiny, conduct heat and electricity well, and have similar chemical properties.
- **Metals** (most elements, on the left and center) are usually shiny and good conductors of heat and electricity. **Nonmetals** (upper right, plus hydrogen) are usually dull and poor conductors. **Metalloids** lie along the zigzag line between them and have some properties of each.

### Electron shells and valence electrons

A simple model, useful for the first 20 elements, places electrons in **shells** around the nucleus: up to 2 in the first shell, up to 8 in the second, and the next 8 in the third. The electrons in the outermost shell are **valence electrons**. They decide how an atom reacts.

For main-group elements, the group number predicts the valence electrons:

- Groups 1 and 2: 1 and 2 valence electrons.
- Groups 13 to 18: subtract 10 (group 13 → 3, group 17 → 7, group 18 → 8).
- Exception: helium (group 18) has only 2, which fills its single shell.

Group 18 elements, the noble gases, have full outer shells and rarely react. This shell picture is a simplified model; high-school chemistry replaces it with orbitals.

### Symbols and formulas

Each element has a **symbol** of one or two letters. The first letter is always a capital, and a second letter is always lowercase: C is carbon, Ca is calcium, Cl is chlorine. Some symbols come from older names, such as Na (sodium) and Fe (iron).

A **chemical formula** shows the elements in a substance and how many atoms of each. A subscript (small number below the line) counts the atom just before it. No subscript means 1. A subscript after parentheses multiplies everything inside.

Ten elements that appear often in this course:

| Element | Symbol | Atomic number |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H | 1 |
| Helium | He | 2 |
| Carbon | C | 6 |
| Nitrogen | N | 7 |
| Oxygen | O | 8 |
| Sodium | Na | 11 |
| Aluminum | Al | 13 |
| Chlorine | Cl | 17 |
| Calcium | Ca | 20 |
| Iron | Fe | 26 |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Particles in sodium-23

A neutral atom of sodium-23. Sodium's atomic number is 11.

1. Protons = $Z$ = 11.
2. Electrons = protons for a neutral atom = 11.
3. Neutrons = $A - Z = 23 - 11 = 12$.
4. Shells: 11 electrons fill as 2, then 8, then 1 → **2, 8, 1**.
5. One valence electron, which matches sodium's place in group 1.
6. Check: 2 + 8 + 1 = 11 electrons. ✓

### Example 2: Comparing carbon-12 and carbon-14

1. Both are carbon, so both have $Z = 6$: 6 protons and, if neutral, 6 electrons.
2. Carbon-12: neutrons = 12 − 6 = 6.
3. Carbon-14: neutrons = 14 − 6 = 8.
4. They are **isotopes**: same element, same protons, different neutrons. They react almost the same chemically because they have the same electrons.
5. Boundary: if the number of **protons** changed, it would no longer be carbon. Adding a proton to carbon would make nitrogen.

### Example 3: Predicting valence electrons from position

Chlorine is in period 3, group 17.

1. Group 17 is a main group: 17 − 10 = 7 valence electrons.
2. Check with shells: $Z = 17$, so 17 electrons fill as 2, 8, 7. The outer shell has 7. ✓
3. Period 3 matches the electrons using three shells.
4. Prediction: chlorine, like the other group 17 elements, tends to gain one electron when it reacts, reaching a full outer shell of 8.

### Example 4: Counting atoms in formulas

1. $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: H has subscript 2 → 2 hydrogen; O has none → 1 oxygen. Total 3 atoms.
2. $\text{CO}_2$: 1 carbon, 2 oxygen. Total 3 atoms.
3. $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$: the 2 multiplies everything in the parentheses. 1 calcium, 2 × 1 = 2 oxygen, 2 × 1 = 2 hydrogen. Total 5 atoms.
4. Capital-letter check: $\text{CO}$ (carbon monoxide) is two elements, C and O. $\text{Co}$ is one element, cobalt.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Electrons orbit the nucleus like planets around the Sun."** The shell picture is a model. Electrons do not follow neat circular paths; later chemistry describes them with orbitals, regions where an electron is likely to be found.
- **"Changing the neutrons changes the element."** Only the number of protons decides the element. Changing neutrons makes a different isotope of the same element.
- **"The decimal on the periodic table is the number of protons plus neutrons."** That is an average atomic mass. The mass number of a single atom is always a whole number.
- **"Everything in a column is the same kind of element."** Groups share similar properties, but there are exceptions. Hydrogen is usually placed at the top of group 1, yet it is a nonmetal gas, not a metal like lithium and sodium.
- **"Atoms are solid little balls."** Most of an atom's volume is the space occupied by its electrons; the nucleus is a tiny fraction of the atom's size.
- **"An ion has a different number of protons."** An ion has gained or lost **electrons**. A sodium ion, Na⁺, still has 11 protons but only 10 electrons.

## Practice

1. Which particle (proton, neutron, or electron) (a) has no charge (b) has a negative charge (c) decides the element?
2. A neutral atom has atomic number 8 and mass number 16. Name the element, then give its protons, neutrons, and electrons.
3. Oxygen-18 also has atomic number 8. How many neutrons does it have? Is it the same element as question 2?
4. Write the shell arrangement for magnesium ($Z = 12$). How many valence electrons does it have, and which group is it in?
5. Using the group rule, how many valence electrons do nitrogen (group 15) and neon (group 18) have?
6. Count the atoms of each element: (a) $\text{NH}_3$ (b) $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ (c) $\text{Al}_2\text{(SO}_4\text{)}_3$
7. A box on the table shows "Fe" and "26." What do the letters and number mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. (a) neutron (b) electron (c) proton
2. Oxygen. 8 protons, 16 − 8 = 8 neutrons, 8 electrons.
3. 18 − 8 = 10 neutrons. Yes, it is still oxygen (an isotope), because it still has 8 protons.
4. 2, 8, 2. Two valence electrons; group 2.
5. Nitrogen: 15 − 10 = 5. Neon: 18 − 10 = 8.
6. (a) 1 N, 3 H (b) 6 C, 12 H, 6 O (c) 2 Al, 3 S, 12 O (the 3 multiplies the 1 S and the 4 O inside the parentheses)
7. Fe is the symbol for iron. 26 is the atomic number: every iron atom has 26 protons.

**Self-check:** For every neutral atom, protons should equal electrons, and protons + neutrons should equal the mass number. For shell arrangements, the shells should add up to the atomic number and none should exceed its limit (2, 8, 8).

</details>
