# APP-03.5 — Cells — The Basic Unit of Life

Every living thing you have studied so far, from a dandelion to a heron, is made of **cells**. A cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life. Some organisms, like bacteria, are a single cell. You are made of trillions. Most cells are far too small to see without a microscope: a human red blood cell is about 8 micrometers across, so about 250 of them would fit side by side across a 2-millimeter pinhead. This lesson covers how scientists discovered cells, the two main kinds of cells, the structures inside them, and the two processes that move energy through them.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 6-7.

## Core ideas

### Discovering cells and the cell theory

- In **1665**, Robert Hooke published *Micrographia*. Looking at thin slices of cork through a lens, he saw tiny box-like spaces and called them "cells."
- In the **1670s**, Antony van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch shopkeeper who ground his own lenses, saw moving single-celled organisms he called "animalcules," and discovered bacteria and protozoa.
- By the **late 1830s**, botanist Matthias Schleiden and zoologist Theodor Schwann, studying plant and animal tissues, proposed the unified **cell theory**. Rudolf Virchow later made important contributions to the theory.

The **cell theory** has three parts:

1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
3. New cells come only from existing cells.

### Microscopes and cell size

A **light microscope** passes light through a thin specimen and two sets of lenses. Student microscopes typically magnify up to about **400 times**. Total magnification is the eyepiece power multiplied by the objective power. The image appears upside down and backward. Most cells are nearly transparent, so they are often stained to show their parts, but staining usually kills the cells.

| Thing | Typical size |
|---|---|
| Pinhead | about 2 mm = 2,000 μm |
| Eukaryotic cells (plants, animals, fungi, protists) | about 10–100 μm |
| Human red blood cell | about 8 μm |
| Prokaryotic cells (bacteria, archaea) | about 0.1–5.0 μm |

(1 μm, a **micrometer**, is one-millionth of a meter, or one-thousandth of a millimeter.)

Why are cells so small? Materials enter and leave a cell through its surface, but the cell's needs depend on its volume. As a cell gets bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so its **surface-area-to-volume ratio** drops. A cell that grew too large could not move materials in and out fast enough.

### What every cell has

All cells share four parts:

1. A **plasma membrane** (cell membrane) that separates the inside from the outside and controls what enters and leaves.
2. **Cytoplasm**, the jelly-like fluid and everything inside the membrane.
3. **DNA**, the cell's genetic instructions.
4. **Ribosomes**, which make proteins.

### Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

| | **Prokaryotic cells** | **Eukaryotic cells** |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | No; DNA sits in the cytoplasm | Yes; DNA is inside a nucleus |
| Membrane-bound organelles | No | Yes (mitochondria, and in plants chloroplasts, and others) |
| Typical size | 0.1–5.0 μm | 10–100 μm |
| Examples | Bacteria and archaea | Animals, plants, fungi, protists |

The names describe the nucleus: *pro-* means "before," *eu-* means "true," and *-karyon* refers to the nucleus.

### Organelles and their jobs

| Organelle | Function | Found in |
|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | Holds the DNA and directs the cell's activities | plant and animal cells |
| **Cell membrane** | Controls what moves in and out | all cells |
| **Ribosomes** | Make proteins | all cells |
| **Mitochondria** | Release energy from food by cellular respiration, making ATP | plant and animal cells |
| **Chloroplasts** | Capture light energy and carry out photosynthesis | plant cells (and algae) |
| **Cell wall** | Rigid outer layer that protects the cell and gives it shape | plant cells, fungi, most bacteria |
| **Large central vacuole** | Stores water and regulates the cell's water; when it shrinks, the plant wilts | plant cells |
| **Lysosomes** | Break down large molecules and worn-out organelles with digestive enzymes | animal cells (plants do this in vacuoles) |

Cells that make many proteins, such as the pancreas cells that produce digestive enzymes, contain especially many ribosomes. This is an example of a pattern in biology: **structure matches function**.

### Plant cells and animal cells

Plant cells have a **cell wall**, **chloroplasts**, and a **large central vacuole**; animal cells do not. Animal cells have **lysosomes** and a **centrosome**; most plant cells do not. Under a microscope, plant cells often look like neat bricks because of their walls, while animal cells have more rounded or irregular shapes. **Both** plant and animal cells have a nucleus, cell membrane, ribosomes, and mitochondria.

### Photosynthesis and cellular respiration

**Photosynthesis** happens in chloroplasts:

> carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose (a sugar) + oxygen
>
> 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

**Cellular respiration** happens in mitochondria, in plants and in animals:

> glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + usable energy (ATP)
>
> C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

The carbon dioxide you breathe out was made in your mitochondria. The two processes are nearly the reverse of each other: one stores the Sun's energy in sugar, and the other releases that energy for the cell to use.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Microscope magnification

A microscope has a 10× eyepiece and a 40× objective lens.

1. Total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 10 × 40 = **400×**.
2. A red blood cell is about 8 μm across. Its image appears 8 × 400 = 3,200 μm across.
3. Convert: 3,200 μm ÷ 1,000 = **3.2 mm**, large enough to see clearly.
4. Check the 4× objective: 10 × 4 = 40×, so the cell's image would be 8 × 40 = 320 μm = 0.32 mm, too small to see much detail. That is why you switch to higher power.

### Example 2: How many cells fit across a pinhead?

1. Pinhead: 2 mm. Convert: 2 × 1,000 = 2,000 μm.
2. Red blood cell: 8 μm.
3. Number side by side: 2,000 ÷ 8 = **250 cells**.
4. A typical bacterium of 2 μm: 2,000 ÷ 2 = 1,000 bacteria across the same pinhead.

### Example 3: Why cells stay small

Model cells as cubes. For a cube with side *s*: surface area = 6 × *s* × *s*; volume = *s* × *s* × *s*.

| Side | Surface area | Volume | Surface area ÷ volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 μm | 6 μm² | 1 μm³ | 6 |
| 2 μm | 24 μm² | 8 μm³ | 3 |
| 4 μm | 96 μm² | 64 μm³ | 1.5 |

1. Each time the side doubles, the surface area multiplies by 4 but the volume multiplies by 8.
2. So the ratio halves: 6 → 3 → 1.5.
3. Meaning: the bigger cube has less surface for each unit of volume, so supplies would reach its center more slowly. Large organisms are made of many small cells instead of a few huge ones.

### Example 4: Identifying a cell

1. **Cell A** has DNA, ribosomes, a cell wall, and no nucleus. No nucleus means **prokaryote**, probably a bacterium.
2. **Cell B** has a nucleus, a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole. Nucleus means eukaryote; wall plus chloroplasts plus central vacuole means **plant cell**.
3. **Cell C** has a nucleus, mitochondria, and lysosomes, but no cell wall or chloroplasts. **Animal cell.**
4. **Cell D** has a nucleus and a cell wall but no chloroplasts. Do not jump to "plant": fungi also have cell walls and no chloroplasts. You would need more evidence, such as what the wall is made of or where the sample came from.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Plants do photosynthesis instead of respiration."** Plants do both. Their mitochondria carry out cellular respiration day and night, using the sugar their chloroplasts make.
- **"Plant cells have a cell wall instead of a cell membrane."** Plant cells have both. The wall is outside the membrane; the membrane still controls what enters and leaves.
- **"Any cell with a cell wall is a plant cell."** Fungi and most bacteria also have cell walls. Chloroplasts and a large central vacuole are better clues for a plant cell.
- **"All cells look like the flat, round diagrams in textbooks."** Cells come in many shapes that match their jobs, such as long nerve cells or disk-shaped red blood cells, and they are three-dimensional.
- **"All bacteria are harmful."** Most microbes are harmless or helpful to people. Only some cause disease.
- **"Viruses are the smallest cells."** Viruses are not made of cells at all. To make new viruses, they must get inside a living cell and use its machinery.

## Practice

1. List the three parts of the cell theory.
2. What did Hooke see in 1665, and what did van Leeuwenhoek discover in the 1670s?
3. A microscope has a 10× eyepiece and a 10× objective. What is the total magnification?
4. Convert 0.05 mm to micrometers. Is a cell this size more likely prokaryotic or eukaryotic?
5. Name three structures found in plant cells but not animal cells.
6. Which organelle releases energy from glucose, and is it found in plant cells, animal cells, or both?
7. A cube-shaped cell has sides of 3 μm. Find its surface area, its volume, and its surface-area-to-volume ratio.
8. Write the word equation for photosynthesis. Then name one product of photosynthesis that is used in cellular respiration.

> **Safety note:** *Activity to try, with an adult:* peel the thin, clear skin from inside an onion layer, lay it flat in a drop of water on a slide, and add a cover slip. If you use iodine or another stain, an adult should handle it while you both wear goggles and gloves; stains are harmful if swallowed and stain skin and clothes.
