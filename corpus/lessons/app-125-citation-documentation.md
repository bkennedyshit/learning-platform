# APP-12.5 — Citation & Documentation

**Documentation** is the system that tells a reader exactly where your borrowed words, ideas, and data came from. It has two parts in every major style: a short **in-text citation** at the point where you use a source, and a full entry in a list at the end (or in a note) that lets the reader find the source. Citation gives credit, lets readers check your evidence, and separates your thinking from other people's.

This lesson teaches three styles you will meet in high school and college: **MLA** (Modern Language Association, 9th edition, 2021), common in English and other language and literature courses; **APA** (American Psychological Association, 7th edition), common in psychology and the social sciences; and **Chicago** (*The Chicago Manual of Style*, 18th edition, 2024), which offers a notes-and-bibliography system favored in history and the arts and an author-date system used in many sciences and social sciences. Always follow the style your teacher assigns.

[APP-12.4 — Research Skills & Source Evaluation](/app-124-research-skills-source-evaluation): you should keep a source log with full publication information. ## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Decide what needs a citation and what counts as **common knowledge**.
2. Write **MLA** in-text citations and Works Cited entries for a journal article, a book, and a web page.
3. Write **APA** in-text citations and reference entries for the same sources.
4. Write **Chicago** notes, shortened notes, and bibliography entries, and recognize Chicago author-date.

## Core ideas

### What needs a citation

Cite whenever you use:

- an exact **quotation**;
- a **paraphrase** or **summary** of someone else's ideas;
- specific **facts, statistics, or data** that are not common knowledge;
- **images, charts, tables**, or media you did not create;
- another person's **argument, interpretation, or method**;
- content produced by a **generative AI tool** that you quote, paraphrase, or incorporate (MLA Style Center guidance).

You do not need to cite **common knowledge** (facts widely known and easily verified, such as the year World War II ended) or your own original ideas and observations. When unsure, the UNC Writing Center's student test helps: if you did not know it before the course, or it did not come from your own brain, cite it.

### The three styles at a glance

| | MLA 9 | APA 7 | Chicago 18 notes-bibliography |
|---|---|---|---|
| In text | (Author page): *(Graham and Perin 450)* | (Author, year): *(Graham & Perin, 2007)*; add *p.* for quotations | superscript number linked to a footnote or endnote |
| End list | Works Cited | References | Bibliography |
| Author names in list | Last, First | Last, Initials | Last, First |
| Date position | near the end | right after the author | near the end (year in parentheses for journals in notes) |
| Article titles | Title Case, in quotation marks | Sentence case, no quotation marks | Title Case, in quotation marks |
| Book and journal titles | *italics*, Title Case | *italics*; books in sentence case, journals in Title Case | *italics*, Title Case |

All three use a **hanging indent** (every line after the first indented) in the final list and alphabetize it by the first word of each entry, usually the first author's last name.

### MLA 9: the core elements

MLA builds every Works Cited entry from **core elements** in order, skipping any that do not apply:

> Author. Title of source. Title of container, Other contributors, Version, Number, Publisher, Publication date, Location.

The **container** is the larger work that holds the source: a journal holds an article, a website holds a page. MLA uses the DOI as a full link beginning *https://doi.org/* but removes *https://* from ordinary URLs. In-text citations give the author's last name and page number with no comma: *(Graham and Perin 450)*. For three or more authors, use the first author plus *et al.*

### APA 7: author and date first

APA reference entries begin with the author's last name and initials, then the year in parentheses. Article and book titles are in **sentence case** (capitalize only the first word, the first word after a colon, and proper nouns). Journal names and volume numbers are italicized; the issue number follows in parentheses without italics. List up to 20 authors, with an ampersand (&) before the last. Include a DOI as a link whenever one exists.

In-text citations take two forms:

- **Parenthetical:** *(Graham & Perin, 2007)*, using & inside parentheses.
- **Narrative:** *Graham and Perin (2007) found...*, using *and* in the sentence.

For **three or more authors**, use the first author and *et al.* in every citation, even the first: *(Clark et al., 2018)*. For quotations, add a page (*p. 112*) or, for sources without pages, a section name or paragraph number (*para. 4*). If there is no date, use *n.d.*

### Chicago 18: notes and bibliography

In the **notes-and-bibliography** system, a superscript number in your sentence points to a numbered **footnote** or **endnote**. The first note for a source gives full information; later notes for the same source use a **shortened note** (author's last name, short title, page). A **bibliography** at the end lists sources alphabetically. In the 18th edition, Chicago says a place of publication "is no longer required in book citations." For three or more authors, a note lists the first author followed by *et al.*; the bibliography lists up to six.

Chicago's **author-date** system instead uses parenthetical citations such as *(Graham and Perin 2007, 450)* and a reference list with the year right after the author.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: One journal article in four formats

**Source facts** (from the article's Crossref metadata record): authors Steve Graham and Dolores Perin; article "A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students"; *Journal of Educational Psychology*, volume 99, issue 3, 2007, pages 445-476; DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445. Suppose you paraphrase an idea from page 450 (an illustrative page number for practice).

**MLA 9**

1. Two authors: first author inverted, second in normal order, joined by *and*.
2. Article title in quotation marks and Title Case; journal in italics as the container.
3. *vol.*, *no.*, year, *pp.*, then the DOI as the location. MLA shortens the second page number when the hundreds match: *445-76*.

> Graham, Steve, and Dolores Perin. "A Meta-Analysis of Writing Instruction for Adolescent Students." *Journal of Educational Psychology*, vol. 99, no. 3, 2007, pp. 445-76, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445.

In text: *(Graham and Perin 450)*.

**APA 7**

1. Last names and initials, & before the last author, year in parentheses.
2. Article title in sentence case, no quotation marks.
3. Journal name and volume in italics, issue in parentheses, full page range, DOI.

> Graham, S., & Perin, D. (2007). A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, *99*(3), 445–476. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445

In text: *(Graham & Perin, 2007)* for a paraphrase; *(Graham & Perin, 2007, p. 450)* for a quotation.

**Chicago 18, notes and bibliography**

> 1. Steve Graham and Dolores Perin, "A Meta-Analysis of Writing Instruction for Adolescent Students," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 99, no. 3 (2007): 450, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445.
>
> 2. Graham and Perin, "Meta-Analysis of Writing Instruction," 452.

> Graham, Steve, and Dolores Perin. "A Meta-Analysis of Writing Instruction for Adolescent Students." *Journal of Educational Psychology* 99, no. 3 (2007): 445–76. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445.

Notice: the note uses commas and the specific page; the bibliography uses periods and the full page range; the shortened note drops the opening *A* and shortens the title.

**Chicago 18, author-date**

> Graham, Steve, and Dolores Perin. 2007. "A Meta-Analysis of Writing Instruction for Adolescent Students." *Journal of Educational Psychology* 99 (3): 445–76. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445.

In text: *(Graham and Perin 2007, 450)*.

### Example 2: A book with three authors

**Source facts:** *Biology 2e* by Mary Ann Clark, Matthew Douglas, and Jung Choi, published by OpenStax in 2018. Suppose you quote page 112 of the PDF edition (an illustrative page). If you read the online version, add its URL as the last element in each style.

> **MLA:** Clark, Mary Ann, et al. *Biology 2e*. OpenStax, 2018.
> In text: *(Clark et al. 112)*.

> **APA:** Clark, M. A., Douglas, M., & Choi, J. (2018). *Biology 2e*. OpenStax.
> In text: *(Clark et al., 2018, p. 112)*.

> **Chicago note:** 1. Mary Ann Clark et al., *Biology 2e* (OpenStax, 2018), 112.
> **Chicago bibliography:** Clark, Mary Ann, Matthew Douglas, and Jung Choi. *Biology 2e*. OpenStax, 2018.

Reasoning: MLA and Chicago notes shorten three or more authors to *et al.*; APA's reference list and Chicago's bibliography list all three; APA uses *et al.* only in text.

### Example 3: A web page with no named author and no date

**Source facts:** the page "Monarch Butterfly Biology" on the USDA Forest Service website; no individual author or date shown.

**MLA.** The organization is also the website's publisher, so MLA starts with the title and lists the site once as the container. Because no date is shown, add the date you accessed it.

> "Monarch Butterfly Biology." *USDA Forest Service*, www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/Monarch_Butterfly/biology/index.shtml. Accessed 19 Oct. 2026.

In text: *("Monarch Butterfly Biology")*. Web pages have no page numbers, so none is given.

**APA.** APA treats the organization as the group author. When the author and site name are the same, the site name is omitted. Use *n.d.* for no date.

> USDA Forest Service. (n.d.). *Monarch butterfly biology*. https://www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/Monarch_Butterfly/biology/index.shtml

In text: *(USDA Forest Service, n.d.)*.

### Example 4: Citing generative AI in MLA

The MLA Style Center recommends **not** treating an AI tool as an author. Instead, describe what was generated (often the prompt) in the title position, name the tool as the container, and give its version, the company, the date the content was generated, and the tool's general URL. Here is the pattern with placeholders, modeled on the MLA Style Center's examples:

> "Explain the difference between revising and editing" prompt. *Name of AI tool*, version, Company, Day Month Year generated, general URL of the tool.

In text, use a short form of the title: *("Explain the difference")*. MLA also advises acknowledging functional uses, such as editing your prose or translating, in a note or your text, and checking any sources the tool cites before you rely on them. APA Style publishes separate guidance, linked in the sources. Your teacher's policy on whether AI may be used at all comes first.

### Example 5: Finding errors in a Works Cited list

**Draft (MLA):**

> Perin, Dolores and Steve Graham. A Meta-Analysis of Writing Instruction for Adolescent Students. Journal of Educational Psychology, 2007.
> Biology 2e. Clark et al. OpenStax.

Corrections and reasons:

1. The authors must appear in the order printed on the article: Graham first.
2. A comma is needed after the first author's first name before *and*: *Graham, Steve, and Dolores Perin.*
3. The article title needs quotation marks; the journal title needs italics.
4. Volume, issue, pages, and DOI are missing.
5. The book entry starts with the author, not the title, and the author's full name is needed: *Clark, Mary Ann, et al.*; the date is missing.
6. Alphabetical order: *Clark* comes before *Graham*.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"If I paraphrase, I don't need to cite."** Paraphrases and summaries always need citations.
- **"A citation generator is always right."** Generators and citation managers save time, but they often miscapitalize titles, drop issue numbers, or mislabel source types. Check every entry against the style guide.
- **"Every sentence with source information needs its own citation."** Cite once where the borrowed material appears and again when you return to that source after citing another one. Make clear in your sentences where your own ideas begin.
- **"APA and MLA are basically the same."** They differ in name format, date placement, capitalization, and in-text form. Mixing them in one paper is a common, visible error.
- **"Websites can't be cited because they have no author."** Start with the title (MLA) or the organization as group author (APA), and use *n.d.* or an access date as each style directs.
- **"An AI tool can be listed as an author."** MLA advises against it. Describe the generated content and name the tool as the container.
- **Boundary: common knowledge varies by audience.** What is common knowledge to specialists may not be to your readers or to you. When in doubt, cite.
- **Boundary: style editions change.** Rules shown here follow MLA 9, APA 7, and Chicago 18. Earlier editions differ (for example, older Chicago book citations included the city of publication), so check which edition your teacher expects.

## Practice

1. Cite or not? (a) a statistic on teen sleep from a health agency; (b) *The Declaration of Independence was adopted in 1776*; (c) a classmate's idea from a discussion that you build on; (d) a chart you copied from a website; (e) your own observation from a lab you ran.
2. Write the APA in-text citation for a paraphrase from a 2021 article by four authors whose first author is Ana Torres.
3. Convert this APA reference to MLA:

    > Graham, S., & Perin, D. (2007). A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, *99*(3), 445–476. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445
4. Put these MLA entries in Works Cited order: *"Monarch Butterfly Biology."...* / *Graham, Steve, and Dolores Perin...* / *Clark, Mary Ann, et al...*
5. What is wrong with this MLA in-text citation for a web page: *(USDA Forest Service, p. 2)*?
6. Write a Chicago shortened note for page 30 of *Biology 2e* after a full note has already been given.
7. **Extended task.** Choose three sources from your research log (one journal article, one book, one web page). Write a Works Cited list (MLA) or References list (APA), plus one in-text citation for each. Check your work with the checklist.

**Citation checklist rubric** (1 point each; 10 points total):

1. Every in-text citation matches exactly one list entry, and every entry is cited in the text.
2. Author names are in the correct order and format for the style.
3. Titles use the style's capitalization (Title Case or sentence case).
4. Italics and quotation marks are used for the right titles.
5. The date is in the correct position.
6. Journal entries include volume, issue, and pages.
7. DOIs are given as *https://doi.org/* links when available.

> **Academic integrity note:** Citation errors and plagiarism are different things. A missing comma is a formatting mistake; presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own is plagiarism. If you are unsure how to cite something, ask your teacher or librarian before the deadline, and never invent a source, page number, or quotation to fill a gap.
