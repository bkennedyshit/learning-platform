# APP-06.7 — Economic Geography

Why are some places wealthy and others poor? Why are cars assembled in one country from parts made in a dozen others? Why does a phone, a T-shirt, or a cup of coffee travel thousands of kilometers before it reaches you? Economic geography studies where economic activity happens, how places are connected by trade, and how wealth and well-being vary across the world.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its thinking skill is **choosing and questioning indicators**: every measure of "development" captures some things and hides others, so you will calculate several and compare what they show. This lesson is educational only and does not give financial or investment advice.

## Prerequisites

- [APP-06.6 — Political Geography](/app-066-political-geography) (states and international organizations) and [APP-06.4 — Human Geography — Population & Migration](/app-064-human-geography-population-migration) (rates per 1,000 and per capita).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Compare **traditional, command, market, and mixed economies**, and classify economic activities into **primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary sectors**.
2. Calculate and interpret **development indicators**, including GDP and GNI per capita, purchasing power parity, infant mortality, and the Human Development Index, and classify economies with the World Bank's current **income thresholds**.
3. Explain **absolute and comparative advantage** with a numerical example, and describe how trade and **globalization** connect places.
4. Evaluate broad generalizations and models such as the **Global North and South** and **Rostow's stages of growth**, using evidence of variation within and between countries.

## Core ideas

### Economic systems

The OpenStax textbook *Principles of Economics* describes three ways societies organize an economy:

| System | Who decides what to produce and how | Example (OpenStax) |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Custom: people produce as their families always have; occupations stay in the family | The oldest system; OpenStax says it is still used in parts of Asia, Africa, and South America |
| **Command** | A ruler or government sets production, prices, and wages | Ancient Egypt; medieval manors; today, Cuba and North Korea |
| **Market** | Decentralized decisions by private individuals and businesses responding to demand | Markets bring buyers and sellers together |

OpenStax stresses that "most economies in the real world are **mixed**," combining command and market (and even traditional) elements. The U.S. economy is toward the market-oriented end of the spectrum.

### Economic sectors

Geographers sort jobs and businesses into sectors:

- **Primary:** taking resources directly from the Earth: farming, fishing, forestry, mining.
- **Secondary:** processing and manufacturing: turning wheat into bread or ore into steel.
- **Tertiary:** services: retail, transportation, health care, teaching.
- **Quaternary:** knowledge and information services: research, software, finance. (Some geographers include these in the tertiary sector.)

As economies develop, the share of workers in the primary sector usually falls and the share in services rises.

### Measuring an economy

- **Gross domestic product (GDP)** is "the value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given year" (OpenStax).
- **Gross national income (GNI)** counts the income earned by a country's residents, including income from abroad. The World Bank uses GNI per capita, converted to U.S. dollars by its "Atlas method," to classify economies.
- **Per capita** means per person: total ÷ population. A large economy can have a low per capita income if it has a large population.
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** adjusts for differences in the cost of living, so that comparisons show what incomes can actually buy. The open textbook *Introduction to Human Geography* (Dorrell and Henderson) compares the same salary in New York City and Lincoln, Nebraska: most items are cheaper in Lincoln, so the salary is worth more there.

### World Bank income groups (fiscal year 2027)

The World Bank classifies economies each July 1 based on the previous year's GNI per capita. For its current fiscal year (2027), based on 2025 data:

| Income group | GNI per capita (Atlas method, 2025) |
|---|---|
| Low income | 1,175 U.S. dollars or less |
| Lower-middle income | 1,176 to 4,635 U.S. dollars |
| Upper-middle income | 4,636 to 14,375 U.S. dollars |
| High income | more than 14,375 U.S. dollars |

The World Bank notes that its word "country" refers to any territory that reports separate economic statistics and "does not imply political independence."

### Beyond income: well-being indicators

- **Infant mortality rate:** deaths of infants under one year old per 1,000 live births. It is sensitive to health care, nutrition, clean water, and sanitation.
- **Human Development Index (HDI).** The UN Development Programme (UNDP) created the HDI "to emphasize that people and their capabilities should be the ultimate criteria for assessing the development of a country, not economic growth alone." It combines three dimensions:
  - a long and healthy life, measured by **life expectancy at birth**;
  - knowledge, measured by **mean years of schooling** for adults 25 and older and **expected years of schooling** for children;
  - a decent standard of living, measured by **GNI per capita**, using its logarithm "to reflect the diminishing importance of income with increasing GNI."

  Each dimension is turned into an index from 0 to 1, and the HDI is their **geometric mean**.

### Trade, comparative advantage, and globalization

**Absolute advantage** means a country can produce more of a good with the same resources. OpenStax gives geographic examples: oil in Saudi Arabia is relatively easy to extract; Chile has copper and Guatemala grows coffee. But OpenStax stresses that "trade really occurs because of **comparative advantage**": a country has a comparative advantage when it can produce a good "at a lower cost in terms of other goods." The cost that matters is the **opportunity cost**, what you give up to make something else. The economist David Ricardo argued in 1817 that specialization and trade can benefit all partners, even relatively inefficient ones.

**Globalization**, in National Geographic's definition, is "the increasing connectedness and interdependence of world cultures and economies." Today a single product may be designed in one country, made from materials mined in several others, assembled in another, and sold worldwide. Such **global supply chains** create jobs and lower prices in many places, and they also mean that a disruption in one region can affect others.

### Generalizations and models: use with care

- **Global North and South.** Wealthier countries tend to be in the "Global North" (North America, Europe, Japan, Australia, New Zealand) and poorer ones in the "Global South," sometimes separated on maps by the "Brandt line." Dorrell and Henderson warn that "such generalizations are problematic." Mexico, for example, lies south of the Brandt line, yet its per capita income places it in the top third of all countries and its economy is among the largest in the world.
- **Rostow's stages of growth (1960).** W. W. Rostow argued that countries follow a predictable path through five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and an age of high mass consumption. Critics, as Dorrell and Henderson summarize, note that the model "doesn't account for geographic differences, historical variations, or the long-standing effects of colonialism," and some see it as more political than descriptive.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Per capita income and purchasing power

**Problem (hypothetical country):** Country M has a GDP of 500 billion U.S. dollars and a population of 25 million. Its prices for a standard basket of goods average 60 percent of U.S. prices. Find GDP per capita, and estimate GDP per capita at PPP.

1. **Per capita:** 500,000,000,000 ÷ 25,000,000 = **20,000 dollars per person**.
2. **Adjust for prices:** if goods cost 60 percent as much, each dollar buys 1 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 1.67 times as much as in the United States.
3. **PPP estimate:** 20,000 ÷ 0.6 ≈ **33,300 dollars per person** in U.S.-price terms.
4. **Interpret:** at market exchange rates, people in M look poorer than their actual buying power suggests. PPP gives a fairer comparison of living standards.
5. **Limit the claim:** an average per person says nothing about how income is **distributed**; a few very rich people can raise the average while most people earn far less.

### Example 2: Classifying income groups

**Problem:** Using the World Bank's FY2027 thresholds, classify economies with 2025 GNI per capita of (a) 900; (b) 4,700; (c) exactly 14,375; and (d) 30,000 U.S. dollars.

1. **(a) 900:** at or below 1,175, so **low income**.
2. **(b) 4,700:** above 4,635 but not above 14,375, so **upper-middle income**.
3. **(c) 14,375:** high income requires *more than* 14,375, so exactly 14,375 is still **upper-middle income**. Edge cases depend on the exact wording.
4. **(d) 30,000:** **high income**.
5. **Note:** the thresholds are revised every year, so an economy's group can change without any change in its own income.

### Example 3: Why the HDI uses a geometric mean

**Problem:** Country A has three HDI dimension indices of 0.80, 0.80, and 0.80. Country B has 0.95 (health), 0.95 (education), and 0.50 (income). Compare their arithmetic means and geometric means.

1. **Arithmetic means:** A: (0.80 + 0.80 + 0.80) ÷ 3 = **0.80**. B: (0.95 + 0.95 + 0.50) ÷ 3 = **0.80**. They look identical.
2. **Geometric means:** A: cube root of (0.80 × 0.80 × 0.80) = **0.80**. B: cube root of (0.95 × 0.95 × 0.50) = cube root of 0.451 ≈ **0.77**.
3. **Interpret:** the geometric mean gives B a lower score because one dimension lags far behind. A high score in one dimension cannot fully make up for a low score in another.
4. **Connect to UNDP's purpose:** this design matches UNDP's view that development means progress across health, education, and income together, not growth alone.

### Example 4: Comparative advantage with numbers

**Problem (hypothetical):** In one day, a worker in Country A can make 10 shirts or 5 bushels of wheat. A worker in Country B can make 4 shirts or 4 bushels. Which country has the absolute advantage in each good? Which has the comparative advantage?

1. **Absolute advantage:** A makes more of both (10 > 4 shirts; 5 > 4 bushels), so A has the absolute advantage in **both**.
2. **Opportunity cost of 1 bushel of wheat:** A gives up 10 ÷ 5 = **2 shirts**; B gives up 4 ÷ 4 = **1 shirt**. B's cost is lower, so **B has the comparative advantage in wheat**.
3. **Opportunity cost of 1 shirt:** A gives up 5 ÷ 10 = **0.5 bushel**; B gives up 4 ÷ 4 = **1 bushel**. A's cost is lower, so **A has the comparative advantage in shirts**.
4. **Conclusion:** if A specializes in shirts and B in wheat and they trade at a price between their opportunity costs (for example, 1 bushel for 1.5 shirts), both can consume more than if each made everything itself. This is Ricardo's insight.
5. **Limit the claim:** the model leaves out transport costs, the workers who lose jobs when production shifts, and differences in bargaining power, all of which matter in real trade.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A country's GDP tells you how well off its people are."** Total GDP depends heavily on population size. Per capita figures, PPP, and well-being indicators such as the HDI and infant mortality tell different parts of the story.
- **"Average income describes a typical person."** Averages hide inequality within countries and between regions, such as cities and rural areas.
- **"Countries trade only when one is better at everything."** Trade depends on comparative advantage, so even a country with an absolute advantage in everything can gain from trade.
- **"Development follows one path, the same for every country."** Rostow's model is one view, and critics point to colonialism, geography, and history as reasons paths differ.
- **"The Global South is uniformly poor."** The category hides great variation; Dorrell and Henderson's Mexico example shows a country "south" of the Brandt line in the top third by per capita income.
- **"Economic systems are pure."** Most real economies are mixed, combining market and government decisions.
- **Boundary: classifications are conventions.** The World Bank's thresholds are revised annually, and "country" in its lists includes territories that report separate statistics.

## Practice

1. Classify each job by sector (primary, secondary, tertiary, or quaternary): (a) coal miner; (b) auto-assembly worker; (c) nurse; (d) software researcher; (e) commercial fisher.
2. A country has a GDP of 12 billion U.S. dollars and a population of 8 million. Find GDP per capita. If its prices are half of U.S. prices, estimate GDP per capita at PPP.
3. Classify economies with 2025 GNI per capita of (a) 1,175; (b) 1,176; (c) 14,376 U.S. dollars, using the FY2027 thresholds.
4. Country C has HDI dimension indices of 0.90, 0.70, and 0.60. Find the arithmetic mean and the geometric mean, and explain why they differ.
5. In one hour, a worker in Country X makes 6 tables or 12 chairs; in Country Y, 2 tables or 8 chairs. Find each country's opportunity cost of one table, and decide which country has the comparative advantage in tables.
6. Name two things the HDI includes that GDP per capita does not, and one thing neither measures.
7. Explain one criticism of Rostow's stages of growth, and suggest one piece of evidence a geographer could use to test the model against a real country.
8. **Suggested activity: indicator comparison (pairs, 30–40 minutes).** If your school allows it, use the World Bank's open data site or UNDP's Human Development Reports data center to look up, for two countries in different income groups, GNI per capita, life expectancy, and the HDI. Record the year and source of each figure. Write three sentences comparing what each indicator shows and one question the indicators cannot answer.
