# 59.7 - Editing — The Real Work

A first draft gets ideas onto the page; editing turns them into something a reader can use. **Revision** literally means to "see again," as the UNC Writing Center puts it: "an ongoing process of rethinking the paper: reconsidering your arguments, reviewing your evidence, refining your purpose, reorganizing your presentation, reviving stale prose." **Editing** is the rereading that checks content, structure, clarity, and style; **proofreading** is "the final stage," focused on surface errors such as spelling, grammar, and punctuation. Most weak final drafts come from doing these in the wrong order: polishing sentences in paragraphs that should have been cut.

This lesson is for college students and professionals revising papers, reports, proposals, or articles, alone or with a peer reviewer. It gathers the sentence-level moves from earlier lessons into a complete process.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Edit **higher-order concerns** before **lower-order concerns**, and know when to depart from that order.
2. Build and use a **reverse outline**.
3. Apply the **Paramedic Method** and report word counts.
4. **Proofread** systematically and explain the limits of checkers.

## Core ideas

### Higher-order and lower-order concerns

Purdue OWL divides revision priorities into **higher-order concerns (HOCs)**, the "big picture" elements "such as thesis or focus, audience and purpose, organization, and development," and **lower-order concerns (LOCs)** "such as sentence structure and grammar." Address HOCs first, because fixing a sentence that will later be deleted wastes time. OWL adds two qualifications. Experienced writers often move between the two naturally. And sometimes LOCs come first: "if the first sentence of your introductory paragraph is riddled with punctuation and spelling errors, readers may not move far enough into your work to get to your thesis statement."

A practical sequence of passes:

| Pass | Questions | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Purpose and claim | Does the draft answer the assignment or need? Can you state the main point in one sentence? Do you still agree with it? | one-sentence summary; reread the prompt |
| 2. Structure | Is each paragraph doing one job, in the best order? What's missing or repeated? | reverse outline |
| 3. Paragraphs | Does each have a clear topic sentence and stay on it? Are transitions doing work? | topic-sentence list |
| 4. Sentences | Actors and actions, voice, concision, rhythm (Lessons 59.1-59.2) | Paramedic Method; reading aloud |
| 5. Proofreading | Spelling, punctuation, formatting, citations | one error type per pass |

UNC's revising handout quotes *The Scott, Foresman Handbook for Writers*: "THINK BIG, don't tinker." It also warns that proofreading a draft with a weak thesis and messy organization is "putting a band-aid on a bullet wound."

### Distance: the cold read

You cannot see a draft clearly right after writing it, because you read what you meant rather than what you wrote. UNC's editing handout says to put a paper aside "for a few hours, days, or weeks," or give it to a friend: "you can't get much more distance than that." Changing the document's font, size, or spacing can also help you see it as unfamiliar. UNC's revising handout notes, with a wink, that "the Roman poet Horace thought one should wait nine years, but that's a bit much. A day—a few hours even—will work."

### The reverse outline

A **reverse outline** is an outline of what you actually wrote, made after drafting. UNC's handout on reorganizing drafts describes the method: number the paragraphs, write a brief note on what each one does, list the notes on a separate page, and then examine the list. Ask how each point relates to the thesis and to its neighbors, where words or ideas repeat, whether the order matches the order the thesis promises, and whether the draft covers everything the prompt asks. UNC stresses outlining "what is actually in your paper, rather than what you intended to have in it."

### Cutting and "darlings"

UNC's revising handout says writers "must be willing to sacrifice their favorite bits of writing for the good of the piece as a whole," because "the idea or metaphor or paragraph that I think is most wonderful and brilliant is often the very thing that confuses my reader." The popular phrase "kill your darlings" goes back to Arthur Quiller-Couch, who told Cambridge students in his lecture "On Style" (January 28, 1914): "Whenever you feel an impulse to perpetrate a piece of exceptionally fine writing, obey it--whole-heartedly--and delete it before sending your manuscript to press. *Murder your darlings.*" His target was "extraneous Ornament," writing that shows off instead of serving the reader. The test for any passage you love is whether the reader needs it.

### The Paramedic Method

Purdue OWL describes the **Paramedic Method**, "originally developed by Richard Lanham in *Revising Prose*," as seven steps for wordy sentences:

1. Circle the prepositions (*of, in, about, for, onto, into*).
2. Draw a box around the *is* verb forms.
3. Ask, "Where's the action?"
4. Change the action into a simple verb.
5. Move the doer into the subject ("Who's kicking whom").
6. Eliminate any unnecessary slow wind-ups.
7. Eliminate any redundancies.

OWL's own example cuts *The point I wish to make is that the employees working at this company are in need of a much better manager of their money* (25 words) to *Employees at this company need a better money manager* (9 words).

### Proofreading systematically

UNC's editing and proofreading handout recommends a plan rather than a quick skim:

- **Don't rely entirely on checkers.** Spell checkers "will not catch misspellings that form another valid word" (*your/you're*, *to/too*, *there/their*). Grammar checkers "work with a limited number of rules," so they miss errors and flag correct sentences; use them as prompts you evaluate.
- **Proofread for one kind of error at a time.**
- **Read slowly and aloud**; read each sentence on its own line; circle every punctuation mark; read backwards word by word to check spelling.
- **Keep a list of your recurring errors** and search for them specifically. Purdue OWL gives the same advice for LOCs.

### Peer review

A reader who did not write the draft provides the distance you lack. Ask for the kind of feedback the draft needs at its stage: early drafts need responses to the argument and structure (Where did you get lost? What did you expect next?), not comma corrections. UNC's audience handout suggests asking your reader to say back what they understood; if they cannot, the draft has a structural problem.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reverse-outlining a draft

**Illustrative thesis:** *The university's switch to open textbooks in introductory courses lowered costs for students without reducing course quality.*

**Reverse outline of the draft (what each paragraph actually does):**

1. Intro; thesis.
2. History of textbook prices nationally.
3. Survey of students on costs (cost part 1).
4. Faculty reactions to the switch, mostly about workload.
5. Cost savings per student (cost part 2).
6. Comparison of grades before and after the switch.
7. More faculty workload complaints.
8. Conclusion that introduces a new idea: open textbooks for graduate courses.

**Diagnosis:**

- Cost evidence is split across 3 and 5: combine.
- The thesis promises "without reducing course quality," but only paragraph 6 addresses quality, and grades alone are a thin measure. **Development gap.**
- Paragraphs 4 and 7 discuss faculty workload, which the thesis never mentions: cut, merge into one paragraph as a counterargument, or revise the thesis to include it.
- Paragraph 2 may be too much background; shorten to two sentences.
- The conclusion introduces a new topic (graduate courses), which UNC's conclusions handout lists as a strategy to avoid.

**Revised plan:** 1 intro → 2 cost evidence (old 3 + 5) → 3 quality evidence (old 6, plus a second measure) → 4 workload as counterargument and response (old 4 + 7) → 5 conclusion on implications for intro courses.

Reasoning: no sentence was edited yet. The reverse outline exposed the draft's biggest problems, an unsupported half of the thesis and an unannounced topic, which sentence-level polishing would never have fixed.

### Example 2: Applying the Paramedic Method

**Illustrative sentence (27 words):** *It is the recommendation of the committee that there be a reduction in the number of meetings that are held on a weekly basis by the department.*

1. **Prepositions:** *of* (the committee), *in* (the number), *of* (meetings), *on* (a weekly basis), *by* (the department).
2. **Is verbs:** *is*, *be*, *are held*.
3. **Where's the action?** *recommendation* (recommend) and *reduction* (reduce).
4. **Simple verbs:** *recommends*, *hold fewer*.
5. **Doers as subjects:** the committee recommends; the department holds.
6. **Slow wind-up:** *It is ... that there be* → delete.
7. **Redundancy:** *on a weekly basis* → *weekly*; *a reduction in the number of* → *fewer*.

**Revision (10 words):** *The committee recommends that the department hold fewer weekly meetings.*

Reasoning: every step had a visible target in the sentence. The meaning is unchanged; the reader now sees two actors and two actions instead of five prepositional phrases and three forms of *be*.

### Example 3: Deciding about a darling

**Illustrative paragraph from a lab report's introduction:**

*Enzymes are the silent architects of life, the unseen hands that sculpt every heartbeat and every breath. In this experiment, we measured how temperature affects the reaction rate of catalase in potato extract.*

**Test:** Does the reader of a lab report need the first sentence? It does not define enzymes, state the question, or provide context the second sentence lacks. It is "exceptionally fine writing" in Quiller-Couch's sense: ornament.

**Revision:** *Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that speed up chemical reactions, and their activity depends on conditions such as temperature. In this experiment, we measured how temperature affects the reaction rate of catalase in potato extract.*

Reasoning: the revision keeps the job the first sentence should do (introduce the concept) and drops the decoration. In a popular-science essay, the original metaphor might earn its place; the decision depends on the genre and the reader. (This paragraph illustrates editing choices; it does not report a performed experiment.)

### Example 4: A proofreading plan for a 10-page paper

1. **Citations pass:** check every quotation and paraphrase against its source and its in-text citation; check the reference list format.
2. **Personal-error pass:** search for the three errors you make most often (for example, *it's/its*, comma splices, *affect/effect*).
3. **Punctuation pass:** circle every comma and semicolon in a printed copy and justify each one (Lesson 59.2).
4. **Spelling pass:** read backwards word by word, paying special attention to names, technical terms, and numbers, which checkers handle worst.
5. **Format pass:** headings, page numbers, figure labels, and spacing against the required style guide.

Reasoning: each pass looks for one kind of error with a technique suited to it, following UNC's advice to "proofread for only one kind of error at a time." The order puts citations first because errors there can be integrity problems, not just typos.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Editing means fixing commas and spelling."** UNC: "That's called proofreading." It comes last, after revision of ideas and structure.
- **"I should edit as I draft so I finish faster."** UNC warns that revising only as you go means "you never get to think of the big picture" and may "short-circuit your creativity." Its tip: "Don't proofread as you go."
- **"Spell check and grammar check are enough."** They miss correctly spelled wrong words and apply a limited set of rules. Evaluate their suggestions; don't accept them automatically.
- **"Kill your darlings means cut everything you like."** Quiller-Couch targeted ornament written to impress. Keep a vivid passage if it serves the reader and the genre; cut it if it serves only you.
- **"HOCs always come first, no exceptions."** OWL notes that severe LOCs at the start of a paper can stop readers before they reach the thesis, and experienced writers move between levels.
- **"Cutting always improves writing."** Example 1 found a development gap that needed *more* evidence. Editing adds as often as it subtracts.
- **Boundary: editing someone else's work.** When you edit a colleague's or classmate's writing, suggest rather than impose, preserve their meaning and voice, and flag (don't silently fix) factual or citation problems. In a course, follow the instructor's rules on how much help is allowed.
- **Boundary: editors and publication standards.** Journals, publishers, and organizations often have house styles and their own copyediting stage. Follow their instructions even when they differ from this lesson's preferences.

## Practice

1. Label each concern as higher-order (HOC) or lower-order (LOC): (a) the thesis changed halfway through the paper; (b) inconsistent capitalization in headings; (c) two paragraphs make the same point; (d) a comma splice in paragraph 4; (e) the paper never addresses the second half of the prompt; (f) the first sentence has three spelling errors.
2. Apply the Paramedic Method and give before-and-after word counts: (a) *There are many students who are in need of assistance with the process of registration for classes.* (b) *The decision was made by the board to engage in a review of the policy concerning attendance.*
3. A spell checker found no errors in this sentence. Find four: *Their going too review the data before it's publication, so please except my apologies for the delay.*
4. Make a reverse outline (one line per paragraph) of an article, chapter, or report you are reading this week, of at least five paragraphs. Write two sentences on whether its order matches its stated purpose.
5. Your peer reviewer has 15 minutes with your first draft. Write three questions you would ask them that address HOCs, and explain why you would not ask them to check punctuation yet.
6. **Extended task.** Take a draft of your own (at least two pages). Complete passes 1-5 from the table in "Core ideas," keeping an editing log that lists, for each pass, what you checked, what you changed, and why. Include the reverse outline before and after. Score the work with the rubric.

> **Integrity note:** Editing help has limits in academic work. A peer, tutor, or writing center may comment on your draft, but the revisions should be yours unless your instructor allows otherwise; record significant help if your course requires it. When you edit someone else's writing, do not change the meaning of their claims or the content of their quotations, and flag suspected citation problems rather than silently rewriting them.
