# APP-01.8 — Writing — Essays & Research

An **essay** is a group of paragraphs that work together to make one main point. In an **opinion essay**, that point is a **claim**, something you believe and want the reader to agree with: *Our school should plant a butterfly garden.* In an **informative essay**, the point is a **focus**, a topic you will explain clearly: *How a monarch butterfly grows.* Either way, the essay needs evidence, and evidence usually comes from **research**: finding trustworthy sources, taking notes in your own words, and telling the reader where your information came from.

This lesson puts together everything from this course. You will plan an essay, find and judge sources, take notes, give credit with MLA citations, and revise.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 4-5. It is written for the student.

## Prerequisites

[APP-01.7 — Writing — Sentences to Paragraphs](/app-017-writing-sentences-to-paragraphs): you should be able to write a paragraph with a topic sentence, supporting details, transitions, and a conclusion. Summarizing a passage, from [APP-01.6 — Reading Comprehension — Informational Text](/app-016-reading-comprehension-informational-text), is the main skill used in note-taking.

> **Adult note:** Help the learner choose a narrow topic they care about and can research in a few sources, such as one animal, one local place, or one school question. A topic like "animals" is too big for a five-paragraph essay; "why monarch butterflies migrate" is about right.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Plan and draft a **five-paragraph** opinion or informative essay.
2. Support an opinion with **reasons** and **evidence**, and tell a **fact** from an **opinion**.
3. Decide whether a source is **credible**.
4. **Quote**, **paraphrase**, and **summarize** without plagiarizing, and write **MLA** citations.

## Core ideas

### The five-paragraph essay

| Paragraph | What it does |
|---|---|
| 1. Introduction | Hooks the reader, gives a little background, and ends with the **claim** (opinion) or **focus** (informative). |
| 2-4. Body paragraphs | Each one starts with a topic sentence for **one** reason or subtopic, then gives evidence and explains it. |
| 5. Conclusion | Restates the claim or focus in new words and leaves the reader with a final thought or call to action. |

The five-paragraph essay is a common, useful starting plan, but it is not the only way to write an essay. Longer topics with more sources need more paragraphs.

### Opinion writing: claim, reasons, evidence

- A **claim** is your opinion, stated clearly.
- A **reason** tells *why* you hold the claim.
- **Evidence** proves the reason: facts, numbers, examples, or quotations from a source.

A **fact** can be checked and shown to be true (*Monarchs have four life stages*). An **opinion** is what someone thinks or feels (*Monarchs are the most beautiful butterflies*). Opinions are fine in an opinion essay, as long as they are supported by reasons and facts.

One planning tool from a U.S. Department of Education writing guide is **TREE**: **T**opic sentence (your claim), **R**easons (three or more), **E**nding, and **E**xamine (check that the plan is complete). For each reason, plan at least one piece of evidence.

### Informative writing

An informative essay teaches the reader. It uses facts, definitions, and examples, organized by subtopic, and it leaves out the writer's opinions. The text structures from Lesson 1.6, such as sequence and compare and contrast, are good ways to organize the body paragraphs.

### Research steps

1. **Ask** a focused question: *Why do monarchs migrate, and where do they go?*
2. **Find** sources: library books, encyclopedias, and websites your teacher or librarian recommends.
3. **Judge** each source's credibility (below).
4. **Take notes** in key words, in your own words, and write down where each note came from.
5. **Organize** notes into the paragraphs of your plan.
6. **Draft**, **cite**, **revise**, and **edit**.

### Is this source credible?

Ask four questions:

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Who** wrote it? | An author or organization with knowledge of the topic, such as a scientist, university, museum, or government agency |
| **Why** was it written? | To inform, or to sell something or persuade? Watch for slogans and one-sided claims |
| **When** was it written or updated? | Science facts can change; history may call for older sources from the time |
| **Can I cross-check it?** | Do other trustworthy sources say the same thing? Does it list its own sources? |

### Quote, paraphrase, summarize

| Way to use a source | What it is | Needs a citation? |
|---|---|---|
| **Quote** | the author's exact words, in quotation marks | yes |
| **Paraphrase** | one idea restated fully in your own words and sentence structure | yes |
| **Summary** | the main idea of a longer passage, much shorter, in your own words | yes |

**Plagiarism** is using someone else's words or ideas without giving them credit. Copying a sentence and changing one or two words is still plagiarism, even with a citation, because the wording is still the author's.

### MLA citations

MLA style, from the Modern Language Association, has two parts:

1. A short **in-text citation** in parentheses after the information, usually the author's last name and a page number, for example *(Clark et al. 112)*. If there is no author, use a short title in quotation marks. Web pages usually have no page numbers, so leave the number out.
2. A **Works Cited** list at the end, in alphabetical order by the first word of each entry, with every line after the first indented (a **hanging indent**).

Each Works Cited entry lists these **core elements** in order, skipping any that do not apply:

> Author. Title of source. Title of container, Other contributors, Version, Number, Publisher, Publication date, Location.

The **container** is the larger thing that holds the source, such as the website a page belongs to. Titles of whole works like books and websites are in *italics*; titles of parts like a single web page go in "quotation marks."

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Planning an opinion essay with TREE

Prompt: *Should our class plant milkweed in the school garden?*

1. **Topic sentence (claim):** *Our class should plant milkweed in the school garden.*
2. **Reason 1:** It could give monarchs a place to lay eggs. **Evidence:** the U.S. Forest Service says migrating monarchs lay eggs on milkweeds as they move north in February and March.
3. **Reason 2:** We could watch a life cycle up close. **Evidence:** monarchs go through four stages, egg, larva, pupa, and adult, and the pupa can become an adult in as few as five days, so changes happen fast enough to observe.
4. **Reason 3:** Caring for a garden gives every student a job. **Evidence:** a plan for weekly watering turns (from our own class, not a source).
5. **Ending:** *Planting milkweed would help monarchs and help us learn.*
6. **Examine:** three reasons, each with evidence; the third uses our own plan, not a source, which is acceptable for an opinion essay but should be labeled as such. Reason 1 says "could," because one garden is not proven to help monarchs. Do not claim more than the evidence shows.

### Example 2: Judging two sources

Question: *How long do monarch butterflies live?*

- **Source A:** "Monarch Butterfly Biology," a page from the U.S. Forest Service, a government agency. It says summer monarchs live 2-5 weeks and some migrating monarchs live as long as 9 months.
- **Source B:** *Illustrative example, not a real site.* A post titled "AMAZING butterfly facts!!!" with no author, no date, and no sources. It says monarchs live for 10 years.

1. **Who?** A is a government science agency; B has no author.
2. **Why?** A is written to inform; B uses exclamation points and no evidence.
3. **When?** B has no date at all.
4. **Cross-check:** Source B's "10 years" disagrees with Source A's "as long as 9 months," and B gives no way to check it.
5. **Decision:** use Source A. Do not use Source B.

### Example 3: Quote, paraphrase, or plagiarism?

Original sentence from Source A: *"The eggs hatch about four days after they are laid."*

1. **Quote:** *According to the U.S. Forest Service, "The eggs hatch about four days after they are laid" ("Monarch Butterfly Biology").* Exact words, in quotation marks, with a citation. Correct.
2. **Too close (plagiarism):** *The eggs hatch about four days after being laid ("Monarch Butterfly Biology").* Only two words changed, so it is still the author's sentence. Not acceptable as a paraphrase.
3. **Paraphrase:** *A tiny caterpillar comes out of a monarch egg roughly four days after the egg is laid ("Monarch Butterfly Biology").* New words and new sentence structure, same meaning, with a citation. Correct.

How to paraphrase: read the passage until you understand it, cover it up, write the idea from memory, then check your version against the original to make sure the meaning is right and the wording is yours.

### Example 4: Writing MLA citations

**A book:** *Biology 2e*, written by Mary Ann Clark, Matthew Douglas, and Jung Choi, published by OpenStax in 2018.

1. Author: three or more authors, so list the first author last-name-first, then *et al.* (Latin for "and others"): *Clark, Mary Ann, et al.*
2. Title of source: a whole book, so italics: *Biology 2e*.
3. Publisher and date: *OpenStax, 2018.*

> Clark, Mary Ann, et al. *Biology 2e*. OpenStax, 2018.

In-text: *(Clark et al.)*, adding a page number if you used a print copy with pages.

**A web page:** "Monarch Butterfly Biology," on the USDA Forest Service website, with no author and no date shown.

1. No author, so start with the title, a single page in quotation marks: *"Monarch Butterfly Biology."*
2. Container: the website, in italics: *USDA Forest Service*. The publisher has the same name as the site, so list it only once.
3. Location: the URL without *https://*.
4. No date is shown, so add the date you looked at it.

> "Monarch Butterfly Biology." *USDA Forest Service*, www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/Monarch_Butterfly/biology/index.shtml. Accessed 19 Oct. 2026.

In-text: *("Monarch Butterfly Biology")*. Works Cited order: *Clark* comes before *Monarch* alphabetically (the opening quotation mark is ignored), so the book is listed first.

### Example 5: An informative introduction and conclusion

Focus: *the monarch's migration.*

> **Introduction:** Each year, a butterfly that weighs less than a gram flies thousands of miles. The last monarchs of the year do not stay and lay eggs like the generations before them. Instead, they fly to Mexico to spend the winter. This essay explains how monarch generations differ, how far the migration goes, and how the butterflies return north.

> **Draft conclusion:** Monarchs make the longest trip of any insect in the world, which proves they are the toughest butterflies.

Revision decisions:

1. Check each fact against the notes. The source says the trip is 2,000 to 3,000 miles. It does not say it is the longest trip of any insect, so that claim is removed.
2. "Toughest butterflies" is an opinion, and this is an informative essay, so it is removed too.
3. Restate the focus in new words and end with the cycle starting again.

> **Revised conclusion:** By changing how the last generation of the year lives, monarchs survive the winter in Mexico and then head north to start the cycle again.

Every fact in an essay should be one you can point to in a source.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Every essay must have exactly five paragraphs."** Five paragraphs is a starting plan. Use more body paragraphs if you have more reasons or subtopics.
- **"Changing a few words makes it my own."** That is still plagiarism. A paraphrase changes the words *and* the sentence structure, and it still needs a citation.
- **"If I paraphrase, I don't need to cite."** Paraphrases and summaries need citations too. The idea still came from someone else.
- **"If it is on the internet, it is true."** Anyone can post anything. Check who wrote it, why, and when, and cross-check facts with another trustworthy source.
- **"An opinion essay doesn't need facts."** Opinions are stronger with evidence. "Milkweed is where monarchs lay eggs" persuades more than "butterflies are pretty."
- **"Citations go only at the end."** MLA needs both: in-text citations where you use the information, and the Works Cited list at the end.
- **"Revising means fixing spelling."** Revising means improving the ideas, evidence, and order; editing fixes capitals, punctuation, and spelling. A common classroom memory aid is **ARMS** for revising (Add, Remove, Move, Substitute) and **CUPS** for editing (Capitals, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling).
