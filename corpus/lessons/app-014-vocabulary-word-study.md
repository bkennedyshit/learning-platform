# APP-01.4 — Vocabulary & Word Study

You can sound out *unbreakable* letter by letter, but that does not tell you what it means. **Vocabulary** is knowing what words mean and how to use them. **Word study** is looking inside a word for smaller parts that carry meaning. *Un-* means "not," *break* is the base, and *-able* means "able to be." Put them together: *unbreakable* means "not able to be broken."

The more words you understand, the easier it is to understand what you read. When you meet a word you do not know, you have three tools: look at its **parts**, look at the **context** (the words and sentences around it), and, if you still need to, look it up in a **dictionary**.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 3-4. It is written for the student.

## Prerequisites

[APP-01.3 — Fluency & Word Recognition](/app-013-fluency-word-recognition): you should be able to read one-syllable words with vowel teams and r-controlled vowels, and sound out longer words a part at a time. Knowing what a noun, verb, and adjective are helps but is not required.

> **Adult note:** A children's dictionary (print or online) is useful for checking answers. The National Reading Panel found that vocabulary instruction can improve comprehension, and that it works best with several methods together: teaching some words directly, meeting words many times in different contexts, and learning words before reading a text that uses them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Split a word into **prefix + base + suffix** and explain the whole word using the meanings of *un-*, *re-*, *pre-*, *mis-*, *-ful*, *-less*, *-ness*, *-tion*, and *-er*.
2. Figure out an unknown word from **context clues** and name the kind of clue that helped.
3. Pick the right meaning of a **multiple-meaning word** from the sentence.
4. Use *bio-*, *geo-*, and *-graph* to explain words like *biography* and *geography*.

## Core ideas

### Prefixes, bases, and suffixes

A **base word** is the main part of a word, like *happy* or *read*. A **prefix** is added to the front and changes the meaning. A **suffix** is added to the end. It can change the meaning and often changes the word's job in a sentence.

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| un- | not; the opposite of | *unhappy*, *unlock* |
| re- | again | *reread*, *rebuild* |
| pre- | before | *preview*, *preheat* |
| mis- | wrongly, badly | *misspell*, *misread* |

| Suffix | Meaning | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| -ful | full of | *hopeful*, *careful* | makes an adjective |
| -less | without | *hopeless*, *careless* | makes an adjective |
| -ness | state of being | *kindness*, *darkness* | makes a noun from an adjective |
| -tion | act or state of | *action*, *protection* | makes a noun, often from a verb |
| -er | person or thing that does | *teacher*, *painter* | makes a noun from a verb |
| -er | more | *taller*, *faster* | compares two things |

Notice that *-er* has two different jobs. You can tell which one by the base: *teach* is a verb, so *teacher* is "a person who teaches." *Tall* is an adjective, so *taller* means "more tall."

Sometimes the spelling of the base changes when you add a suffix: *happy* + *-ness* = *happiness* (the *y* changes to *i*).

### Compound words

A **compound word** is two whole words joined together: *sun* + *flower* = *sunflower*, *back* + *pack* = *backpack*, *book* + *shelf* = *bookshelf*. The meaning usually comes from both parts. A *bookshelf* is a shelf for books.

### Context clues

**Context** is the words and sentences around an unknown word. Writers often leave clues:

| Clue type | Signal words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | *is*, *means*, commas | A *habitat*, the place where an animal lives, must have food and water. |
| Synonym (same meaning) | *or*, *also* | The puppy was *timid*, or shy, around new people. |
| Antonym (opposite) | *but*, *unlike*, *however* | Unlike his *cautious* sister, Leo was reckless. |
| Example | *such as*, *like*, *for example* | *Reptiles*, such as snakes, lizards, and turtles, have scaly skin. |

### Multiple-meaning words

Some words have more than one meaning. *Bark* can be the hard outer covering of a tree or the loud noise a dog makes. *Bat* can be a wooden stick for hitting a ball or a small flying animal. The sentence tells you which meaning the writer wants.

### Tiers of words

Some teachers sort words into three tiers, an idea from the vocabulary researchers Isabel Beck, Margaret McKeown, and Linda Kucan. **Tier 1** words are everyday words most children already know (*dog*, *happy*, *run*). **Tier 2** words show up in books across many subjects but are less common in conversation (*compare*, *observe*, *fortunate*, *predict*). **Tier 3** words belong to one subject (*photosynthesis*, *denominator*). Tier 2 words are worth extra practice because you will meet them everywhere.

### Greek word parts

Many science and school words are built from Greek parts:

| Part | Meaning | Words |
|---|---|---|
| bio- | life | *biology* (the study of living things), *biography* (a written story of someone's life) |
| geo- | earth | *geography* (the study of Earth's places and features), *geology* |
| -graph | something written or drawn | *autograph*, *biography*, *geography* |

The ending *-logy* is used in names of sciences, so *biology* and *geology* are both sciences.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Taking a word apart

Word: **misunderstanding**

1. Look for a prefix you know at the front: *mis-*, meaning "wrongly."
2. Look for a suffix at the end: *-ing*.
3. What is left is the base: *understand*.
4. Put the meanings together: "understanding something wrongly."
5. Check in a sentence: *There was a misunderstanding about the time, so Jo arrived an hour early.* That fits: someone understood the time wrongly.

### Example 2: Using a context clue

Sentence: *The desert is **arid**; it gets very little rain.*

1. Find the unknown word: *arid*.
2. Read the words around it. The semicolon is followed by an explanation: "it gets very little rain."
3. That is a **definition** clue. A place with very little rain is dry.
4. Test it: *The desert is dry; it gets very little rain.* It makes sense.
5. Answer: *arid* means "very dry."

### Example 3: Which meaning fits?

Sentence: *The owl and the **bat** both hunt at night.*

1. List the meanings you know: (a) a stick used to hit a ball; (b) a small flying animal.
2. Look at the clues: the sentence compares it with an owl, and both "hunt at night."
3. A stick cannot hunt, so meaning (a) does not fit.
4. Answer: meaning (b), the flying animal.

### Example 4: When a word part fools you

Word: **invaluable**

1. It looks like *in-* (which can mean "not") + *value* + *-able*.
2. If you add the parts mechanically, you get "not able to be valued," which sounds like "worthless."
3. Check the context: *Her help was invaluable; we could not have finished without her.* That means her help was very important.
4. A dictionary confirms it: *invaluable* means "extremely useful."

Reasoning: word parts are a strong clue, not a guarantee. Always check the meaning in the sentence.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Every word that starts with *un* or *re* has a prefix."** In *uncle*, *under*, *ready*, and *real*, the letters are part of the word, not a prefix. Test it: if you take the letters away, is a real base word left that makes sense? *Cle* and *ady* are not words.
- **"Every *-er* means 'a person who.'"** *Taller* means "more tall." In *water* and *under*, *-er* is not a suffix at all.
- **"Context clues always tell you the exact meaning."** Sometimes the sentence gives only a hint, like "it is something good" or "it is a feeling." If the word matters, check a dictionary.
- **"Use the first definition in the dictionary."** Many words have several meanings. Pick the one that fits your sentence.
- **"Word parts always add up."** *Invaluable* means very useful, not worthless. Check the parts against the context.
- **"A compound word always means its two parts."** Most do (*bookshelf*), but some do not: a *butterfly* is not a fly made of butter.

## Practice

1. Split each word into prefix, base, and suffix, then explain it: *unhelpful*, *reheated*, *preview*, *misbehave*.
2. Add *-ful* or *-less* to make a word that fits: *The broken toy was use____.* *Thank you for being so help____.*
3. Which *-er* means "a person who," and which means "more"? *farmer*, *colder*, *singer*, *smaller*.
4. Use the context to define the bold word and name the clue type: *Wolves are **carnivores**, or meat-eaters.*
5. Use the context: *Unlike the **noisy** classroom next door, our room was silent.* What does *silent* mean? What kind of clue helped?
6. What does *bark* mean in each sentence? (a) *The bark of the old oak tree was rough.* (b) *We heard a bark from the yard.*
7. Is *re* a prefix in *rewrite*? In *reach*? Explain how you know.
8. Using word parts, what do you think a *geographer* studies? What does an *autobiography* tell about? (Hint: *auto-* means "self.")

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. *un* + *help* + *ful* = not full of help, not helpful. *re* + *heat* + *ed* = heated again. *pre* + *view* = see before. *mis* + *behave* = behave badly.
2. *useless* (without use); *helpful* (full of help).
3. "A person who": *farmer*, *singer*. "More": *colder*, *smaller*.
4. *Carnivores* means meat-eaters. Synonym clue, signaled by *or*.
5. *Silent* means quiet, making no noise. Antonym clue, signaled by *unlike*: silent is the opposite of noisy.
6. (a) The outer covering of a tree. (b) The noise a dog makes.
7. In *rewrite*, yes: *write* is a real base, and *rewrite* means "write again." In *reach*, no: taking away *re* leaves *ach*, which is not a base word.
8. A *geographer* is a person who studies Earth's places and features (*geo-* earth + *-graph* written or drawn + *-er* person). An *autobiography* is the story of a person's own life, written by that person (*auto-* self + *bio-* life + *-graph* written).

**Self-check:** For each answer, put your meaning back into the sentence in place of the word. If the sentence still makes sense, your meaning is probably right.

</details>

> **Adult note:** When a learner meets a new Tier 2 word, try using it in conversation over the next few days. Hearing and using a word several times in different settings helps it stick.
