# 19.8 - Contemporary America

The most recent decades are the hardest to study as history. Many participants are still alive, records are still being released, and Americans disagree sharply about what recent events mean. Historians handle this by relying on documented facts, such as election results, laws, court decisions, and official reports, while labeling interpretations as interpretations. This lesson follows that approach for the years from 1980 to the mid-2020s.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). It does not take sides in current political debates. Facts about recent events were checked against official sources on October 19, 2026; for anything more recent, check an official source yourself. Its main skill is **separating evidence from interpretation**.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

## Core ideas

### The Reagan years and the end of the Cold War

In the 1980 election, Ronald Reagan won 489 electoral votes to President Jimmy Carter's 49 (National Archives). In his inaugural address, Reagan said: "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." His supporters and critics still debate the results of his policies of tax cuts, reduced regulation, and increased military spending; those debates are interpretations, while the election results and his words are evidence.

The Cold War ended quickly. Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's decision to loosen control over Eastern Europe "led to the collapse of the Berlin Wall in November 1989, and then the overthrow of Communist rule throughout Eastern Europe." On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned as president of the Soviet Union, and the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time (Office of the Historian). Historians still debate how much credit belongs to U.S. pressure, to Gorbachev's reforms, to the Soviet economy's weaknesses, and to the people of Eastern Europe who demanded change.

In 1990–1991, the United States led a coalition in the **Gulf War** after Iraq invaded Kuwait.

### Economic and social change

| Trend | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Market volatility | On October 19, 1987 ("Black Monday"), the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6 percent in one day | Federal Reserve History |
| Trade and globalization | The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) aimed "to abolish trade barriers between the United States, Mexico, and Canada" | *American Yawp* |
| Immigration | The foreign-born share of the U.S. population grew from 7.9 percent in 1990 to 12.9 percent in 2010 | *American Yawp* |
| Diversity | The chance that two randomly chosen people are from different racial or ethnic groups rose from 54.9 percent (2010) to 61.1 percent (2020) | U.S. Census Bureau |
| Financial crisis | The **Great Recession** lasted from December 2007 to June 2009, "the longest since World War II"; unemployment peaked at 10 percent in October 2009. It followed a crisis in high-risk ("subprime") mortgages | Federal Reserve History |
| Health care | The Affordable Care Act, "enacted in March 2010," aimed to make affordable health insurance available to more people | U.S. Department of Health and Human Services |
| Pandemic | CDC reported the first confirmed U.S. COVID-19 case on January 20, 2020; the World Health Organization declared a pandemic on March 11, 2020; unemployment reached a record 14.7 percent in April 2020 | CDC; Bureau of Labor Statistics |

Rights and law also changed: the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) is "a federal civil rights law that prohibits discrimination against people with disabilities in everyday activities" (ADA.gov), and in *Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) the Supreme Court held 5–4 that the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees same-sex couples the right to marry (Oyez).

### September 11, 2001, and its aftermath

The 9/11 Commission's report describes the attacks: two hijacked airliners struck the World Trade Center's towers, a third hit the Pentagon, and a fourth "crashed in a field in southern Pennsylvania" after passengers fought back. "More than 2,600 people died at the World Trade Center; 125 died at the Pentagon; 256 died on the four planes." The attacks were carried out by 19 men acting for Islamist extremists based in Afghanistan.

| Date | Response | What the document says |
|---|---|---|
| September 18, 2001 | **Authorization for Use of Military Force** (Public Law 107-40); the Senate vote was 98–0 | "The President is authorized to use all necessary and appropriate force against those nations, organizations, or persons he determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks" |
| October 26, 2001 | **USA PATRIOT Act** (Public Law 107-56) | Its full title begins "Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism"; it expanded investigative powers |
| 2002 | **Department of Homeland Security** | Created by combining "22 different federal departments and agencies into a unified, integrated Cabinet agency" (DHS) |
| October 16, 2002 | **Iraq resolution** (Public Law 107-243) | Authorized the use of armed forces against Iraq; the U.S.-led invasion began in 2003 |

The war in Afghanistan began in October 2001, and the war in Iraq in March 2003. Americans have debated these wars and the balance between security and civil liberties ever since, much as they did during earlier wars (Lessons 19.2, 19.6, and 19.7).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Evidence or interpretation?

| Statement | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Reagan won 489 electoral votes in 1980." | Evidence (documented fact) | Official National Archives record |
| "Reagan's policies won the Cold War." | Interpretation | A causal claim; historians weigh several causes |
| "The Senate voted 98–0 for the 2001 AUMF." | Evidence | Senate roll call |
| "The PATRIOT Act made Americans safer." | Interpretation | Requires evidence about outcomes and a definition of "safer" |
| "The Great Recession lasted from December 2007 to June 2009." | Evidence | Federal Reserve History |
| "The Affordable Care Act was a success." | Interpretation | Depends on which goal and which data you use |

**Method:** a fact can be checked in a record. An interpretation makes a claim about causes, meaning, or value; it can be strong or weak depending on the evidence and reasoning, but it is never simply "true" the way a vote count is.

### Example 2: Reading the 2001 AUMF closely

1. **Who is covered?** "nations, organizations, or persons **he determines** planned, authorized, committed, or aided the terrorist attacks ... or harbored such organizations or persons."
2. **Who decides?** The president determines who falls under the authorization.
3. **Is there a time limit or a geographic limit in the quoted text?** No.
4. **Compare:** the Tonkin Gulf Resolution (1964) also gave broad authority and was later repealed (Lesson 19.7); the War Powers Resolution (1973) requires reports and sets time limits when forces are introduced without authorization.
5. **Reasoning:** broad wording passed quickly in a crisis can be used for many years. This is a documented feature of the text; whether its later uses were appropriate is an interpretation you can argue with evidence.

### Example 3: Reading Census data with care

The Census Bureau reports that the share of the population identifying as White alone and not Hispanic was 57.8 percent in 2020, down from 63.7 percent in 2010, and the diversity index rose from 54.9 to 61.1 percent.

1. **What changed?** The measured population became more diverse.
2. **What else changed?** The Census Bureau says the results reflect "demographic changes as well as improvements to the ways in which race and ethnicity data are collected and processed."
3. **Reasoning:** part of the change is real population change, and part comes from better measurement. A careful writer says "the 2020 Census measured a more diverse population," not "population change alone cut the White non-Hispanic share by 5.9 points" (63.7 − 57.8 = 5.9).

### Example 4: Writing about a contested election

**Question:** How should a history essay describe the 2000 election?

1. **Start with records:** electoral votes 271–266; Bush received fewer popular votes; a D.C. elector cast a blank ballot.
2. **Describe the ruling precisely:** 7–2 that the recount scheme violated equal protection; 5–4 on stopping the recount.
3. **Attribute interpretations:** supporters of the decision argued it applied equal protection and ended uncertainty; critics argued the Court should have allowed recounts to continue. Name who holds each view.
4. **Avoid loaded wording** like "stolen" or "rightful" unless you are quoting someone and identifying them.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Recent events aren't history yet."** Historians study the recent past using the same methods; they are especially careful because records are incomplete and many participants are still alive.
- **"One president ended the Cold War."** The Office of the Historian's account credits Gorbachev's choices and Eastern European independence movements as well as U.S. policy; historians debate the balance.
- **"Winning the popular vote means winning the presidency."** In 2000 the winner of the Electoral College received fewer popular votes. Presidents are chosen by electoral votes (see [APP-05.7](/app-057-political-participation-public-opinion)).
- **"Impeachment means removal."** The House impeaches (charges); the Senate holds the trial. All three presidential impeachments since 1980 ended in Senate verdicts of not guilty.
- **"The 2001 AUMF was a declaration of war."** It is a joint resolution authorizing force, not a formal declaration of war; Congress has not formally declared war since World War II.
- **Boundary: numbers can change.** Agencies revise data (for example, census methods changed between 2010 and 2020). Always give the date and source of a statistic.
- **Boundary: current events.** For anything after the date this lesson was checked, use official sources and label claims about very recent events as provisional.
