# 59.8 - Writing for the Digital Age

Most writing is now read on a screen, often on a phone, often in a hurry, and often after a search. People reading on screens do not usually move through a page from top to bottom the way a reader works through a printed essay. They **scan**: they look at headings, the first words of paragraphs, links, and bold text, decide whether the page answers their question, and leave or commit. Writing for the web is therefore a matter of structure as much as sentences: putting the answer where a scanning eye lands, labeling sections honestly, and making every link and image understandable out of context, including to people using screen readers.

This final lesson of the Writing Craft & Rhetoric course is for college students and professionals who write web pages, documentation sites, newsletters, posts, or institutional announcements. It applies the clarity, argument, and editing skills from Lessons 59.1-59.7 to digital formats.

## Core ideas

### How people read on screens

In 1997, Jakob Nielsen summarized Nielsen Norman Group's early research bluntly: "People rarely read Web pages word by word; instead, they scan the page." In that research, "79 percent of our test users always scanned any new page they came across; only 16 percent read word-by-word." NN/g's recommendations from that work remain the core of web writing: highlighted keywords, "meaningful sub-headings (not 'clever' ones)," bulleted lists, one idea per paragraph, the **inverted pyramid** (conclusion first), and about half the word count of conventional writing.

Later eyetracking work refined the picture. NN/g identified the **F-shaped pattern** in 2006: readers scan across the top, then a shorter line lower down, then down the left edge. A 2019 article by Kara Pernice describes four main patterns for scanning text:

| Pattern | What the eye does |
|---|---|
| **F-pattern** | scans the top lines and the left side, reading less as it moves down |
| **Spotted** | skips large chunks of text, looking for something specific (a number, a link, a keyword) |
| **Layer-cake** | fixates mostly on headings and subheadings, reading body text only under the heading of interest |
| **Commitment** | reads all or most of the text: "traditional reading, not scanning," usually when the reader is highly motivated |

Two corrections matter. First, NN/g's 2017 revisit says "the F-pattern is negative for users and businesses," and that "good design can prevent F-shape scanning": the F is what happens when a page gives readers nothing better to scan. Its antidotes include putting "the most important points in the first two paragraphs" and using headings and subheadings that look more important than body text. Second, the commitment pattern shows that people do read in full when the content matters to them. The goal is not to write less but to make the page easy to evaluate before the reader commits.

### The inverted pyramid

NN/g's Amy Schade describes the **inverted pyramid** as starting "content with the most important piece of information so readers can get the main point, regardless of how much they read." It comes from journalism, where the who, what, when, where, and why come first. On the web, it means the first sentence of a page or section should answer the question the heading raises.

### Writing for accessibility

The W3C Web Accessibility Initiative's "Writing for Web Accessibility" tips are writing guidelines as much as technical ones:

- **Informative, unique page titles**, with the specific information first.
- **Headings that convey meaning and structure**: "Good headings provide an outline of the content." WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 2.4.6 (Headings and Labels) states: "Headings and labels describe topic or purpose."
- **Meaningful link text** that describes the target; avoid "click here" or "read more."
- **Text alternatives for images** that provide "the information or function of the image"; purely decorative images need none.
- **Clear and concise content**: short sentences and paragraphs, acronyms expanded on first use, and lists where appropriate.

### Search: people-first content

Google Search Central's guidance on helpful content says its ranking systems are "designed to prioritize helpful, reliable information that's created to benefit people, and not content that's created to manipulate search engine rankings." It recommends **people-first** rather than **search engine-first** content and lists warning signs, including producing lots of content on many topics "in hopes that some of it might perform well," using "extensive automation to produce content on many topics," and writing to a particular word count: "Are you writing to a particular word count because you've heard or read that Google has a preferred word count? (No, we don't.)" It also warns against changing page dates "to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed," and suggests asking **Who, How, and Why** about your content: who created it, how, and why.

For writers, SEO writing and good web writing mostly converge: a clear title and headings that use the words your readers use, a first paragraph that answers the question, and accurate, original information. Keyword stuffing and padding work against both.

### Channels: newsletters and short posts

Different channels have different constraints:

- **Newsletters** arrive in an inbox among many others. The subject line is microcontent (Lesson 59.6) that must make sense alone and, for commercial email in the U.S., must accurately reflect the content under the CAN-SPAM Act. NN/g's 1997 summary added that a newer study found users read email newsletters "even more abruptly than they read websites," so put the most important item first.
- **Short social posts** compress a message into one or two sentences plus a link. Character limits differ by platform and account type and change over time, so check the platform's current help pages. Compression is a clarity exercise: one point, the key detail, and a clear next step.
- **Threads** (a series of linked posts) should make sense post by post, because readers may see one post shared out of context.

### AI-assisted editing

Generative AI tools can flag long sentences, unclear antecedents, or inconsistent terms, and they can also produce fluent text that is generic, inaccurate, or not yours. The MLA's guidance on citing generative AI recommends that you cite a tool whenever you "paraphrase, quote, or incorporate into your own work any content ... that was created by it," "acknowledge all functional uses of the tool (like editing your prose or translating words) in a note, your text, or another suitable location," and "take care to vet the secondary sources it cites." Your institution, instructor, publisher, or employer may have stricter rules, which take precedence.

A defensible approach, labeled here as a **suggested practice** rather than a rule: use AI tools to **find** problems, decide on fixes yourself, keep a log of what you accepted and rejected, and verify every factual claim or source the tool mentions.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Revising a dense announcement for scanning

**Illustrative draft (one paragraph, 95 words):**

*The library has been working over the past several months on a number of changes to how students can access course reserves, and after consultation with faculty and the student government, we are pleased to announce that beginning on January 12 students will be able to borrow reserve textbooks for four hours instead of two, and many reserves will also be available as e-books that can be read from off campus by logging in with your university ID, although some publishers do not license e-book versions, so not every title will be available that way.*

**Revision (49 words):**

> **Course reserves: 4-hour loans start January 12**
>
> Starting January 12, you can borrow print reserve textbooks for **4 hours** (up from 2).
>
> - **E-book reserves:** Many titles can be read off campus. Log in with your university ID.
> - **Not every title has an e-book:** Some publishers don't license e-book versions.

| Change | Principle |
|---|---|
| The news (4-hour loans, date) moved to a heading and the first sentence | inverted pyramid; NN/g's "most important points in the first two paragraphs" |
| Background on months of work and consultation cut | the reader's question is "what changes for me?" |
| Two separate facts put in a list with bold lead-ins | scannable text; bolded keywords for spotted and layer-cake scanning |
| *you* instead of *students* | addresses the reader directly |

Reasoning: no fact the reader needs was removed; the word count halved because the background paragraph was for the writer, not the reader. If the consultation matters (for example, to show the change was approved), a single sentence at the end can mention it.

### Example 2: Fixing link text and alt text

| Element | Before | After | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Link | *For the full policy, [click here].* | *Read the [full reserves policy (PDF, 2 pages)].* | describes the target and its format, as W3C advises |
| Repeated links | *[Read more]* under each of five news items | the headline of each item as its link | each link makes sense in a screen reader's list of links (WCAG 2.4.4) |
| Informative image | a chart of loan counts with alt text "chart" | alt text stating what the chart shows, such as "Reserve loans rose each semester from fall 2024 to fall 2026," with the data also in a table or the text | alt text conveys the image's information (the figures here are illustrative) |
| Decorative image | a photo of bookshelves with alt text "image of books on shelves in the library building" | empty alt text (`alt=""`) so screen readers skip it | W3C: purely decorative images need no text alternative |

Square brackets mark the link text in this table.

### Example 3: One message, three channels

**Illustrative message:** the campus Writing Center is adding evening drop-in hours in Main Library room 210, Monday through Thursday, 6-9 p.m., starting September 8; no appointment needed.

- **Web page heading and first sentence:** *Evening drop-in hours start September 8.* / *Starting September 8, you can get writing help without an appointment in Main Library room 210, Monday through Thursday, 6-9 p.m.*
- **Newsletter item:** subject line *New: evening writing help, no appointment (starts Sept. 8)*; one-paragraph item leading with the hours and location, then a link to the page.
- **Short social post (169 characters including the placeholder link):** *New this fall: evening drop-in help at the Writing Center, Main Library room 210, Mon-Thu 6-9 p.m., starting Sept. 8. No appointment needed. Details: example.org/writing*

Reasoning: every version leads with the same essential facts (what, when, where, no appointment) and drops detail in proportion to the channel's space. The subject line and the post both make sense with no other context. (`example.org` is a domain reserved for examples, used here as a placeholder.)

### Example 4: An AI-assisted editing log (suggested practice)

**Situation:** a student revises a 1,200-word essay. The course syllabus permits AI tools for feedback on clarity but not for generating text, and requires disclosure.

| Request to the tool | Tool's suggestion (summarized) | Decision and reason |
|---|---|---|
| "List sentences over 35 words." | flagged 4 sentences | Split 3; kept 1 periodic sentence on purpose (Lesson 59.2). |
| "Where is a pronoun's antecedent unclear?" | flagged *this* in paragraph 3 | Accepted: changed to *this delay*. |
| (none) | offered a rewritten conclusion | Rejected: generating text is not permitted, and the rewrite introduced a claim the essay did not support. |
| "Is the term *open access* used consistently?" | noted two variants | Accepted: standardized. |

**Disclosure note (in the format the instructor requested):** *I used [tool name] to identify long sentences, unclear pronouns, and inconsistent terms in my draft. All revisions and all content are my own.*

Reasoning: the log makes the tool's role checkable and keeps the writer's judgment in charge. It follows MLA's advice to acknowledge functional uses and the syllabus's limits. The scenario is illustrative; this lesson does not test or endorse any particular tool.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Design for the F-pattern."** NN/g calls the F-pattern "negative for users and businesses." Design so readers don't need it: front-load key points and use strong headings.
- **"People don't read online, so content doesn't matter."** The commitment pattern shows motivated readers do read fully. Scanning is how they decide whether to commit.
- **"Long content ranks better" or "There's a magic word count."** Google says plainly that it has no preferred word count and warns against writing to one.
- **"SEO writing means repeating keywords."** Google's guidance favors people-first content; use the words readers search for in titles and headings because they help readers, not as filler.
- **"Every image needs a description."** W3C says purely decorative images need no text alternative; informative images need alt text that conveys their information or function.
- **"The Z-pattern is proven like the F-pattern."** NN/g's 2019 article on text scanning lists the F, spotted, layer-cake, and commitment patterns. "Z-pattern" layouts appear in some design advice for sparse pages; treat them as a layout heuristic, not as one of those measured text-scanning patterns.
- **"AI tools can write it for me if I edit afterward."** Whether that is allowed depends on your course, publisher, or employer, and MLA recommends citing any AI-generated content you incorporate. Even where allowed, the tool cannot supply your evidence, your examples, or responsibility for accuracy.
- **Boundary: dated research.** The 79%/16% figures come from 1997 studies; NN/g's later eyetracking work (2006, 2017, 2019) found scanning still common but more varied. Cite the date when you cite the numbers.
- **Boundary: legal requirements.** Accessibility and commercial-email rules vary by country and organization (for example, many public institutions must meet specific accessibility standards). This lesson is educational, not legal advice.
