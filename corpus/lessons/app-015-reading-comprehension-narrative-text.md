# APP-01.5 — Reading Comprehension — Narrative Text

A **narrative** is a text that tells a story: who did what, where, and why, and what happened in the end. Fairy tales, fables, chapter books, and true stories about a person's life are all narratives. Reading a story well means more than knowing what happened. Good readers notice why characters act the way they do, figure out things the author hints at but does not say, and think about the bigger lesson the story teaches.

Stories are built from the same parts again and again. Once you know the parts, you can use them like a map to understand, remember, and retell almost any story.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 3-4. It is written for the student.

## Prerequisites

You should be able to read a one-page story on your own or with a little help, as in [APP-01.3 — Fluency & Word Recognition](/app-013-fluency-word-recognition), and use context clues to figure out unfamiliar words, as in [APP-01.4 — Vocabulary & Word Study](/app-014-vocabulary-word-study).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Find the **characters**, **setting**, **goal**, **problem**, **main events**, and **resolution** in a short story and put them on a story map.
2. Make an **inference** about a character and support it with a clue from the text plus what you already know.
3. Write a **theme** as a complete sentence and explain how it is different from the topic and from a summary.
4. Tell whether a story is told in **first person** or **third person**, and compare the themes of two stories.

## Core ideas

### Story elements

| Element | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Characters | Who is the story about? |
| Setting | Where and when does it happen? |
| Goal | What does the main character want? |
| Problem (conflict) | What gets in the way? |
| Events (plot) | What happens, in order? |
| Resolution | How is the problem solved, and how does it end? |
| Theme | What lesson or big idea about life does the story show? |

The **plot** usually rises toward a turning point (the **climax**), when the problem is most intense, and then falls toward the resolution. Not every story has every element. Some have more than one problem.

### Character motivation and change

**Motivation** is the reason a character does something. Authors rarely say it directly. You find it in what a character says, does, thinks, and how others react. Many main characters also **change**: they learn something or act differently at the end than at the beginning. Comparing the character in the first scene with the character in the last scene is a good way to find the theme.

### Inference: clue + what I know

An **inference** is a conclusion the author does not state but the text supports. A reliable formula is:

> **Text clue** + **what I already know** = **inference**

*Her hands shook as she opened the envelope.* The text never says "she was nervous," but shaking hands + you know people's hands shake when they are nervous = she was nervous. An inference must be supported by evidence. If you cannot point to a clue, it is a guess.

### Theme, topic, and summary

- **Topic:** one or two words: *friendship*, *a kite*.
- **Summary:** the main events in a few sentences, in your own words.
- **Theme:** a message about life that could apply to other stories and to real people, written as a **full sentence**: *Being generous can repair a friendship.*

"Friendship" is a topic, not a theme. A theme says something *about* friendship.

### Point of view

- **First person:** the narrator is a character in the story and uses *I*, *me*, *we*. You only know what that character knows and thinks.
- **Third person:** the narrator is outside the story and uses *he*, *she*, *they*, and names. The narrator may tell what one character or several characters think.

### Strategies good readers use

Before, during, and after reading, good readers **predict** what will happen, **ask questions** (*Why did she do that?*), **picture** the scene in their mind, **notice when they are confused** and reread, **make inferences**, and **summarize**. These strategies are recommended in a U.S. Department of Education practice guide on reading comprehension.

## Worked examples

All the stories in this lesson were written for it, except the fable in Example 3, which is a retelling of a traditional tale.

### Example 1: Mapping a story

*Illustrative story.*

> Maya had saved her allowance for six weeks to buy a red kite. On the first windy Saturday, she ran to the park with her little brother, Theo. "Can I hold it?" Theo asked. "No," said Maya. "You'll lose it." Theo kicked at the grass and sat down on a bench.
>
> The kite climbed higher and higher. Then a gust yanked the string, and the kite crashed into the top of an oak tree. Maya tugged. The string snapped. Her eyes stung.
>
> Theo walked over. He did not say, "I told you so." Instead, he pointed. "The branch is bending. When the wind blows the other way, it might fall." They waited together on the bench. Ten minutes later, the wind shifted, and the kite fluttered down onto the grass, a little torn.
>
> Maya tied the string back together. Then she held it out to Theo. "Your turn," she said.

| Element | Answer | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Characters | Maya and her little brother, Theo | named in the first paragraph |
| Setting | a park on a windy Saturday | "the first windy Saturday … the park" |
| Goal | Maya wants to fly her new kite | she saved for six weeks and ran to the park |
| Problem | the kite gets stuck in a tree; Maya also refuses to share | "crashed into the top of an oak tree"; "No … You'll lose it." |
| Events | Maya refuses Theo → the kite gets stuck → the string snaps → Theo helps → they wait | paragraphs 1-3 |
| Resolution | the kite falls down; Maya lets Theo fly it | last paragraph |

### Example 2: Inference and character change

Question: *How does Maya feel when the string snaps, and why does she give Theo a turn at the end?*

1. **Clue:** "Her eyes stung." **What I know:** your eyes sting when you are about to cry. **Inference:** Maya is upset, close to tears.
2. **Clue:** at the start Maya says, "You'll lose it." **What I know:** people do not share things they worked hard for when they are worried about losing them. **Inference:** Maya's motivation for refusing was protecting something she saved for.
3. **Clue:** Theo "did not say, 'I told you so'" and helps instead. Then Maya says, "Your turn." **Inference:** Theo's kindness, after Maya was unkind to him, changes how she acts.
4. **Change:** beginning, Maya will not share. End, she offers the kite first.
5. **Theme** (built from the change): *When someone is kind to you even after you were unkind, it can teach you to be generous.* It is a full sentence, and it applies beyond kites.

### Example 3: Comparing themes in two stories

*Retelling of Aesop's fable "The Ant and the Grasshopper."* All summer a grasshopper sang and played while an ant carried grain to its nest. "Why work so hard?" laughed the grasshopper. When winter came, the grasshopper had no food, and the ant had plenty.

1. Map the fable: characters, an ant and a grasshopper; setting, summer then winter; problem, the grasshopper does not prepare; resolution, the grasshopper is hungry in winter.
2. Theme: *It is wise to prepare today for what you will need later.* (If you read a version of this fable with a different ending, check whether the theme changes too. The theme must come from the story you actually read.)
3. Compare with Example 1. **Alike:** in both stories, a character's choice early in the story leads to a consequence later. **Different:** Maya's story is about generosity between people and ends with a character changing; the fable is about planning ahead and ends with a lesson for the reader.

### Example 4: Switching the point of view

Third person (original): *Theo kicked at the grass and sat down on a bench.*

First person, told by Theo: *I kicked at the grass and sat down on the bench. Maya never lets me hold anything.*

1. The pronoun changes from *Theo* to *I*.
2. Now the reader can hear Theo's thoughts ("Maya never lets me hold anything"), which the third-person version did not tell us.
3. The reader also loses something: we no longer know that Maya's eyes stung unless Theo sees it.

Reasoning: point of view controls what the reader is allowed to know.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The theme is one word, like *friendship*."** One word is a topic. A theme is a full sentence that says something about the topic.
- **"Retelling every detail is a summary."** A summary keeps only the main events, in order, in a few sentences. Small details like the color of the kite can usually be left out.
- **"An inference is a guess."** An inference must be backed by a clue in the text. If two readers disagree, the one with stronger evidence wins.
- **"The narrator is always the author."** In first-person stories the narrator is a character. The author, a real person, made the narrator up.
- **"A story has only one correct theme."** A story can support more than one theme, as long as each one is backed by evidence from the story.
- **"The main character is always good."** Characters can make mistakes, as Maya does. That is often what lets them change.

## Practice

Read this illustrative story, then answer the questions.

> On the morning of the class play, Sam's stomach felt like it was full of bees. He had practiced his lines every night, but when he peeked through the curtain and saw rows of parents, he backed away. "I can't," he whispered. Ms. Ortiz knelt beside him. "Look for your grandma in the third row," she said. "Say your lines just to her." When it was Sam's turn, he found his grandma's yellow hat and spoke to it. His voice shook at first, then grew steady. When the audience clapped, Sam grinned and bowed twice.

1. Who are the characters, and what is the setting?
2. What is Sam's goal, and what is the problem?
3. What does "his stomach felt like it was full of bees" tell you about Sam? Write your inference using a clue and what you know.
4. What is the turning point of the story?
5. How does Sam change from the beginning to the end? Give one clue from each part.
6. Which is a theme, and which is a topic? (a) *stage fright* (b) *Focusing on one friendly face can help you face your fears.*
7. Is this story told in first person or third person? How can you tell?
8. Write a two- or three-sentence summary of the story.

2. Goal: say his lines in the play. Problem: he is too nervous to go on stage.
3. Clue: his stomach feels "full of bees." What I know: people get a fluttery stomach when they are nervous. Inference: Sam is very nervous.
