# APP-03.7 — Evolution & Natural Selection

On the Galápagos Islands, different kinds of finches have different beaks: thick, strong beaks for cracking seeds and thin, pointed beaks for catching insects. Since 1976, scientists measuring one finch population every year have watched the average beak size change after the food supply changed. **Evolution** is change in the inherited traits of a population over generations. **Natural selection** is the main way that populations become adapted to their environments. This lesson explains how natural selection works, what evidence supports evolution, and how new species form.

## Core ideas

### Darwin, Wallace, and the finches

From **1831 to 1836**, Charles Darwin sailed around the world on HMS *Beagle*, with stops in South America, Australia, and the southern tip of Africa. His last stops included the **Galápagos Islands**, west of Ecuador. There he saw species on different islands that were clearly similar but had distinct differences. The ground finches had a graded series of beak sizes and shapes, and they closely resembled a finch species on the South American mainland. Darwin came to think the island species might have been modified from a mainland ancestor, and that each beak suited a particular food.

Alfred Russel Wallace, collecting in the Amazon and in the Malay Archipelago, independently reached the same explanation. In **1858**, papers by Darwin and Wallace were presented together at the Linnean Society in London. The next year, **1859**, Darwin published *On the Origin of Species*.

### How natural selection works

Darwin and Wallace reasoned from a few facts that anyone can observe:

1. **Variation:** individuals in a population differ in their traits.
2. **Inheritance:** many of those traits are passed from parents to offspring.
3. **Overproduction and competition:** organisms produce more offspring than can survive, so they compete for limited food, space, and other resources.
4. **Differential survival and reproduction:** individuals whose inherited traits suit the environment better tend to survive and have more offspring.
5. **Change over generations:** those helpful traits become more common in the next generation. Over many generations, the population changes. Darwin called this **descent with modification**.

Biologists measure **fitness** as reproductive success: how many surviving offspring an individual leaves, not how strong or fast it is.

### Watching selection happen: the Grants' finches

Peter and Rosemary Grant and their colleagues have studied Galápagos finches every year since **1976**. On the island of Daphne Major, medium ground finches vary in beak size, and beak size is inherited. After an El Niño brought unusually heavy rain, large hard seeds became scarce and small soft seeds became plentiful. Small-beaked birds survived and reproduced better, and in the following years the Grants found that the **average beak size in the population was smaller**. When conditions improved in 1987 and larger seeds became more available, the trend toward smaller beaks stopped. Selection followed the food supply.

Notice what changed: not the beak of any single bird, but the **average** across the population over generations.

### Evidence for evolution and common ancestry

| Evidence | What it shows | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fossils** | Organisms of the past differ from those today, and fossils show gradual changes over time | detailed fossil records of horses and of human ancestors |
| **Homologous structures** | Same basic bone layout used for different jobs, inherited from a common ancestor | the forelimbs of humans, bats, birds, and whales |
| **Vestigial structures** | Leftover parts with little or no apparent function | wings on flightless birds, hind-leg bones in whales |
| **Embryos** | Related animals share early developmental features | human embryos have a tail structure that is lost before birth |
| **DNA** | More closely related organisms have more similar DNA sequences | the pattern of DNA similarity matches the branching expected from common descent |
| **Biogeography** | Where organisms live fits with their history and moving continents | groups that evolved before the supercontinent Pangaea broke apart (about 200 million years ago) are found worldwide |

**Homologous vs analogous:** homologous structures share an ancestral origin even if they now do different jobs (a human arm and a bat wing). **Analogous** structures do similar jobs but evolved separately (an insect wing and a bat wing both fly, but they are built completely differently).

### Natural selection we can observe today: antibiotic resistance

**Antibiotics** are medicines that kill bacteria or keep them from growing. **Antimicrobial resistance** happens when bacteria or fungi develop the ability to defeat the drugs designed to kill them. It follows the same steps as the finches: a population of bacteria varies; if a few happen to carry a resistance trait, an antibiotic kills the others; the survivors multiply; and the population becomes mostly resistant. The resistance was already present in some bacteria; the drug did not create it. The CDC reports more than 2.8 million antimicrobial-resistant infections in the United States each year.

### New species

A **species** is a group of organisms that can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring. New species often form when a population is split by a geographic barrier, such as water between islands or a new mountain range. The separated groups face different conditions and gradually accumulate different traits until they could no longer interbreed even if they met again. This is **allopatric speciation** ("other homeland"). Speciation can also happen without geographic separation, within one place (**sympatric speciation**, "same homeland"), for example after an error in cell division gives offspring extra sets of chromosomes.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A model population of beetles

*Illustrative numbers, not measured data.* A field has 100 beetles: 50 green and 50 brown. The ground is brown, and birds spot green beetles more easily. Beetle color is inherited, and offspring have their parents' color.

1. **Starting fraction:** green = 50/100 = **50 percent**.
2. **Selection:** birds eat 30 of the green beetles and 10 of the brown. Survivors: 20 green, 40 brown.
3. **Reproduction:** each surviving beetle has 2 offspring. Next generation: 40 green, 80 brown = 120 beetles.
4. **New fraction:** green = 40/120 = 1/3 ≈ **33 percent**.
5. **Another round, same pattern:** suppose birds again eat 60 percent of green and 20 percent of brown. Survivors: 16 green and 64 brown, which produce 32 green and 128 brown. Green = 32/160 = **20 percent**.
6. **Interpret:** no beetle changed color. The population changed because green beetles left fewer offspring.

### Example 2: Explaining antibiotic resistance step by step

A patient's infection contains millions of bacteria. A few carry an inherited trait that lets them survive a particular antibiotic.

1. **Variation:** most bacteria are killed by the antibiotic; a few are not.
2. **Inheritance:** when bacteria divide, the new cells inherit the trait.
3. **Selection:** the antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria, removing the resistant ones' competition.
4. **Reproduction:** the resistant survivors multiply.
5. **Result:** the bacterial population is now mostly resistant, so that antibiotic no longer works well against it.
6. **Check:** the antibiotic did not *cause* the resistance trait; it *selected* the bacteria that already had it.

### Example 3: Homologous or analogous?

1. **A human arm and a whale flipper.** Both have one upper bone, two lower bones, wrist bones, and finger bones, in the same order, even though one is used for grasping and one for swimming. **Homologous:** evidence of a common ancestor.
2. **A bird wing and a butterfly wing.** Both are used to fly, but a bird wing has bones and feathers, while a butterfly wing is a thin membrane with no bones. **Analogous:** similar job, separate origins.
3. **A bird wing and a bat wing.** As wings they evolved separately, but the *bones* inside both are a modified version of the same vertebrate forelimb. **Homologous as forelimbs.** This is a boundary case: the answer depends on whether you compare the bones or the flight surfaces.

### Example 4: Reading a change in a population

*Illustrative data.* A sample of 10 finches has these beak depths in millimeters before a drought: 8, 9, 9, 10, 10, 10, 11, 11, 12, 10. After the drought, a sample of the next generation has 9, 10, 10, 11, 11, 11, 12, 12, 13, 11.

1. **Mean before:** sum = 100; 100 ÷ 10 = **10.0 mm**.
2. **Mean after:** sum = 110; 110 ÷ 10 = **11.0 mm**.
3. **Change:** 11.0 − 10.0 = 1.0 mm deeper on average.
4. **Reasoning:** if larger, harder seeds were the main food left during the drought, birds with deeper, stronger beaks could eat more and leave more offspring.
5. **Limits:** ten birds is a small sample; a real study measures many birds over many years, and must show that beak depth is inherited.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Evolution is just a theory, meaning a guess."** In science, a **theory** is a thoroughly tested explanation supported by a large body of evidence, like the theory of gravity. A guess is closer to a hypothesis.
- **"Individuals evolve."** Individuals develop and grow, but they do not evolve. Evolution is change in a *population's* inherited traits over generations.
- **"Organisms evolve on purpose, or because they need to."** The variation is already present in a population before the environment changes. A changed environment favors some existing variants; nothing in the organism "tries."
- **"Survival of the fittest means the strongest wins."** Fitness means leaving more surviving offspring. Camouflage, resistance to a disease, or an efficient beak can matter more than strength.
- **"Humans evolved from chimpanzees."** Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor; neither species evolved from the other as it exists today.
- **"Evolution explains how life began."** The theory of evolution explains how populations change and how life diversifies. It does not explain the origin of the first cells.

## Practice

1. During which years did Darwin sail on the *Beagle*, and in what year was *On the Origin of Species* published?
2. List the steps of natural selection in order, starting with variation.
3. In a population of 200 mice, 50 are dark and 150 are light. What percent are dark? After several generations of owls hunting on dark lava rock, would you expect this percent to rise or fall? Explain.
4. Explain why the Grants' finch study is evidence of natural selection and not of individual birds changing.
5. Classify each pair as homologous or analogous: (a) a cat's front leg and a human arm; (b) a fly's wing and a bat's wing.
6. Give one example of a vestigial structure and explain why it counts as evidence of common ancestry.
7. A doctor prescribes an antibiotic. Use natural selection to explain why bacteria that survive a treatment can be harder to treat later.
8. A river changes course and splits a population of salamanders into two groups that never meet. Describe how this could lead to two species.
