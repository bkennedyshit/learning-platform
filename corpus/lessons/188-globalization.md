# 18.8 - Globalization

**Globalization** is the growing connection of the world's economies, cultures, and populations through trade, investment, migration, technology, and shared institutions. Earlier lessons showed older networks of exchange: the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean, and the Atlantic world. What makes recent globalization different is its speed and scale. Goods are designed in one country, assembled in a second, and sold in a hundred more. Billions of people use the same internet. A virus first reported in one city can reach every continent in weeks.

This lesson covers the period from the end of World War II to the present. It is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its historical-thinking skill is **evaluating evidence about the present**: recent history is still unfolding, figures are revised every year, and people disagree about what the trends mean. You will practice stating the source, date, and limits of every number.

**Date note:** the current figures in this lesson were checked against the publishers' websites on **October 19, 2026**. They will change; check the linked sources for newer editions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain how the **Bretton Woods institutions** (1944), **GATT** (1947), and the **WTO** (1995) built a postwar global economic order, and describe what each was designed to do.
2. Analyze the **benefits and costs** of economic globalization using specific evidence, including multinational corporations, China's WTO entry in 2001, and the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse.
3. Interpret **current global indicators** on internet use, extreme poverty, HIV, and climate policy, stating each figure's date, source, and limits.
4. Compare the **HIV/AIDS** and **COVID-19** pandemics as global crises and explain how global connections both spread disease and enabled responses.

## Core ideas

### Building a postwar economic order

Leaders who had lived through the Great Depression and two world wars believed that closed economies and trade wars had helped cause disaster. They built new institutions:

| Institution | Created | Purpose (as described by the source) |
|---|---|---|
| **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** | July 1944, Bretton Woods Conference (New Hampshire) | Oversee "a system of fixed exchange rates centered on the U.S. dollar and gold"; provide short-term financial assistance |
| **World Bank** (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development) | July 1944, Bretton Woods | Finance reconstruction of war-damaged nations and development of poorer countries |
| **GATT** (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade) | Signed by 23 countries on October 30, 1947 | Lower tariffs; its first round covered "about one fifth of the world's total" trade; provided trade rules "from 1948 to 1994" |
| **World Trade Organization (WTO)** | January 1, 1995 | Replaced GATT's provisional system; "the biggest reform of international trade since after the Second World War" |

The fixed exchange-rate system created at Bretton Woods "endured for the better part of three decades" before the exchange crises of 1971 (when President Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold) and 1973, after which floating exchange rates "became the norm." The IMF and World Bank continued with new roles.

### Trade blocs, multinationals, and the rise of China and India

Countries also formed regional trade blocs, such as MERCOSUR in South America (created in 1991), ASEAN in Southeast Asia, and APEC around the Pacific. **China** joined the WTO in **2001**, "a clear sign of its integration into global market systems." China and India, the world's two most populous countries, became major centers of manufacturing, technology, and services after opening their economies to more trade and investment from the late twentieth century.

A **multinational corporation** (MNC) is "a corporate business entity that controls the production of goods and services in multiple countries." Lower trade barriers let MNCs build supply chains across many countries.

| Arguments that globalization benefits people | Arguments that it harms people |
|---|---|
| MNCs bring jobs and "quality consumer goods ... at competitive prices, raising the standard of living" | Workers are often "paid far less than the multinational can afford," prevented from unionizing, and made to work in unsafe conditions |
| Trade growth helped reduce extreme poverty (see the indicator table below) | Production can move away from communities that depended on it |
| Shared technology spreads quickly | Environmental harm, such as deforestation for export agriculture, can be shifted to poorer regions |

On **April 24, 2013**, the **Rana Plaza** building, a complex of garment factories in Bangladesh, collapsed, killing **1,134** workers and injuring thousands of others. The workers made clothing for international brands. The factory owners "knew it had structural problems but still demanded that employees continue to work or lose their jobs." OpenStax compares it to New York's 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire, after which the city set up a Factory Investigating Commission.

### The technological revolution

The internet grew out of **ARPANET**, a U.S. Department of Defense research network developed during the Cold War. Smartphones later spread rapidly, "putting smartphones, and thus internet access, in the pockets of users around the world." In developing countries, they let farmers and traders bank and find customers by phone and let students without libraries study online. During the 2010–2011 protests in Egypt, Tunisia, and other countries, activists used phones and social media to organize.

### Two global health crises

- **HIV/AIDS:** UNAIDS estimates that 92.0 million people have acquired HIV since the start of the epidemic. Treatment expanded greatly, from 7.6 million people on antiretroviral therapy in 2010 to 32.1 million at the end of 2025.
- **COVID-19:** on **January 30, 2020**, the World Health Organization declared the outbreak a **Public Health Emergency of International Concern**; that day, WHO reported 7,818 confirmed cases worldwide, including 82 cases in 18 countries outside China. On **March 11, 2020**, WHO assessed that COVID-19 "can be characterized as a pandemic."

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading a statistic with a range

**Source:** UNAIDS reports "1.2 million [950 000–1.7 million] people newly acquired HIV in 2025."

1. **Identify the parts:** 1.2 million is the best estimate; 950,000 to 1.7 million is the uncertainty range.
2. **Why a range:** many infections are never diagnosed or reported, so the total is modeled from surveys and testing data.
3. **Calculate the width:** 1.7 million − 0.95 million = 0.75 million, a wide range relative to the estimate.
4. **Write it correctly:** "UNAIDS estimates about 1.2 million new HIV infections in 2025 (range 950,000 to 1.7 million)."
5. **Avoid the error:** do not write "exactly 1.2 million people," and do not compare two years' best estimates without checking whether their ranges overlap.

### Example 2: Comparing poverty figures across changing definitions

**Two sources:** OpenStax reports that in 2015 about 85 percent of the world's extreme poor lived in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, using a line of 1.90 dollars a day. The World Bank now reports 837 million people in extreme poverty in 2026, using a line of 3 dollars a day in 2021 prices.

1. **Notice the definitions differ:** the poverty line changed (1.90 to 3 dollars) and so did the price base.
2. **Consequence:** the two figures are not directly comparable. A higher line counts more people as poor, even if no one's income changed.
3. **What can be compared:** trends **within** one consistent series, such as the World Bank's own estimate of about 2.3 billion in 1990 and 837 million in 2026 on the same line.
4. **Calculate that trend:** (2,300 − 837) ÷ 2,300 × 100 ≈ 64 percent decline in the number of people in extreme poverty.
5. **Interpretation:** a large decline over 36 years, but the World Bank warns that "at the current pace of progress, it could take decades to eradicate extreme poverty."

### Example 3: Weighing the Rana Plaza evidence

**Question:** Does the Rana Plaza collapse show that globalization harms workers?

1. **The evidence:** 1,134 workers were killed producing clothing for international brands; the owners knew the building had structural problems but ordered employees to keep working.
2. **What it supports:** global supply chains can push costs and risks onto low-wage workers in poorer countries, where safety enforcement may be weak.
3. **The comparison:** OpenStax links it to the 1911 Triangle fire in New York, which led to a Factory Investigating Commission. Unsafe factories are not unique to globalization; they appeared during industrialization everywhere.
4. **Counterevidence to consider:** OpenStax also notes MNC jobs can pay more than local businesses and lower consumer prices.
5. **Claim:** "Rana Plaza shows that globalized supply chains can allow deadly working conditions to persist far from the consumers who buy the products, though unsafe factories also existed before modern globalization." This is an **interpretation**; people who agree on the facts can still weigh benefits and harms differently.

### Example 4: Comparing two pandemics across centuries

| | Black Death (1340s, Lesson 18.2) | COVID-19 (2020) |
|---|---|---|
| How it spread | Fleas and rats on caravans and ships along protected trade routes | Air travel and human contact in a highly connected world |
| Speed | Several years from Central Asia to Europe | Weeks from first reports to cases in many countries (82 cases in 18 countries outside China by January 30, 2020) |
| Global response | Local measures; no shared scientific understanding of the cause | WHO emergency declaration (January 30, 2020), China's public release of the virus's genetic sequence (January 12, 2020), and international coordination |

1. **Continuity:** connected trade and travel networks spread disease in both cases.
2. **Change:** speed increased dramatically, and so did the capacity for coordinated scientific response.
3. **Claim:** globalization both accelerates the spread of disease and creates the institutions and communication needed to fight it.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Globalization began in the 1990s."** Long-distance exchange is ancient (Lessons 18.2 and 18.4). The postwar period is distinctive for its speed, scale, and institutions.
- **"Globalization benefits everyone equally" or "harms everyone."** Evidence shows both large gains (such as falling extreme poverty) and serious costs (such as unsafe working conditions and environmental damage), unevenly distributed.
- **"A single number tells the whole story."** Internet-use figures do not measure quality or affordability; poverty counts depend on where the line is drawn.
- **"Newer data always means the situation changed."** Sometimes the method or definition changed. Check before comparing.
- **"Recent history is just current events."** Historians apply the same methods: sourcing, corroboration, and context. The difference is that the evidence is still accumulating and interpretations are more likely to change.
- **Boundary: this lesson does not take policy positions.** Debates over trade, technology, climate, and health policy involve values as well as evidence. The lesson provides evidence and methods; you weigh the arguments.

## Practice

1. Match each institution to its purpose: IMF, World Bank, GATT, WTO.
2. Explain why leaders after World War II wanted new international economic institutions. Connect your answer to Lesson 18.7.
3. Give one argument that globalization has benefited people and one that it has harmed people, each with specific evidence from the lesson.
4. Using the ITU 2024 data, calculate the gap in internet use between high-income and low-income countries in percentage points, and explain one thing the figure does not measure.
5. UNAIDS reports 32.1 million people on HIV treatment at the end of 2025 and 7.6 million in 2010. By what factor did treatment grow? What might explain the growth?
6. Explain why OpenStax's 2015 poverty figure and the World Bank's 2026 figure should not be compared directly.
7. **Suggested activity: indicator update (individual, 30 minutes).** Choose one indicator from the table (internet use, extreme poverty, HIV, or Paris Agreement Parties). Visit the linked source and find the most recent figure. Record the figure, its date, and any change in definition or method. Then write two sentences: what the newest figure shows, and one limit of it. If the figure has changed since October 19, 2026, note the change; that is expected.
