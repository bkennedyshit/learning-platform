# 19.1 - Colonial America

North America in 1491 was not an empty wilderness waiting to be "discovered." It was home to many Native nations with their own governments, trade routes, religions, and wars. Over the next 260 years, Spain, France, the Netherlands, and England built colonies on that land, and the meeting of these peoples, with Africans brought by force, produced the societies that later became the United States. This lesson covers that long period, from before European contact to the Albany Plan of 1754, on the eve of the French and Indian War.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). It teaches the history and also the method historians use: reading primary sources (documents created at the time), separating evidence from interpretation, and stating how sure we can be.

## Prerequisites

- No earlier lesson in this course is required. This is the first lesson in [US History](/subject/us-history).
- Basic map reading (the Atlantic coast, the Mississippi River, the Great Lakes, the Rio Grande).
- Helpful but optional: [APP-12.3 — Evidence Analysis & Integration](/app-123-evidence-analysis-integration), which practices quoting and explaining evidence.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Describe the **diversity of Native American societies** before 1492 with at least two specific examples, and explain the **Columbian Exchange**.
2. Compare **Spanish, French, Dutch, and English** colonization goals and methods with specific evidence.
3. Explain why the **New England, Middle, and Southern colonies** developed different economies and labor systems, including how Virginia law made **slavery hereditary** in 1662.
4. Analyze colonial **primary sources** using a four-step method.

## Core ideas

### A method for reading sources

The National Archives teaches the same four steps for every kind of primary source: "Meet the document. Observe its parts. Try to make sense of it. Use it as historical evidence." In practice:

| Step | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Meet the document | What type of source is it? When and where was it made? |
| Observe its parts | Who wrote it, and for whom? What words or details stand out? |
| Try to make sense of it | What was the author trying to do? What was happening at the time? |
| Use it as evidence | What claim about the past can this source support? What can it **not** tell you? |

Use these steps on every document in this lesson.

### North America before 1492

Native American societies differed as much from one another as European kingdoms did. Three examples from *The American Yawp*, a free college-level textbook:

- **Cahokia**, near present-day St. Louis, was the largest Mississippian settlement. Roughly a thousand years ago it "peaked at a population of between ten thousand and thirty thousand," which rivaled European cities of the time. Its residents built large earthen mounds.
- In the **Southwest**, Puebloan peoples farmed in a dry climate and began building highly defensible cliff dwellings in 1190 CE.
- The **Lenape** of the mid-Atlantic organized communities through kinship. Sachems (leaders) spoke for their people in councils that included men, women, and elders, and archaeologists find little evidence of large-scale warfare.

When Europeans arrived, they started what scholars call the **Columbian Exchange**: the "global exchange of people, animals, plants, and microbes" between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres. Horses, wheat, and cattle crossed west; maize, potatoes, and tobacco crossed east. Diseases such as smallpox, to which Native peoples had no prior exposure, killed huge numbers of people. Population figures for this period are estimates, so historians give ranges rather than exact counts.

### Four colonial empires compared

| Empire | Main goals | Methods | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spain | Wealth, territory, Catholic conversion | Military expeditions, **missions**, and the **encomienda** system (grants of Native labor) | By 1680 only about 3,000 colonists lived in Spanish New Mexico, while the Puebloan population had fallen from as many as 60,000 in 1600 to about 17,000 |
| France | Fur trade, Catholic missions | Small trading posts and alliances with Native nations; Jesuit missionaries often lived among Native communities; many traders married Native women | Quebec, founded in 1608 under Samuel de Champlain, became the foothold of New France |
| Netherlands | Trade | Commercial colony of New Netherland, with New Amsterdam on Manhattan | In 1664 the land between the Hudson and Delaware rivers passed to English control under the Duke of York |
| England | Profit, land, and (for some colonists) religious goals | Joint-stock companies, then royal and proprietary colonies; large numbers of settlers who farmed land taken from Native nations | Jamestown (1607); Plymouth (1620); Massachusetts Bay (1630) |

The pattern matters: the French depended on Native trading partners, so they usually had reasons to cooperate. English settlers wanted land for farms, which made conflict over territory more frequent. Violence still occurred in every empire. In 1680 the Puebloan religious leader **Popé** led the **Pueblo Revolt**, which drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for more than a decade, and in 1637 English forces fought the **Pequot War** in southern New England.

### English beginnings: Jamestown and New England

According to the National Park Service, 104 English men and boys arrived in Virginia in 1607 and chose the Jamestown site on May 13. The colony nearly failed. It survived after John Rolfe introduced a new strain of tobacco in 1612; tobacco "became the long awaited cash crop for the Virginia Company," the investors who funded the colony. On July 30, 1619, Governor Yeardley called the first representative legislative assembly in what is now the United States. In the same year, "the first documented Africans were forcibly captured and brought to Virginia to work the tobacco fields." The Park Service notes that historians still debate whether they were treated as indentured servants or as enslaved people at first.

New England began differently. The Pilgrims who landed at Plymouth signed the **Mayflower Compact** in 1620 (Example 1). In 1630, John Winthrop, leader of the Puritans founding Massachusetts Bay, told colonists, "wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us." Puritans sought to build a community that practiced their faith; they did not offer religious freedom to everyone. Roger Williams, exiled from Massachusetts, founded Providence in 1636, and Anne Hutchinson, also exiled, settled nearby.

### Three regions, three economies

| Region | Colonies (examples) | Economy | Labor |
|---|---|---|---|
| New England | Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire | Small family farms in rocky soil, fishing, shipbuilding, trade | Mostly family labor; some servants and enslaved people, especially in port towns |
| Middle | New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware | Grain farms ("breadbasket" colonies), ports such as New York and Philadelphia | Family labor, indentured servants, and enslaved people; very diverse population |
| Southern | Virginia, Maryland, the Carolinas, Georgia | Plantation crops for export: tobacco in the Chesapeake, rice and indigo in South Carolina | Indentured servants early, then increasingly enslaved Africans |

Geography and climate explain part of this pattern (long growing seasons favored export crops), but choices by colonists and laws passed by colonial assemblies explain the rest. Slavery was legal in every colony; it was most central to the Southern economy.

### Empire and colonial self-government

England tried to control colonial trade. The **Navigation Act of 1651** required colonial goods to be shipped to England on English ships, and King Charles II added new Navigation Acts in the 1670s and 1680s. At the same time, colonists ran many local affairs through elected assemblies such as Virginia's. This combination of imperial rules and local self-government shaped later conflicts (Lesson 19.2).

The colonies rarely acted together. On July 10, 1754, with war against France looming, representatives of seven colonies meeting at Albany, New York, adopted the **Albany Plan of Union**, a plan associated with Benjamin Franklin for a more centralized colonial government. The State Department's Office of the Historian calls it "the first important proposal to conceive of the colonies as a collective whole united under one government," but it was "never carried out." The main purpose of the Albany Congress was a treaty with the Iroquois Confederacy, a reminder that Native nations were still major powers in 1754.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading the Mayflower Compact (1620)

1. **Meet the document.** A short written agreement, signed aboard the *Mayflower* in 1620 before the passengers went ashore.
2. **Observe its parts.** The signers call themselves "the Loyal Subjects of our dread Sovereign Lord King James." They "covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil Body Politick" and promise to "enact, constitute, and frame, such just and equal Laws ... as shall be thought most meet and convenient for the general Good of the Colony."
3. **Make sense of it.** The passengers were outside the area their charter covered, so they needed an agreed basis for government. They create a government by mutual consent while still declaring loyalty to the king.
4. **Use it as evidence.** The Compact supports the claim that some colonists founded governments by written agreement. It does **not** show that the colony was a democracy in the modern sense: it was signed by men, it accepted royal authority, and it says nothing about the Wampanoag people whose land the colonists entered.

### Example 2: How a law can show change over time

The Virginia General Assembly passed this act in December 1662:

> "WHEREAS some doubts have arrisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or ffree, Be it therefore enacted ... that all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother."

1. **Observe:** the law begins with "some doubts have arrisen." Before 1662, then, the status of these children was not settled.
2. **Context:** Encyclopedia Virginia explains that this "was a reversal of the usual common law presumption that the status of the child was determined by the father."
3. **Reason about effects:** if status follows the mother, every child of an enslaved woman is enslaved, no matter who the father is. Slavery becomes hereditary, and enslavers gain new enslaved laborers through births.
4. **Claim:** racial slavery in Virginia was **built by law over decades**, not fully formed in 1619. The 1662 act is one step; later laws (such as a 1667 act declaring that baptism did not free enslaved people) added others.

### Example 3: Building a comparative claim

**Question:** Why did French and English colonists usually relate differently to Native nations?

1. **Evidence about France:** the fur trade needed Native hunters and trading partners; French traders "placed a higher value on cooperating with Indigenous people" (*American Yawp*); intermarriage was common.
2. **Evidence about England:** tobacco and family farms needed land, and English colonies grew through large-scale settlement; wars such as the Pequot War (1637) were fought over territory and power.
3. **Claim:** The French colonial economy depended on Native partners, while the English colonial economy depended on Native land, so the English more often pushed Native nations off their land.
4. **Qualify it:** this is a tendency, not a rule. The French also fought wars against Native nations, and English colonists also traded and made alliances.

### Example 4: Weighing two interpretations of Bacon's Rebellion

In 1676–1677, Nathaniel Bacon led Virginia colonists against Governor William Berkeley, after Bacon's followers attacked Native nations, including some of the colony's allies. Bacon's army burned Jamestown; Bacon died in October 1676, and fighting continued into January.

| Interpretation | Evidence offered | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| A. A revolt against English tyranny and an early step toward the American Revolution | Colonists defied the royal governor | Encyclopedia Virginia states this argument "has been discredited": the conflict began with attacks on Native people and became a civil war among colonists |
| B. Part of "a general crisis in Virginia's social, economic, and political arrangements" | Disputes over land, Native policy, and power among colonists | Requires explaining why the crisis took this form |

**Reasoning:** interpretation B fits more of the evidence. This shows how historians revise interpretations when evidence does not support an older story.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Columbus discovered an empty land."** Millions of people already lived in the Americas, in societies as large as Cahokia. "Encounter" or "contact" describes 1492 more accurately.
- **"All colonies were founded for religious freedom."** Jamestown was a business venture of the Virginia Company. Massachusetts Bay sought freedom for Puritan worship, not for everyone, and it exiled dissenters such as Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson.
- **"Slavery in America was fully established in 1619."** The status of the first Africans in Virginia is debated, and laws such as the 1662 act built hereditary racial slavery over decades. This does not lessen the coercion: the 1619 Africans were "forcibly captured."
- **"Slavery existed only in the South."** Slavery was legal in every colony. It was most central to plantation economies in the South.
- **"The Mayflower Compact was a constitution for a democracy."** It was a short agreement among male passengers who declared themselves loyal subjects of the king.
- **"Native peoples were passive victims."** Native nations negotiated, traded, allied, and resisted. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for more than a decade.
- **Boundary: numbers are estimates.** Population figures for 1491–1700 come from limited records and archaeology. When you use a number, say it is an estimate and name the source.
