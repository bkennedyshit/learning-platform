# APP-01.1 — Phonemic Awareness & the Alphabet

Say the word *cat* slowly: /k/ … /a/ … /t/. You just heard three small sounds. Those small sounds are called **phonemes**. A phoneme is the smallest sound in a spoken word. The word *go* has two phonemes, /g/ and /ō/. The word *she* also has two, /sh/ and /ē/, even though it is spelled with three letters.

**Phonemic awareness** means noticing and playing with those sounds when you *hear* and *say* words. You can do it with your eyes closed, because it uses only your ears and your voice. **Letters** are the marks we write to show those sounds. Learning to read starts by putting the two together: hearing the sounds in a word, and knowing which letters stand for them.

This is the first lesson in the Early Literacy & Writing course. The course plan places it in kindergarten. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

None in this course. The learner should be able to listen to and repeat short words. Counting to 10, as in [APP-02.1 — Number Sense & Counting](/app-021-number-sense-counting), helps with counting words, syllables, and sounds.

> **Adult note:** Most activities here are oral: say the words aloud and let the child answer aloud. Slashes like /m/ mean "the sound," not the letter name. Gather 4–6 small counters (buttons, coins, or paper squares) and a sheet of paper with three boxes drawn in a row. For the letter activities, use letter cards or write letters on paper.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Count the words in a short spoken sentence and the syllables (beats) in a spoken word.
2. Say the first, middle, and last sound in a spoken word like *sun*, and blend three spoken sounds into a word.
3. Break a spoken word like *map* into its sounds, pushing one counter into a box for each sound.
4. Name a letter in uppercase and lowercase and say the most common sound it stands for.

## Core ideas

### From big pieces to small pieces

Spoken language can be cut into smaller and smaller pieces:

| Piece | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Word | A sentence is made of words | *The dog ran.* has 3 words |
| Syllable | A beat in a word | *rab-bit* has 2 beats |
| Onset and rime | The first sound(s) before the vowel, and the rest | *c* + *ool* in *cool* |
| Phoneme | The smallest sound | *sun* = /s/ /u/ /n/ |

A U.S. Department of Education practice guide recommends starting with the bigger pieces, which children already know well, and moving to smaller and smaller ones.

### Four sound jobs

- **Isolate:** find one sound. "What is the first sound in *mop*?" /m/.
- **Blend:** push sounds together. "/f/ /i/ /sh/. What word?" *fish*.
- **Segment:** pull a word apart. "Say the sounds in *sit*." /s/ /i/ /t/.
- **Change:** swap one sound. "Say *mat*. Change /m/ to /s/." *sat*.

### Sounds and letters are not the same thing

The number of letters and the number of sounds do not always match:

| Word | Sounds | Number of sounds | Letters |
|---|---|---|---|
| *sun* | /s/ /u/ /n/ | 3 | 3 |
| *ship* | /sh/ /i/ /p/ | 3 | 4 (the *s* and *h* work together) |
| *see* | /s/ /ē/ | 2 | 3 |
| *box* | /b/ /o/ /k/ /s/ | 4 | 3 (the *x* stands for two sounds) |

That is why phonemic awareness is practiced by listening, not by counting letters.

### The alphabet: names, shapes, and sounds

Every letter has a **name** (*bee*), two **shapes** (uppercase *B* and lowercase *b*), and at least one **sound** (/b/). The name and the sound are different. For many letters the name gives a clue: the name *bee* starts with /b/, and the name *em* ends with /m/. Some names give no clue or a wrong clue. The name *aitch* does not contain the /h/ sound, and the name *double-u* does not contain the /w/ sound.

When you say a letter's sound, keep it short and clean. Say /b/, not "buh." Some sounds can be stretched, like /mmm/, /sss/, and /fff/. Others are quick, like /b/, /t/, and /p/.

> **Adult note:** The National Reading Panel found that phonemic awareness and letter knowledge are the two best predictors, at school entry, of how well children learn to read in their first two years. It also found that phonemic awareness instruction works best when it focuses on one or two kinds of sound tasks at a time and, once children know some letters, connects the sounds to letters. Phonemic awareness is one part of a reading program, not the whole program.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Counting words and beats

*Activity to try.* Say: "*The cat sat on a mat.*"

1. Say the sentence again slowly, and hold up one finger for each word: *The* (1) *cat* (2) *sat* (3) *on* (4) *a* (5) *mat* (6).
2. Count the fingers: **6 words**.
3. Now take one long word: *butterfly*. Put a hand under your chin and say it slowly. Your chin drops on each beat: *but-ter-fly*.
4. Count the drops: **3 syllables**.

Check: small words like *a* and *on* still count as words. A word with one beat, like *cat*, has one syllable.

### Example 2: Segmenting with sound boxes

*Activity to try.* Draw three boxes in a row and put a counter above each box.

1. The adult says a word: *fan*. The learner repeats it slowly, stretching it: /fffaaannn/.
2. On the first sound, /f/, push the first counter into the first box.
3. On /a/, push the second counter into the middle box.
4. On /n/, push the third counter into the last box.
5. Slide a finger under the boxes from left to right and say the sounds faster and faster until they become the word again: *fan*.

Reasoning: the word *fan* was picked because /f/, /a/, and /n/ can all be stretched, which makes each sound easier to hear. Three counters means three sounds. Once the learner knows the letters *f*, *a*, and *n*, the counters can be replaced with letter cards. This connects the sounds to the letters that spell them.

### Example 3: Blending sounds into a word

*Activity to try.* The adult says sounds with a short pause between them. The learner says the word.

1. /m/ … /o/ … /p/. Hold the sounds in your head in order: first /m/, then /o/, then /p/.
2. Put the first two together: /mo/.
3. Add the last sound: /mo/ + /p/ = *mop*.
4. Ask: "Is *mop* a real word I know?" Yes. Done.

If the word does not make sense, say the sounds again and listen for one you missed.

### Example 4: Matching a letter to its sound

*Activity to try.* Show the cards **S s**.

1. Point to the uppercase *S*: "This letter's name is *es*."
2. Point to the lowercase *s*: "This is the same letter, written small."
3. Say the sound: /sss/, like a snake.
4. Think of words that start with /s/: *sun*, *sock*, *sit*.
5. Check the name clue: the name *es* ends with the /s/ sound.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Phonemic awareness is the same as knowing letters."** Phonemic awareness is about sounds you hear. A child can do it in the dark. Phonics, the next lesson, connects the sounds to letters.
- **"Every letter makes one sound, and every sound is one letter."** In *ship*, two letters (*sh*) make one sound. In *box*, one letter (*x*) makes two sounds.
- **"The letter name is the sound."** The letter *w* is named *double-u*, but its sound is /w/. The letter *h* is named *aitch*, but its sound is /h/.
- **"Adding *uh* makes the sound clearer."** Saying "buh-a-tuh" for *bat* makes the sounds hard to blend. Short, clean sounds blend into the word.
- **"Rhyming words have the same first sound."** Rhyming words share the *ending* part: *cat*, *hat*, and *sat* rhyme. *Cat* and *cup* start the same but do not rhyme.
- **"*b* and *d* are the same letter facing different ways."** They are different letters with different sounds. Mixing them up is common for beginners and gets better with practice.

> **Adult note:** Sounds can differ a little across dialects and accents, so accept the child's natural pronunciation of words. If a child still cannot hear or repeat sounds after many weeks of short, regular practice, talk with their teacher. They can check hearing and early reading skills. This lesson does not diagnose reading difficulties.

## Practice

1. How many words are in this sentence: "*I like red apples.*"?
2. Clap the syllables. How many beats are in *pencil*? In *dog*? In *banana*?
3. What is the first sound in *sun*? The last sound in *dog*? The middle sound in *sit*?
4. Blend these sounds into a word: /f/ /i/ /sh/.
5. Push one counter for each sound. How many sounds are in *map*? In *ship*?
6. Say *mat*. Now change /m/ to /h/. What is the new word?
7. Which word rhymes with *hen*: *ten*, *hat*, or *hop*?
8. Write the uppercase and lowercase letter for the sound at the start of *top*.

<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. 4 words: *I*, *like*, *red*, *apples*.
2. *pen-cil* has 2 beats; *dog* has 1; *ba-na-na* has 3.
3. First sound in *sun*: /s/. Last sound in *dog*: /g/. Middle sound in *sit*: /i/.
4. *fish*.
5. *map* has 3 sounds: /m/ /a/ /p/. *ship* has 3 sounds: /sh/ /i/ /p/, even though it has 4 letters.
6. *hat*.
7. *ten*. It shares the ending /en/ with *hen*.
8. **T t**.

**Adult self-check:** Watch for whether the child answers with *sounds* (/s/) or *letter names* ("es") in items 3–5. Both are useful, but these items ask for sounds. A child who can do items 1–2 but not 3–6 is still working at the word and syllable level; keep practicing with stretchy sounds like /m/, /s/, and /f/ before quick sounds like /t/ and /p/.
