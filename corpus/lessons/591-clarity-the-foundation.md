# 59.1 - Clarity — The Foundation

A sentence is **clear** when its intended reader can tell, on the first reading, who is doing what, to whom, and why it matters. Clarity is judged by the reader, not by the writer: a sentence that is obvious to you may be opaque to someone who lacks your background. The UNC Writing Center puts the goal plainly: in academic writing it is "not to sound intelligent, but to get your intelligent point across." The same is true of reports, documentation, proposals, and email.

This is the first lesson of the Writing Craft & Rhetoric course, written for college students and working adults who write for academic or professional readers. Every later lesson (rhythm, argument, voice, documentation, copywriting, editing, digital writing) assumes the habits taught here.

## Prerequisites

You should be able to find the subject and main verb of a sentence and tell an independent clause from a dependent one ([38.1 — Grammar Foundations: Parts of Speech & Sentence Structure](/381-grammar-foundations-parts-of-speech-sentence-structure)). Familiarity with register and word precision ([38.3 — Vocabulary: Roots, Precision & Register](/383-vocabulary-roots-precision-register)) helps. If you have not met active and passive voice before, the short introduction in [APP-12.8 — Advanced Rhetoric & Style](/app-128-advanced-rhetoric-style) is a good warm-up.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Diagnose an unclear sentence by finding its **actors and actions**, then rewrite it so the main actor is the subject and the main action is the verb.
2. Replace **hidden verbs** (nominalizations), **stock phrases**, and needless **qualifiers**, and explain what each change does for the reader.
3. Choose **active or passive voice** on purpose.
4. Find **curse-of-knowledge** gaps in a draft and revise it for a named reader.

## Core ideas

### Actors and actions

Most unclear prose hides its story. Readers expect the grammatical **subject** to name the actor and the **verb** to name the action. When the action is buried in a noun and the actor is missing or tucked into a prepositional phrase, the reader has to reconstruct the sentence before understanding it. The UNC Writing Center's handout on style suggests a diagnostic question for exactly this problem: *Who did what to whom?* Answer it, then make the answer the sentence.

### Hidden verbs (nominalizations)

A **nominalization** is a verb or adjective turned into a noun: *decide* becomes *decision*, *analyze* becomes *analysis*, *fail* becomes *failure*. The U.S. government's plain-language guidance on Digital.gov calls these **hidden verbs** and notes two signs: endings such as *-ment*, *-tion*, *-sion*, and *-ance*, and partner verbs such as *achieve*, *effect*, *give*, *have*, *make*, *reach*, and *take*. Its example: write *We analyze data*, not *We conduct an analysis of the data*.

George Orwell made the same complaint in "Politics and the English Language" (*Horizon*, April 1946). He called phrases like *render inoperative*, *militate against*, *give rise to*, and *have the effect of* "verbal false limbs" and said their keynote "is the elimination of simple verbs." Nominalizations are not always wrong; a sentence about a concept (*Inflation erodes savings*) needs the noun. Revise them when they hide an action that a person or thing actually performs.

### Stock phrases, qualifiers, and needless words

William Strunk Jr.'s rule 13 in *The Elements of Style* is "Omit needless words," and he explains that this "requires not that the writer make all his sentences short ... but that he make every word tell." UNC's style handout lists common substitutions: *due to the fact that* → *because*; *in the event that* → *if*; *is able to* → *can*; *it is important that* → *must* or *should*. It also flags piles of qualifiers (*very*, *basically*, *really*, *generally*) and redundant pairs (*hopes and dreams*, *whole entire*). Keep a qualifier when it is true and the reader needs it; *most patients* and *all patients* make different claims.

### Concrete and abstract language

Orwell's most famous demonstration translates a verse from Ecclesiastes ("the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong...") into what he calls "modern English of the worst sort": "Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity..." He observes that the original has 49 words but only 60 syllables and six vivid images, while the parody has 38 words and 90 syllables and "not a single fresh, arresting phrase." The parody is *shorter* and still less clear. Clarity comes from concrete nouns and real verbs, not from a low word count.

### Orwell's six rules, read as tools

Orwell closes his essay with six rules: (i) never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech you are used to seeing in print; (ii) never use a long word where a short one will do; (iii) if it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out; (iv) never use the passive where you can use the active; (v) never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent; and (vi) "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." He adds that "one could keep all of them and still write bad English." Treat the rules as revision prompts, not laws. Rule (vi) is the important one: the reader's understanding outranks every rule.

### Active and passive voice

In the **active voice** the subject performs the action (*The committee approved the budget*). In the **passive voice** the subject receives it (*The budget was approved [by the committee]*). Digital.gov gives a quick test: a passive sentence usually has a form of *to be* plus a past participle. Its guidance favors the active voice because it "makes it clear who should do what" and warns not to confuse the passive with the past tense.

The passive is not a grammatical error; UNC lists that belief as a myth. UNC gives three good reasons to use it: to **emphasize the object** (*One hundred votes are required to pass the bill*), when the **actor is unknown** (*Over 120 different contaminants have been dumped into the river*), and when **readers don't need to know who acted**. Strunk makes a subtler point: choose the voice that puts the paragraph's topic in subject position. *The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day* suits a paragraph about those dramatists; *Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration* suits a paragraph about modern readers. Avoid the passive when it hides responsibility the reader needs, as in *Mistakes were made*, an example UNC cites under Orwell's "swindles and perversions."

### The curse of knowledge

Economists Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber named the **curse of knowledge** in a 1989 *Journal of Political Economy* paper. Their abstract states the core finding: "Better-informed agents are unable to ignore private information even when it is in their interest to do so." Writers suffer from it constantly: you skip a step because it feels obvious, use an acronym you use every day, or refer to "the issue" as if the reader were in yesterday's meeting. UNC's handout on audience describes the effect on drafts: when you reread your own work, you skip gaps "because the gap-filler is in your head."

Two habits counter it. First, write for a **named reader**: UNC recommends imagining an "intelligent but uninformed audience," such as a smart classmate outside your field. Second, test the draft on that reader, or simulate one by reading aloud slowly and by waiting before you reread.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Finding the actors and actions

**Illustrative draft sentence (26 words):** *The implementation of the new scheduling policy was the subject of a decision by the committee following a review of the feedback provided by staff members.*

1. **Find the actions.** They are hidden in nouns: *implementation* (implement), *decision* (decide), *review* (review), *feedback* (staff gave feedback).
2. **Find the actors.** *The committee* decides and reviews; *staff members* gave feedback. The committee is buried after *by*.
3. **Order the story.** The committee reviewed feedback, then decided to implement the policy.
4. **Rewrite with actor as subject and action as verb (13 words):** *After reviewing staff feedback, the committee decided to implement the new scheduling policy.*

Reasoning: the revision halves the length, but that is a side effect. The main gain is that the reader meets the actor (*the committee*) and the action (*decided*) in the main clause instead of decoding a chain of nouns joined by *of*, *by*, and *following*.

### Example 2: Cutting stock phrases without losing meaning

**Illustrative draft (32 words):** *It is important to note that there is a possibility that the delivery of the shipment may be subject to a delay due to the fact that the weather conditions are adverse.*

| Phrase | Problem | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| *It is important to note that* | announces importance instead of showing it | delete |
| *there is a possibility that ... may* | says "possibly" twice | *may* |
| *the delivery of the shipment ... be subject to a delay* | hidden verb (*delay*) | *the shipment may be late* |
| *due to the fact that* | stock phrase | *because of* |
| *the weather conditions are adverse* | abstract | *bad weather* |

**Revision (9 words):** *The shipment may be late because of bad weather.*

Reasoning: each change removes words that carried no information, and the remaining sentence keeps the single qualifier (*may*) that is true. Cutting *may* as well would change the claim, which is why concision is a judgment and not a word quota.

### Example 3: Choosing the voice

| Sentence | Decision and reason |
|---|---|
| *In the event that the system experiences a failure, notification of the on-call engineer should be made by the monitoring service.* (21 words) | Revise. The actor (the monitoring service) is known and the reader needs it: *If the system fails, the monitoring service should notify the on-call engineer.* (12 words) |
| *The samples were stored at 4 °C for 48 hours.* | Keep the passive in a methods section if your field expects it; the samples, not the technician, are the topic. |
| *The library's rare-book room was flooded overnight.* | Keep. The cause is unknown at the time of writing. |
| *Errors were made in the grading of the exam.* | Revise if you know who made them. Readers who were affected need the actor: *The teaching assistants made errors when grading the exam.* |

### Example 4: Revising for a named reader

**Illustrative message from an engineer to a new volunteer:** *Once the PR clears CI, push to staging and ping #ops if the smoke tests flake.*

**Named reader:** a volunteer on day one who knows basic Git but not this team's tools.

**Gaps:** *PR*, *CI*, *staging*, *#ops*, *smoke tests*, *flake*, and the unstated step of how to know the PR "cleared."

**Revision:** *After you open a pull request, wait until all the automated checks show a green check mark. Then deploy it to the staging server (the test copy of the site) with the command in the README. If the quick post-deployment tests fail and then pass when you rerun them without any change, post the link to the failed run in the #ops chat channel.*

Reasoning: the revision is longer, and that is correct. Clarity sometimes means **adding** the definitions and steps the reader lacks. A teammate who already knows the vocabulary would find the original clearer; the right version depends on who reads it.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Clear writing is simple writing for simple ideas."** UNC's word-choice handout says, "Using simple words does not indicate simple thoughts." A complex idea needs clear sentences more, not less.
- **"Shorter is always clearer."** Orwell's parody is shorter than the verse and much less clear, and Example 4 got clearer by getting longer. Cut words that carry nothing; add words the reader needs.
- **"Never use the passive voice."** UNC lists this as a myth, and Orwell's own rule (iv) is limited by rule (vi). Use the passive when the object is the topic, the actor is unknown, or the actor does not matter.
- **"Every nominalization is a mistake."** Some ideas are things: *inflation*, *evolution*, *a decision* that a later sentence discusses. Revise nominalizations that hide an action someone performs.
- **"Jargon is always bad."** UNC notes that you may need technical terms that are important in your field. The test is the reader: define a term for outsiders, and use it without apology for specialists who expect it.
- **Boundary: readability scores.** Tools that estimate reading difficulty from sentence length and word length (such as the Gunning Fog Index or Flesch-Kincaid formulas) can flag long sentences, but they cannot tell whether a sentence names its actor, defines its terms, or makes sense. Use them as hints, not verdicts.
- **Boundary: rules versus conventions.** Some fields and publications require the passive in methods sections or forbid contractions. When an instructor, journal, or style guide sets a convention, follow it and apply clarity within it.

## Practice

1. Rewrite each sentence so the actor is the subject and the action is the verb: (a) *A determination was made by the review board that the application lacked sufficient documentation.* (b) *There was a discussion among the team members about the possibility of a delay in the launch.* (c) *The provision of feedback to students is the responsibility of teaching assistants.*
2. Replace each stock phrase with one or two words: *prior to*; *in the event that*; *has the ability to*; *with regard to*; *despite the fact that*; *at this point in time*.
3. For each sentence, decide whether to keep the passive or make it active, and give your reason: (a) *The vaccine schedule was changed last spring.* (b) *Your account has been suspended.* (c) *The bridge was completed in 1937.* (d) *The decision was made to cut the program.*
4. The word *very* appears in each sentence. Replace the phrase with a precise word or delete *very* and explain why: (a) *The results were very important.* (b) *She was very tired after the shift.* (c) *The deadline is very close.*
5. **Curse of knowledge.** Rewrite this note for a first-year student who has never used a library database: *Run your search in the discovery layer, then filter to peer-reviewed and pull the full text through the resolver.*
6. **Extended task.** Choose about 250 words of your own recent writing (a paper, report, or work email). Revise it using this lesson's four moves (actors and actions, hidden verbs, stock phrases, voice) and keep a revision log listing at least five changes and the reason for each. > **Integrity note:** Clarity edits belong in your own prose and your paraphrases. Do not "clarify" a direct quotation by changing its words; quote exactly, or paraphrase and cite. When you paraphrase a source more clearly than the original, keep the author's meaning and still give credit.
