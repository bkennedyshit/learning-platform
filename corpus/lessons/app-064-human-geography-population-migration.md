# APP-06.4 — Human Geography — Population & Migration

There are about 8.2 billion people on Earth, but they are spread very unevenly. Some places are crowded cities; others, such as polar regions and dry deserts, have almost no one. Population geography asks where people live, why, how populations grow or shrink, and why people move. These questions shape everything from school construction to food supply to national politics.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its thinking skill is **reading population numbers critically**: every population statistic depends on a definition (what counts as a "city" or a "migrant"?) and a date, and the same data can look very different depending on how they are measured.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Describe where the world's people live, calculate **arithmetic and physiological population density**, and explain what each measure reveals and hides.
2. Calculate the **rate of natural increase** and **doubling time** from birth and death rates, and interpret fertility, replacement level, and the projected peak of world population using UN data.
3. Explain the stages of the **demographic transition model**, read **population pyramids**, and evaluate the model's limits.
4. Classify **migration** with push and pull factors and the terms international migrant, refugee, asylum-seeker, and internally displaced person, and interpret current UN migration and **urbanization** figures, including megacities.

## Core ideas

### Where people live and population density

People cluster where land, water, climate, and economic opportunity support them: river valleys, fertile plains, and coasts. Large areas of polar land, high mountains, and deserts are sparsely populated.

**Population density** is the number of people divided by the area of land, often given as people per square kilometer. National Geographic's education site points out two cautions and two alternative measures:

- Density "is more useful when applied to small areas, such as neighborhoods, than to entire regions or countries," because a country's average can combine a crowded city with an empty desert.
- **Physiological density** is the number of people per unit of **arable** (farmable) land. It shows how much pressure a population puts on its farmland.
- **Agricultural density** compares the number of farmers or rural residents with the amount of arable land.

### Measuring population change

The free textbook *Introduction to Sociology* from OpenStax defines the basic measures:

- **Crude birth rate (CBR):** live births per 1,000 people per year.
- **Crude death rate (CDR):** deaths per 1,000 people per year.
- **Rate of natural increase (RNI):** (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10, expressed as a percent. It ignores migration.
- **Total fertility rate (TFR):** the average number of children born per woman.

A population growing at a steady rate doubles in roughly **70 ÷ growth rate (in percent)** years. This "rule of 70" is an approximation that works well for small rates.

**What the UN reports.** The *World Population Prospects 2024* (UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division) projects that:

- World population, **8.2 billion in 2024**, will peak at about **10.3 billion in the mid-2080s** and then decline slightly to about 10.2 billion by 2100.
- The global fertility rate is **2.25** live births per woman, down from 3.31 in 1990.
- More than half of all countries have fertility below the **replacement level of 2.1**, the level needed for a population to stay constant in the long run without migration.
- In 63 countries and areas, containing 28 percent of the world's people in 2024, population has **already peaked**; this group includes China, Germany, Japan, and the Russian Federation.
- Over one in ten countries, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, still have fertility of four or more births per woman.
- **Population momentum**, the effect of today's large young generations reaching parenthood age, will account for 79 percent of world population growth through 2054. Populations keep growing for decades even after fertility falls to replacement.

### The demographic transition model

The **demographic transition** describes how birth and death rates change as societies industrialize and modernize. Max Roser of *Our World in Data* summarizes it in five stages; the key insight is that **death rates fall first, then birth rates**, and rapid growth happens in the gap between them.

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Growth | Population pyramid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High | High | Very slow | Broad base, narrowing quickly toward the top |
| 2 | High | Falling | **Rapid** | Very broad base |
| 3 | Falling | Low | Slowing | Base begins to narrow |
| 4 | Low | Low | Little or none | Box-shaped: age groups similar in size until old age |
| 5 | Low, possibly below replacement | Low | Uncertain: may decline | Narrower base than middle |

OpenStax describes a four-stage version and gives examples: the United States in the 1800s (stage 1), Afghanistan (stage 2), Mexico (stage 3), and Sweden (stage 4). Different textbooks use different numbers of stages, and Roser notes that what happens at very high levels of development "is not a question we can answer with certainty."

A **population pyramid** is a bar graph of a population's age and sex structure, with males on one side and females on the other, youngest at the bottom. Its shape is a snapshot of past births, deaths, and migration.

### Migration

**Migration** is a change of residence. Geographers classify it in several ways:

- **Internal** (within a country) or **international** (across a border). For statistics, the UN defines an **international migrant** as "any person who has changed his or her country of residence," regardless of legal status.
- **Voluntary** or **forced**. A **refugee**, under the definition the UN cites from the 1951 Refugee Convention, is someone outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion." An **asylum-seeker** has applied for protection but has not yet been recognized as a refugee. An **internally displaced person (IDP)** has been forced from home but has not crossed an international border.
- **Push factors** drive people away from a place (conflict, persecution, lack of jobs, disasters). **Pull factors** attract people to a place (jobs, safety, family, education). Most moves involve both, along with obstacles such as cost, distance, and immigration laws.

In 1885 the British demographer Ernst Ravenstein proposed "laws of migration," such as "most move only a short distance" and "each migration flow produces a counter-flow." The open textbook *Introduction to Human Geography* (Dorrell and Henderson) notes that many still hold, but his claim that women migrate more than men has not consistently held, since slightly more men than women move internationally. The UN reports that women were 48 percent of international migrants in 2024.

**Current figures (as reported by the UN, checked 2026-10-19):**

- International migrants: **304 million in 2024**, 3.7 percent of the world's population, nearly double the 154 million (2.9 percent) of 1990.
- Forced displacement: at the end of 2025, an estimated **117.8 million** people were forcibly displaced by persecution, conflict, violence, and human-rights violations. The UN calls this "the first decline in global forced displacement in a decade," though numbers remain historically high. Among them were **41.6 million** refugees and others in need of protection across international borders, plus tens of millions of IDPs and millions of asylum-seekers.

### Urbanization and megacities

**Urbanization** is the growth in the share of people living in urban areas. Measuring it depends on what counts as "urban," and countries define it differently. The UN's *World Urbanization Prospects 2025* used, for the first time, a harmonized method called the **Degree of Urbanization**. It classifies all land into **cities** (contiguous 1-km² grid cells with at least 1,500 people per km² and at least 50,000 people in total), **towns and semi-dense areas**, and **rural areas**.

| Measure (WUP 2025) | 1950 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Share of world population in cities | 20% | **45%** |
| Share in towns | 40% | 36% |
| Share in rural areas | about 38% (the UN says the rural share "fell by half") | 19% |

- **Megacities** (10 million or more people) grew from 8 in 1975 to **33 in 2025**, 19 of them in Asia, and are projected to reach 37 by 2050. The largest are **Jakarta** (nearly 42 million), **Dhaka** (37 million), and **Tokyo** (33 million). Dhaka is expected to become the largest by mid-century, while Tokyo's population is projected to shrink.
- Most city dwellers do not live in megacities: of the world's 12,000 cities in 2025, 96 percent had fewer than 1 million people.
- The world's rural population is expected to peak in the 2040s.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Two densities, two stories

**Problem:** Country X has 40 million people and 800,000 km² of land, of which 5 percent is arable. Country Y has 40 million people and 200,000 km², of which 40 percent is arable. Compare their arithmetic and physiological densities. (These are hypothetical countries.)

1. **Arithmetic density:** X: 40,000,000 ÷ 800,000 = **50 people per km²**. Y: 40,000,000 ÷ 200,000 = **200 per km²**.
2. **Arable land:** X: 5% × 800,000 = 40,000 km². Y: 40% × 200,000 = 80,000 km².
3. **Physiological density:** X: 40,000,000 ÷ 40,000 = **1,000 per km² of arable land**. Y: 40,000,000 ÷ 80,000 = **500**.
4. **Interpret:** X looks less crowded by arithmetic density, but each square kilometer of its farmland must support twice as many people. X may depend more on imported food or intensive farming.
5. **Limit the claim:** neither number shows how people are distributed inside the country, whether they live in cities, or whether food is traded.

### Example 2: Natural increase and doubling time

**Problem:** Country P has a crude birth rate of 30 and a crude death rate of 8 (per 1,000). Country Q has a CBR of 9 and a CDR of 11. Find each rate of natural increase and, where it applies, the doubling time.

1. **P:** (30 − 8) ÷ 10 = **2.2 percent** per year. Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ 2.2 ≈ **32 years**.
2. **Q:** (9 − 11) ÷ 10 = **−0.2 percent**. Deaths exceed births, so there is **natural decrease**; doubling time does not apply.
3. **Place in the model:** P fits stage 2 (high births, low deaths). Q fits stage 5 (births below deaths).
4. **Limit the claim:** RNI ignores migration. Q's population could still grow if immigration exceeds the natural decrease.

### Example 3: Same world, different "urban" numbers

**Problem:** A 2018 UN release reported that "55% of the world's population lives in urban areas." The 2025 UN report says cities are home to **45 percent** of the world's people. Did the world become less urban?

1. **Check the definitions.** The 2018 figure used each country's own definition of "urban." The 2025 figure uses the harmonized Degree of Urbanization, in which "cities" are only the densest, largest settlements, and **towns** are a separate category.
2. **Add the categories:** in 2025, cities (45%) + towns (36%) = **81 percent** of people living in cities or towns.
3. **Conclusion:** the numbers measure different things. The world did not become less urban; the 2025 method separates cities from towns and applies the same rule to every country.
4. **General rule:** before comparing two statistics, check that they use the same definition, the same date, and the same geographic units.

### Example 4: Reading migration statistics

**Problem:** Use UN figures to answer: (a) What share of the world's people were international migrants in 2024? (b) By how much did the number grow since 1990? (c) What share of the forcibly displaced at the end of 2025 were refugees and others needing protection across borders?

1. **(a)** 304 million ÷ 8.2 billion ≈ 0.037, or **3.7 percent**, matching the UN's figure. In other words, about 96 percent of the world's people were not international migrants.
2. **(b)** 304 ÷ 154 ≈ **1.97**, so the number nearly doubled, while the *share* rose only from 2.9 to 3.7 percent because world population also grew.
3. **(c)** 41.6 ÷ 117.8 ≈ 0.35, or about **35 percent**. Most of the rest were internally displaced, people who never crossed a border.
4. **Interpret:** large absolute numbers and small shares can both be true. Saying which you are reporting avoids misleading readers.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The world's population will keep growing forever."** The UN projects a peak around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, and 63 countries and areas have already peaked.
- **"If fertility falls to replacement, growth stops immediately."** Population momentum keeps populations growing for decades because many young people are still reaching parenthood age.
- **"Most displaced people are refugees."** Of the 117.8 million people forcibly displaced at the end of 2025, about 35 percent (41.6 million) were refugees and others needing protection across borders; most of the rest were internally displaced, inside their own countries.
- **"Migrant," "refugee," and "asylum-seeker" mean the same thing.** They have distinct definitions. The UN's statistical definition of an international migrant includes people regardless of legal status or reason for moving.
- **"The demographic transition is a law every country must follow."** It is a model based largely on European history. Stage 5 is uncertain, and countries move through stages at different speeds.
- **"Megacities are where most urban people live."** Most city dwellers live in small and medium-sized cities.
- **Boundary: definitions change the numbers.** Urban shares, migrant counts, and density figures all depend on definitions and dates. Always report both.

## Practice

1. Country Z has 12 million people on 300,000 km², of which 10 percent is arable. Find its arithmetic and physiological densities.
2. A country has a CBR of 22 and a CDR of 7. Find the RNI and estimate the doubling time. Which demographic-transition stage does it most likely fit?
3. Describe the population pyramid you would expect for (a) a stage 2 country and (b) a stage 4 country, and explain each shape.
4. The UN reports a global fertility rate of 2.25 and a replacement level of 2.1. Explain why world population is still projected to grow until the 2080s.
5. Classify each person and name one push or pull factor: (a) a nurse who moves from one country to another for a better-paid job; (b) a family that flees fighting to another part of its own country; (c) a journalist who crosses a border after receiving threats and applies for protection, still awaiting a decision.
6. Using the WUP 2025 figures, how many people (in billions) lived in cities in 2025? In towns? In rural areas? (World population 8.2 billion.)
7. A news headline says, "World becomes less urban: only 45% live in cities." Write a two-sentence correction.
8. **Suggested activity: population-pyramid comparison (pairs, 30–40 minutes).** If your school allows it, use the UN *World Population Prospects* website or a printed atlas to find population pyramids for two countries at different stages, for example one country with fertility above four and one whose population has already peaked. Sketch both, label the stage you think each fits, and list two pieces of evidence from the shapes. Write one question the pyramids cannot answer. Use public data only; you do not need to share any personal or family information.
