# APP-04.2 — Energy — Forms & Transfers

**Energy** is what makes change happen. It takes energy to move a car, warm soup, light a room, or make a sound. Energy comes in different forms, it can move from one object to another, and it can change from one form into another. It never simply disappears. This lesson follows energy through toys, flashlights, and simple machines.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 3-5. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-04.1 — Forces & Motion — Foundations](/app-041-forces-motion-foundations): forces as pushes and pulls, friction, and gravity. Multiplication and division from [APP-02.3 — Multiplication & Division](/app-023-multiplication-division) are used for the machine calculations.

> **Adult note:** Useful materials: a ball that bounces, a flashlight, a rubber band, a ruler and a pencil (for a lever), a board and books (for a ramp), and a string or a spring scale if you have one. Scientists measure energy in **joules** (J) and force in **newtons** (N). One newton is about the weight of a small apple. The calculations here only need multiplication and division.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Name at least six forms of energy and point to each in a real object.
2. Draw an energy chain for a device and label each step as a transfer or a transformation.
3. Explain, using conservation of energy, why a dropped ball never bounces back higher than where it started.
4. Name the six simple machines and calculate how much a lever or ramp multiplies a force.

## Core ideas

### Forms of energy

Energy is often sorted into two big groups. **Potential energy** is stored energy, or energy of position. **Kinetic energy** is energy of motion.

| Form | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Motion (kinetic) | Energy of a moving object | A rolling skateboard |
| Gravitational (potential) | Energy stored by height | A book on a high shelf |
| Elastic (potential) | Energy stored by stretching or squeezing | A stretched rubber band |
| Chemical (potential) | Energy stored in substances | Food, a battery, wood |
| Thermal | Energy of the moving particles that make up matter; hotter means more | Hot cocoa |
| Light (radiant) | Energy carried by light | Sunlight, a lamp |
| Sound | Energy carried by vibrations | A drum |
| Electrical | Energy carried by moving electric charges | Current in a wire |

### Transfers and transformations

- A **transfer** moves energy from one object to another. A bowling ball hits a pin and the pin starts moving. Warm hands warm a cold cup.
- A **transformation** changes energy from one form to another. A flashlight turns electrical energy into light and thermal energy.

Thermal energy always moves by itself from a hotter object to a colder one, until both are the same temperature. That is why an ice cube melts in your warm hand, and why your hand feels cold.

### Conservation of energy

The **law of conservation of energy** says energy is not created or destroyed. It only moves or changes form. When something seems to "lose" energy, look for where it went. Most often it became thermal energy (things got a little warmer) or sound.

In everyday talk, "saving energy" means using less electricity or fuel. In science, "conservation of energy" is this law, which is a different idea.

### Simple machines

A machine makes work easier. In science, **work** means using a force to move something: work = force × distance. A simple machine does not give you free energy. It lets you use **less force over a longer distance** (or the reverse).

The six simple machines are the **lever**, **wheel and axle**, **pulley**, **inclined plane** (ramp), **wedge**, and **screw**. A wedge is two ramps back to back, and a screw is a ramp wrapped around a post.

The **ideal mechanical advantage (IMA)** tells how many times a perfect machine (with no friction) multiplies your force:

- Lever: IMA = (distance from the pivot to your push) ÷ (distance from the pivot to the load).
- Ramp: IMA = (length of the ramp) ÷ (height it rises).

Real machines always have some friction, so they do a little worse than the ideal number.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: The energy chain in a flashlight

1. **Start:** the battery stores chemical energy.
2. **Transformation:** when the switch closes, chemical energy becomes electrical energy in the wires.
3. **Transfer:** the electrical energy moves through the wires to the bulb.
4. **Transformation:** the bulb turns electrical energy into light and thermal energy. (Touch an old-style bulb after a while and it feels warm.)
5. **Check:** the energy at the start equals all of the energy at the end: light + thermal. None vanished.

### Example 2: Why a bouncing ball bounces lower

*Activity to try.* Hold a ball beside a ruler or a wall and drop it from a marked height. Watch how high it bounces.

1. **At the top:** the ball is still, so it has gravitational potential energy and no motion energy.
2. **Falling:** height turns into speed. Gravitational energy becomes motion energy.
3. **Hitting the floor:** the ball squashes, so motion energy becomes elastic energy, plus a little sound (you hear the bounce) and a little thermal energy.
4. **Going up:** the elastic energy turns back into motion, then into height.
5. **Conclusion:** some energy left as sound and thermal energy at each bounce, so less is left for height. Each bounce is lower. Conservation of energy predicts the ball can never bounce **higher** than where it was dropped unless something adds energy, like a throw.

### Example 3: A lever lifts a heavy rock

*Illustrative example.* A rock pushes down with a force of 60 newtons. A crowbar rests on a pivot. The rock is 30 centimeters from the pivot, and you push down 120 centimeters from the pivot.

1. IMA = 120 cm ÷ 30 cm = 4.
2. Ideal push needed: 60 N ÷ 4 = 15 N.
3. The trade: your end of the bar moves 4 times as far as the rock end. If the rock lifts 5 cm, your hand moves 4 × 5 = 20 cm.
4. Check with work: rock side 60 N × 5 cm = 300; your side 15 N × 20 cm = 300. The work is the same. The lever made the force smaller, not the work.
5. Boundary: a real crowbar has a little friction at the pivot, so you would push slightly more than 15 N.

### Example 4: Using a ramp instead of lifting

*Illustrative example.* A box weighs 100 N. You need to raise it 1 meter onto a stage. A ramp is 4 meters long.

1. Lifting straight up: force 100 N over 1 m.
2. Ramp IMA = 4 m ÷ 1 m = 4.
3. Ideal push along the ramp: 100 N ÷ 4 = 25 N, but over 4 m instead of 1 m.
4. Work check: 100 N × 1 m = 100 joules, and 25 N × 4 m = 100 joules. Same work, smaller force.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Energy gets used up and disappears."** Energy changes form. A car that coasts to a stop turned its motion energy into thermal energy in the brakes, tires, and road.
- **"Machines give you extra energy."** A machine can make the force smaller, but you must push over a longer distance. A real machine always gives back a little less useful energy than you put in, because of friction.
- **"Cold flows into things."** Thermal energy flows from hot to cold. A metal spoon in ice water feels cold because energy leaves your hand into the spoon.
- **"Only moving things have energy."** A stretched rubber band, a battery, and a book on a high shelf all store energy even though they are still.
- **"Heat and temperature are the same thing."** Temperature tells how hot something is. A bathtub of warm water holds much more thermal energy than a cup of hotter water, because it has far more water.

## Practice

1. Name the main form of energy in: (a) a wound-up toy spring (b) a sandwich (c) a car driving (d) a ringing bell.
2. Draw an energy chain for a toaster that is plugged in. Label each step "transfer" or "transformation."
3. A cart rolls down a ramp and then slows to a stop on the carpet. Where did its motion energy go?
4. Which simple machine is each of these? (a) a door ramp (b) a seesaw (c) the blade of an axe (d) a jar lid
5. *Illustrative example:* A lever has the load 20 cm from the pivot and your push 100 cm from the pivot. What is the IMA? What ideal force lifts a 50 N load?
6. *Illustrative example:* A ramp is 6 m long and rises 2 m. What is its IMA? What ideal push moves a 90 N box up the ramp?
7. A friend says a pulley machine lifted a box with less force **and** less pulling distance. Explain why that cannot be true for any simple machine.

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. (a) elastic potential (b) chemical potential (c) motion (kinetic) (d) sound (the bell is also vibrating, which is motion)
2. Electrical energy is transferred through the cord to the toaster; the toaster transforms it into thermal energy (and some light from the glowing coils); thermal energy is transferred to the bread.
3. Mostly into thermal energy from friction with the carpet and the wheels' axles, plus a little sound.
4. (a) inclined plane (b) lever (c) wedge (d) screw
5. IMA = 100 ÷ 20 = 5. Ideal force = 50 N ÷ 5 = 10 N.
6. IMA = 6 ÷ 2 = 3. Ideal push = 90 N ÷ 3 = 30 N.
7. Work = force × distance, and a machine cannot put out more work than you put in. If the force is smaller, the distance must be larger.

**Adult self-check:** For questions 5 and 6, ask the learner to also find how far their hand moves, and to check that force × distance comes out the same on both sides. For the energy chains, check that the last step accounts for every output (useful or not), including heat and sound.

</details>

> **Safety note:** Drop balls only from a height you can reach while standing on the floor, not from chairs. When testing a lever, use books or a bag of rice, not anything that could slip and fall on toes. Toasters and old light bulbs get hot; observe them, but let an adult handle them.
