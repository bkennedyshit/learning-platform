# APP-02.6 — Decimals & Place Value

Our number system is built on one rule: **ten of one place make one of the next place to the left.** Ten ones make a ten, ten tens make a hundred, and so on up to millions. The same rule works to the right of the ones place: one whole split into ten equal parts makes tenths, and a tenth split into ten makes hundredths. Decimals are just the place-value system continued past the ones.

The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-02.1 — Number Sense & Counting](/app-021-number-sense-counting): tens and ones. > **Adult note:** A place-value chart drawn on paper (columns from hundred thousands to thousandths, with a dot for the decimal point) is the main tool. A hundreds grid (10 × 10 squares) shows tenths as columns and hundredths as single squares. Coins work as an everyday example: a dime is one tenth of a dollar and a penny is one hundredth.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Read and write whole numbers up to one million and say what each digit is worth.
2. Write tenths, hundredths, and thousandths as fractions and as decimals.
3. Compare decimals and add or subtract them.
4. Multiply and divide by 10, 100, and 1,000 and explain what happens to the digits.

## Core ideas

### Places up to a million

| Millions | Hundred thousands | Ten thousands | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000,000 | 100,000 | 10,000 | 1,000 | 100 | 10 | 1 |

Each place is **ten times** the place to its right. In 444, the 4 in the hundreds place is worth 400, ten times the 4 in the tens place (40).

**Expanded form** shows each digit's value: 306,512 = 300,000 + 6,000 + 500 + 10 + 2. Its name is "three hundred six thousand, five hundred twelve." The 0 in the ten thousands place matters: it holds the place so the 3 is worth 300,000.

Ten can also be written with an **exponent**: $10^2 = 10 \times 10 = 100$ and $10^3 = 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1{,}000$.

### Places smaller than one

| Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | . | $\frac{1}{10}$ | $\frac{1}{100}$ | $\frac{1}{1000}$ |

The **decimal point** separates the whole-number places from the parts. Examples:

- $0.3 = \frac{3}{10}$, read "three tenths."
- $0.62 = \frac{62}{100}$, read "sixty-two hundredths." It is also 6 tenths and 2 hundredths.
- $2.075 = 2 + \frac{0}{10} + \frac{7}{100} + \frac{5}{1000}$, read "two and seventy-five thousandths."

On a hundreds grid, 0.62 is 6 full columns plus 2 squares. With coins, 0.62 dollar is 6 dimes and 2 pennies.

### Zeros at the end of a decimal

$0.5 = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{50}{100} = 0.50$. Adding zeros at the **end** of a decimal does not change its value, because 5 tenths is the same amount as 50 hundredths. Zeros **between** the decimal point and a digit do change the value: 0.05 is five hundredths, much less than 0.5.

### Comparing decimals

Compare from the **left**, one place at a time, just like whole numbers. The first place where the digits differ decides. To make this easier, write both numbers with the same number of decimal places.

### Adding and subtracting decimals

Line up the decimal points so that tenths are added to tenths and hundredths to hundredths. Fill empty places with zeros if that helps. Then add or subtract as with whole numbers, regrouping when needed, and keep the decimal point in the same column.

### Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100, and 1,000

Multiplying by 10 makes every digit worth ten times as much, so each digit moves **one place to the left**. Multiplying by 100 moves digits two places, and by 1,000 three places. Dividing moves digits to the right.

- 3.6 × 10 = 36 (3 ones become 3 tens; 6 tenths become 6 ones)
- 3.6 × 100 = 360
- 47 ÷ 100 = 0.47
- 5.2 ÷ 1,000 = 0.0052

It is the digits that move compared with the decimal point; the point itself stays between the ones and the tenths.

### Looking ahead: very large numbers

Scientists write very large numbers with powers of ten, for example $3{,}000{,}000 = 3 \times 10^6$. This is called scientific notation, and it is taught in later grades. For now it is enough to see that $10^6$ is a 1 followed by six zeros.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Comparing 0.5 and 0.45

1. Some learners think 0.45 is bigger because 45 is bigger than 5.
2. Write both with two decimal places: 0.50 and 0.45.
3. Compare from the left. Ones: both 0. Tenths: 5 and 4. Five tenths is more than four tenths, so the first number is bigger.
4. Conclusion: 0.5 > 0.45.
5. Check on a hundreds grid: 0.5 is 50 squares shaded, 0.45 is 45 squares shaded.

### Example 2: Adding and subtracting decimals

(a) 2.75 + 1.4

1. Line up the points and write 1.4 as 1.40.
2. Hundredths: 5 + 0 = 5. Tenths: 7 + 4 = 11 tenths, so write 1 and regroup 10 tenths as 1 one. Ones: 2 + 1 + 1 = 4.
3. Answer: 4.15. Estimate: about 3 + 1 = 4, so 4.15 is reasonable.

(b) 5 − 1.37

1. Write 5 as 5.00 so both numbers have hundredths.
2. Regroup: 5.00 is 4 ones, 9 tenths, 10 hundredths.
3. Hundredths: 10 − 7 = 3. Tenths: 9 − 3 = 6. Ones: 4 − 1 = 3.
4. Answer: 3.63. Check: 3.63 + 1.37 = 5.00.

### Example 3: Changing units with powers of ten

*Illustrative example.* A pencil is 0.18 meter long. How many centimeters is that? (1 meter = 100 centimeters.)

1. Each meter is 100 centimeters, so multiply by 100: 0.18 × 100.
2. Multiplying by 100 moves each digit two places left: 1 tenth becomes 1 ten; 8 hundredths become 8 ones.
3. Answer: 18 centimeters.
4. Check: 18 centimeters is less than the 100 centimeters in a meter, and 0.18 is less than 1, so the answer makes sense.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Longer decimals are bigger."** 0.45 has more digits than 0.5 but is smaller (Example 1). Compare by place, not by length.
- **"To multiply by 10, add a zero."** That works for whole numbers (36 × 10 = 360), but 3.6 × 10 is not 3.60, which equals 3.6. Think "each digit moves one place left" instead.
- **Lining up the right-hand digits instead of the decimal points.** Adding 2.75 and 1.4 by lining up 5 and 4 adds hundredths to tenths. Line up the points.
- **Calling the place right of the decimal point "oneths."** There is no oneths place. The first place to the right of the decimal point is tenths, because the ones place is in the middle of the pattern.
- **Mixing up 0.05 and 0.5.** Read them aloud as "five hundredths" and "five tenths" to hear the difference.

## Practice

1. Write 405,030 in expanded form and in words.
2. In 7,777, how many times greater is the value of the 7 in the thousands place than the 7 in the hundreds place?
3. Write as decimals: $\frac{7}{10}$, $\frac{7}{100}$, $\frac{39}{100}$, $\frac{125}{1000}$.
4. Put in order from least to greatest: 0.3, 0.29, 0.305, 0.03.
5. Find 6.08 + 3.9.
6. Find 10 − 4.65.
7. Find 0.7 × 100, 52 ÷ 10, and 1.25 × 1,000.
8. *Illustrative example:* A water bottle holds 0.75 liter. How many milliliters is that? (1 liter = 1,000 milliliters.)
