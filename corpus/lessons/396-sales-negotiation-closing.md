# 39.6 — Sales, Negotiation & Closing

> *"Founders who avoid sales are outsourcing the single most important feedback loop in their company to someone else."*
> — Pete Kazanjy, *Founding Sales*, paraphrased

Sales feels foreign to most technical founders. The mental model is "used car salesman" — manipulative, pushy, dishonest. That model is wrong. Great sales is simply: **deeply understanding what a customer needs, and clearly demonstrating that your product solves it better than any alternative.** When your product genuinely solves the problem, sales is an act of service, not manipulation.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Describe the **full B2B sales cycle** from lead → close and the typical time at each stage.
2. Run a **SPIN discovery call** that surfaces real pain without feeling like an interrogation.
3. Deliver a **product demo** that maps to the customer's specific pain, not your feature list.
4. Handle the **5 most common objections** (price, timing, competition, authority, inertia) with confidence.
5. Apply **negotiation principles** (BATNA, anchoring, concession sequencing) in commercial conversations.
6. Execute a **closing move** that creates urgency without being manipulative.
7. Build a simple **CRM workflow** that keeps your pipeline visible and no deal falls through cracks.

## 📚 1. The B2B Sales Cycle

### 1.1 Stage Map

```
LEAD GENERATION
│  Cold outreach, inbound inquiry, PLG PQL signal, referral
│  Metric: # of leads per week

QUALIFICATION
│  Does this person match ICP? Do they have a problem? Budget? Authority?
│  Metric: % of leads that are qualified

DISCOVERY CALL
│  Deep dive into their situation: pain, timeline, budget, stakeholders
│  Metric: % of discovery calls that convert to demo

DEMO / PROPOSAL
│  Show the product mapped to their specific situation
│  Metric: % of demos that generate proposals

NEGOTIATION
│  Price, terms, timeline. Deal-specific back and forth.
│  Metric: % of proposals that reach negotiation

CLOSE
│  Contract signed, payment method set, account activated
│  Metric: Win rate, average days to close

ONBOARDING
│  Customer gets value immediately; reduces early churn
│  Metric: Onboarding completion rate, time-to-first-value
```

### 1.2 Sales Cycle Time by Deal Size

| ACV (Annual Contract Value) | Typical sales cycle | Typical decision makers |
|-----------------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| < $1k | Self-serve; no sales call | Individual |
| $1k–$10k | 1–4 weeks | 1–2 people |
| $10k–$50k | 1–3 months | 2–5 people (buying committee) |
| $50k–$250k | 3–6 months | 5+ people, legal, procurement |
| $250k+ | 6–18 months | C-suite, board |

For your current stage, focus on the $1k–$10k range: short enough to iterate, valuable enough to fund growth.

## 📚 2. Qualification — BANT and MEDDIC

### 2.1 BANT (Classic Qualification Framework)

**B — Budget:** Do they have money allocated for this type of solution?
**A — Authority:** Can they make the purchasing decision, or do they need approval?
**N — Need:** Do they have a genuine pain that your product solves?
**T — Timeline:** Are they planning to solve this now, or at some unspecified future date?

Disqualify early on any missing BANT dimension. A prospect with no budget, no authority, vague need, and no timeline is not a prospect — it's a conversation that won't close.

### 2.2 MEDDIC (Enterprise-Level Qualification)

For deals $10k+:

**M — Metrics:** What numbers matter to them? ROI they're targeting?
**E — Economic Buyer:** Who controls the budget? Get to them.
**D — Decision Criteria:** What does their evaluation scorecard look like?
**D — Decision Process:** What's the formal process? How many approvals?
**I — Identify Pain:** What's the compelling event or pain driving urgency?
**C — Champion:** Who internally is advocating for your solution?

The **champion** is the most important: someone inside the org who wants you to win because your success is their success.

## 📚 3. The Discovery Call

### Definition 39.6.1 — Discovery Call

A structured conversation designed to understand the prospect's situation deeply enough to (a) qualify them, and (b) demonstrate the product in a way that maps specifically to their pain.

**Most founders demo before they discover.** This is backwards. A demo without discovery is a feature show, not a business conversation.

### 3.1 SPIN Selling Framework

SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham, research-backed) is the gold standard for discovery. The four question types:

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **S — Situation** | Build context; understand current state | "What tools are you currently using for level design?" |
| **P — Problem** | Identify pain; surface dissatisfaction | "What's most frustrating about that workflow?" |
| **I — Implication** | Amplify pain; show consequences | "If you're spending 5 hours per level, how does that affect your release timeline?" |
| **N — Need-Payoff** | Connect solution to their need | "If you could cut level iteration to 1 hour, what would that mean for the team?" |

**The key insight:** The Implication and Need-Payoff questions are what move deals forward. Situation and Problem questions establish context; I and N questions create the emotional urgency that drives decisions.

### 3.2 Discovery Call Structure

**Opening (2 min):** Set the agenda. "I have 30 minutes reserved. What I'd like to do is understand your current workflow for X, then if it makes sense, show you how we approach it. Does that work?"

**Situation questions (5–10 min):** Understand their stack, team, scale, workflow.

**Problem questions (5–10 min):** Surface frustration, limitations, workarounds.

**Implication questions (5 min):** Deepen the pain — time lost, money lost, strategic implications.

**Need-Payoff questions (5 min):** Let them articulate the value of solving the problem (in their words — this is your sales pitch played back to you).

**Transition to demo (2 min):** "Based on what you've described, I think the most relevant thing to show you is X. Let me focus there rather than give you a generic tour."

**Next steps (2 min):** Always close with a specific next step. "I'll send a proposal by Thursday. Does 30 minutes on Friday work to review it together?"

## 📚 4. The Demo

### 4.1 The Anti-Demo Pattern (What Not to Do)

Most founder demos fail because they follow this pattern:
1. "Let me give you a tour of the product..."
2. Show feature A → B → C → D → E
3. "...and that's basically everything. Any questions?"

This is a feature walk, not a business conversation. The prospect leaves without understanding how it solves *their* problem.

### 4.2 The Problem-First Demo Structure

1. **Reflect their pain back:** "You mentioned you spend 5 hours per level on iteration. I want to show you exactly what that looks like with our tool."
2. **Demo the one thing that solves their #1 pain** — not your best feature, their worst problem
3. **Show the before/after:** "Normally this takes you X. Here's what it looks like with us: Y."
4. **Let them interact if possible:** Product-led demos where the prospect clicks and experiences the aha moment are 2× more effective than screen shares
5. **Connect to business outcomes:** "If you ran 20 levels through this in a month, at 4 hours saved each, that's 80 hours — roughly half a sprint cycle."

### 4.3 Demo Calibration

After every demo, ask yourself:
- Did I start with their problem or with my features?
- Did I show them the one thing they care most about?
- Did they lean in at any point? (That's where the value was)
- Did they ask about price? (Buying signal)
- What would make the next demo 20% better?

## 📚 5. Objection Handling

### Definition 39.6.2 — Objection

An objection is a signal of interest, not rejection. A prospect who objects is engaged — they're trying to figure out if this makes sense. Silence or "we'll think about it" is more dangerous.

### 5.1 The Five Common Objections and Responses

**Objection 1: "It's too expensive."**

Framework: Explore whether it's a budget issue (real constraint) or a value issue (they don't see the ROI yet).

Response: "I understand. Can I ask — is it the absolute number that's the concern, or is it that you're not sure the value justifies it? Because if it's the latter, let's talk through the ROI numbers you shared earlier..."

Then: Offer annual pricing (reduces monthly sticker shock), offer a smaller initial engagement, or reduce scope.

**Objection 2: "Now isn't the right time."**

Real question: Is it genuinely timing, or is it a polite no?

Response: "I hear that a lot. What would need to change in your situation for the timing to be right? And is there a version of this that makes sense to pilot now, even if full deployment is Q3?"

Then: If timeline is genuine, agree a specific future date for re-engagement. Put it in your CRM.

**Objection 3: "We already built something internally / we're using X."**

Response: "That makes sense — I'd be curious what led you to build it yourself. What are the areas where the internal tool falls short, if any?"

Rarely do internal tools fully solve the problem. Surfacing gaps re-opens the door.

**Objection 4: "I need to check with [boss / board / procurement]."**

Response: "Of course. Who else is typically involved in decisions like this? And what information would be most useful for that conversation — I'm happy to put together a one-pager for your internal discussion."

Then: Ask if you can schedule a call that includes the economic buyer directly.

**Objection 5: "We're evaluating a few options."**

Response: "That's completely reasonable. What criteria are you using to evaluate? I want to make sure we give you what you need to make the best comparison."

Then: Find out your differentiator in their criteria. If price: compete on value. If features: demo the differentiating feature. If trust/track record: offer a reference call.

## 📚 6. Negotiation

### 6.1 BATNA — Your Best Alternative

**BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement):** The outcome you get if the deal doesn't close. Knowing your BATNA gives you power — you can walk away from a bad deal, and the prospect knows you won't be desperate.

**How to build BATNA:** Have multiple deals in pipeline simultaneously. A founder with one prospect in negotiation will accept almost any terms. A founder with five prospects in negotiation can hold firm.

### 6.2 Anchoring

**Price anchoring:** Name a number first. The first number named in a negotiation becomes the anchor that all subsequent discussion orbits.

- If they ask for your price: name it first, at or above your ideal
- If they name their budget first: it becomes the ceiling, which is why you should always name first

**Example:** "For a team of your size, our typical deal is in the $3,500–$4,500/year range. Where does your budget sit for something like this?"

### 6.3 Concession Sequencing

If you need to make concessions:
- Make them in **decreasing size** (first concession: $500 off; second: $200 off; third: $50 off)
- Each concession should buy **something in return** ("I can do $3,000 if you're willing to pay annually upfront")
- Never give a concession without getting one back
- Set a floor and hold it — "I'm not able to go below $2,500"

### 6.4 The "Good Cop / Deadline" Close

"I want to get this done for you. If we can finalize by [specific date], I can hold this pricing. After that, I can't guarantee the discount."

This is not a manipulation — it's a real constraint (discounts are exceptions to standard pricing, and pricing may actually change). But it creates urgency and gives the prospect a reason to decide.

## 📚 7. Closing Moves

### 7.1 Types of Closes

**The Assumptive Close:** Proceed as if the deal is done.
"Great — let me send over the contract now. Do you need it addressed to you or your company name?"

**The Summary Close:** Recap everything agreed, then ask for the signature.
"So we've agreed on the Builder plan, annual pricing at $2,400, starting Q1. I'll send the agreement over — can you sign by Thursday?"

**The Direct Close:** Simply ask for the business.
"Does this make sense for you to move forward?"

**The Next-Step Close:** Never leave without a committed next step.
"When can we schedule 15 minutes to review the agreement together?" (Not: "I'll send it over — let me know what you think.")

### 7.2 After the "Yes"

The deal isn't done until money is in your account. Common failure modes:
- "Yes, send the contract" → contract never gets signed (champion left company, budget frozen, internal delay)
- Contract signed → payment delayed → deal collapses before onboarding

Fix: shorten every gap. Send contract within 1 hour of verbal yes. Follow up daily on unsigned contracts. Have a "contract started" → "money received" checklist.

## 🛠️ 8. Worked Example — Founder-Led Sales Call

**Product:** AI code review tool
**Prospect:** Maria, senior freelance developer, 8-year experience, builds React apps for clients

**Discovery:**
- Situation: uses ESLint + manual code review, submits to clients without formal review process
- Problem: "Sometimes bugs slip through into client staging — it's embarrassing and I have to comp them time to fix it"
- Implication: "Last time it happened, the client paused the contract for 3 weeks. Cost me ~$6,000 in delayed invoicing"
- Need-Payoff: "If I had something that caught those issues before I submit, I'd never have that conversation with a client"

**Demo:** Show exactly "pre-submit code review" workflow — not the full product, just the one flow that maps to her pain.

**Objection:** "The $69/month feels steep for a solo freelancer."

**Response:** "You mentioned the last incident cost you $6,000 in delayed billing. Even one incident prevented per year makes this $828/year more than ROI-positive. But if the monthly feels uncertain, the annual plan is $599 — that's about 10% of the last incident."

**Close:** "It sounds like this would have saved you exactly that situation. Let's set you up — I'll send the link now. Annual or monthly to start?"

**Result:** Annual, $599, signed same day.

## ⚠️ 10. Common Misconceptions

- **"If I had a better product, I wouldn't need to sell."** The best product rarely wins without distribution and sales. Inferior products with better sales consistently outperform superior products with no sales motion.
- **"Sales is manipulation."** Discovery-led, problem-first sales is the opposite of manipulation — it's helping someone understand if your product genuinely solves their problem. If it doesn't, disqualify them honestly.
- **"My CRM is overkill at my stage."** A spreadsheet is fine for < 20 deals. But a simple HubSpot free tier, Notion CRM, or even a structured Google Sheet prevents follow-up failures that lose real money.
- **"I should do a demo first, then discovery."** Never. Discovery before demo, always. A demo without discovery is a feature show. A demo after discovery is a business solution.
- **"Discounting is the fastest way to close."** Discounting trains buyers to wait for discounts and devalues your product in their mind. Use discounting as a precision tool (annual vs monthly switch, multi-seat commitment), not a panic button.
