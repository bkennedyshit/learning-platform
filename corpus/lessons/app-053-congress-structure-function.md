# APP-05.3 — Congress — Structure & Function

**Congress** is the national legislature. Article I, Section 1 of the Constitution opens: "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States, which shall consist of a Senate and House of Representatives." Because Congress has two chambers, it is **bicameral**. That design came from the Great Compromise of 1787 (Lesson 5.1): representation by population in the House and equal representation of states in the Senate.

This lesson, for high school students (grades 9–12), explains how the two chambers differ, how a bill moves through them, and why most of the real decisions happen before a final vote. Numbers in this lesson were checked against House, Senate, and National Archives pages in October 2026; party leaders and counts of members change, so look up current names on [house.gov](https://www.house.gov/) and [senate.gov](https://www.senate.gov/) rather than memorizing them.

## Prerequisites

- [APP-05.2 — The Constitution — Structure & Principles](/app-052-the-constitution-structure-principles), especially Article I, enumerated powers, and checks and balances.
- Fractions and rounding: you will find three-fifths and two-thirds of a number and round up to a whole vote.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Compare the **House** and **Senate** on size, term, qualifications, representation, and exclusive powers.
2. Sequence the steps of the **legislative process** and identify where a bill can stall or die.
3. Explain the roles of **committees**, **party leaders**, the **Rules Committee**, the **filibuster** and **cloture**, and the **discharge petition**.
4. Calculate **cloture** and **veto override** thresholds and interpret the historical veto record.

## Core ideas

### Two chambers compared

| Feature | House of Representatives | Senate |
|---|---|---|
| Voting members | 435, a number set by law in 1911 and in effect since 1913 | 100, two per state |
| Non-voting members | Five delegates (D.C., U.S. Virgin Islands, Guam, American Samoa, Northern Mariana Islands) and one resident commissioner (Puerto Rico); they cannot vote when the House meets as the House | None |
| Term | 2 years; the whole House is elected every two years | 6 years; about one-third of seats are elected every two years |
| Qualifications | At least 25; a citizen for 7 years; an inhabitant of the state | At least 30; a citizen for 9 years; an inhabitant of the state |
| Representation | Seats divided among states by population after each census; each member usually represents a district | Equal for every state; each senator represents the whole state |
| Originally chosen by | The people | State legislatures, until the Seventeenth Amendment (ratified 1913) provided direct election |
| Presiding officer | The Speaker, elected by the House | The vice president, who votes only to break ties; the president pro tempore presides in the vice president's absence |
| Exclusive powers | Revenue bills must originate here; "sole Power of Impeachment" (by simple majority); chooses the president if no candidate wins a majority of electoral votes | Tries impeachments (conviction requires two-thirds of members present); advice and consent on treaties (two-thirds of senators present) and on nominations (majority) |

The Senate notes that it "does not ratify treaties." It approves or rejects a resolution of ratification; ratification itself happens when the countries exchange formal instruments. It also notes that in its history it has confirmed 126 Supreme Court nominations.

### Who organizes the work

- **Party leadership.** In the House: the Speaker, majority and minority leaders, assistant leaders, whips, and each party's caucus or conference. Whips "assist leadership in managing their party's legislative program on the House floor." The Senate has majority and minority leaders and whips as well.
- **Committees.** The House site says standing committees have "different legislative jurisdictions," consider bills, and have **oversight** responsibility to "monitor agencies, programs, and activities." **Select committees** are formed "for a short time period and specific purpose, frequently an investigation." **Joint committees** include members of both chambers, and **conference committees** are temporary panels that reconcile House and Senate versions of a bill. Each committee has a **chair** (majority party) and a **ranking member** (leader of the minority members). Party ratios on committees roughly match the chamber.
- **The House Rules Committee** "controls what bills go to the House Floor and the terms of debate." It has been weighted toward the majority, 9 majority and 4 minority members, "since the late 1970s."

### Kinds of measures

| Measure | Label (House / Senate) | Goes to the president? | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bill | H.R. / S. | Yes | Most legislation |
| Joint resolution | H.J.Res. / S.J.Res. | Yes, except one proposing a constitutional amendment | Similar to a bill; also proposes amendments |
| Concurrent resolution | H.Con.Res. / S.Con.Res. | No | Matters affecting both chambers' operations |
| Simple resolution | H.Res. / S.Res. | No | Matters for one chamber only, such as a House "rule" for debating a bill |

### How a bill becomes a law

1. **Introduction.** In the House, any member may introduce a bill by placing it in "the 'hopper' at the side of the Clerk's desk." The sponsor must sign it; a public bill may have unlimited cosponsors.
2. **Referral.** The Speaker, with the parliamentarian's help, refers the bill to the committee with jurisdiction. The House calls committee action the stage where "the most intense consideration is given" to proposals.
3. **Committee work.** A committee or subcommittee usually holds **hearings** with witnesses, then a **markup** session where members offer and vote on amendments. The committee can **report** the bill, with or without amendments, or **table** it, "which means no further action on it will occur." Many bills go no further than this stage.
4. **Floor action.** In the House, debate on major bills is usually governed by a **rule**, a simple resolution setting time limits and which amendments may be offered. Opponents may move to **recommit** the bill to committee before final passage. In the Senate, debate is traditionally unlimited, so a bill may face a **filibuster**.
5. **The other chamber.** The bill must pass both chambers "in the same form." Differences are resolved by sending amendments back and forth or by a **conference committee**, whose agreement goes back to both chambers.
6. **The president.** The **enrolled** bill goes to the president, who may sign it, veto it, or let it become law without a signature. If the president does not act within ten days (Sundays excepted), the bill becomes law, "unless the Congress by their Adjournment prevent its Return, in which Case it shall not be a Law." That is a **pocket veto**.
7. **Override.** Congress can override a regular veto when "two-thirds of the Members voting in each chamber agree, by recorded vote, a quorum being present," according to the Congressional Research Service. A pocket veto cannot be overridden because Congress is not in session to receive it.

### Tools for speeding up or slowing down

- **Filibuster.** The Senate defines it as "a loosely defined term for action designed to prolong debate and delay or prevent a vote." A long speech is the familiar image, but the threat of extended debate is often enough.
- **Cloture.** In 1917 the Senate adopted Rule XXII, allowing two-thirds of senators voting to end debate. In 1975 it changed the threshold to "three-fifths of all senators duly chosen and sworn," which is 60 when all 100 seats are filled. The Senate "adopted new precedents in the 2010s to allow a simple majority to end debate on nominations," so today the 60-vote threshold applies to most legislation, not nominations.
- **Hold.** An informal notice from a senator to leadership that he or she "does not wish a particular measure or nomination to reach the floor."
- **Discharge petition.** A House tool to force a bill out of a committee that will not act. The House historian reports that the signature requirement was set at 218 in 1935, a majority of the full House.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Tracing an illustrative bill and its stopping points

**Illustrative scenario:** A representative introduces a bill to fund rural broadband grants. Track where it could stop.

| Step | What happens | Could it die here? |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Placed in the hopper, numbered, referred by the Speaker to the Energy and Commerce Committee | Not yet |
| Committee | Subcommittee hearing, then full-committee markup with three amendments adopted | **Yes**: the chair may never schedule it, or the committee may table it |
| Rules Committee | Writes a rule allowing two hours of debate and five amendments | **Yes**: no rule, no floor time for most major bills |
| House floor | Motion to recommit fails; bill passes | **Yes**: a failed final vote ends it |
| Senate committee | Referred, hearing held, reported with changes | **Yes** |
| Senate floor | Opponents threaten to filibuster; cloture vote needed | **Yes**: if cloture fails, the bill may never get a final vote |
| Resolving differences | House accepts Senate changes | **Yes**: if the chambers cannot agree |
| President | Signs, vetoes, or holds it | **Yes**: a veto that is not overridden, or a pocket veto |

**Reasoning:** a bill must survive every gate, but opponents need only one. That is why the legislative process favors the status quo and why committee chairs and leaders, who control scheduling, have so much influence.

### Example 2: Cloture math with vacancies

The rule is three-fifths of senators "duly chosen and sworn," not of senators present.

1. **All 100 seats filled:** 100 × 3/5 = 60 votes.
2. **Two vacancies (98 sworn):** 98 × 3/5 = 58.8. A vote cannot be split, so round up: **59**.
3. **One vacancy (99 sworn):** 99 × 3/5 = 59.4, so **60**.
4. **Check:** absences do not change the threshold. If only 80 senators show up and all 100 seats are filled, cloture still needs 60 yes votes, so an absent supporter hurts as much as a no vote.

### Example 3: Override math and the historical record

1. **House:** suppose 430 members vote on an override. Two-thirds of 430 is 286.67, so **287** yes votes are needed.
2. **Senate:** suppose 99 senators vote. Two-thirds of 99 is exactly 66, so **66** are needed.
3. **Order:** the CRS notes the chamber where the bill originated acts first, and "if the first-acting chamber fails to override the veto, the other chamber cannot consider it."
4. **Interpreting the record:** the Senate's table "Vetoes, 1789 to Present" (checked October 19, 2026) listed 2,599 vetoes: 1,533 regular and 1,066 pocket vetoes, with 112 overridden. That is 112 ÷ 1,533 ≈ 7.3% of regular vetoes, or about 4.3% of all vetoes. **Conclusion:** the veto threat is powerful because overrides are rare; presidents can shape bills before they are passed by signaling that they will veto them.

### Example 4: Same state, two kinds of representation

**Illustrative comparison:** State A has 1 House seat; State B has 40.

1. In the **Senate**, both states have 2 senators, so each controls 2% of the 100 votes.
2. In the **House**, State A's single member is about 0.2% of 435 votes (1 ÷ 435), while State B's 40 members are about 9.2% (40 ÷ 435).
3. **Why it matters:** a bill must pass both chambers, so states with small populations have a stronger relative voice in the Senate, and states with large populations have more weight in the House. That is the Great Compromise operating today.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Senate ratifies treaties."** The Senate gives advice and consent by approving a resolution of ratification; ratification occurs when instruments are exchanged.
- **"A filibuster means someone talking for hours."** The Senate calls it a loosely defined term for delaying action. The marathon speech is the most familiar form, not the only one.
- **"Every bill needs 60 votes in the Senate."** Passing a bill takes a majority; 60 is the cloture threshold for ending debate on most legislation. Nominations need only a majority for cloture under precedents from the 2010s. Some budget measures follow special rules not covered here.
- **"A bill dies if the president does not sign it."** It becomes law after ten days (Sundays excepted) unless Congress's adjournment prevents its return.
- **"House passage always takes 218 votes."** The House's own explainer uses "218 of 435" as shorthand for a simple majority, but a bill passes with a majority of members voting, a quorum being present (Article I, Section 5 makes "a Majority of each" chamber a quorum). When members are absent or seats are vacant, fewer than 218 can pass a bill.
- **"Delegates are full voting members."** Delegates and the resident commissioner have the same powers as other members except voting when the House meets as the House.
- **"Conference committees handle every disagreement."** The House notes the chambers may also exchange amendments on the floor; conference is one option.
- **Boundary: rules change.** Cloture thresholds, discharge rules, and committee practices are set by each chamber's rules and precedents, and the chambers have changed them several times. Check the current rules before stating them as fixed.

2. Put these steps in order: markup, enrolled bill presented to the president, introduction, Rules Committee sets terms, referral to committee, conference report adopted, hearing, House floor vote, Senate floor vote.
3. Calculate: (a) cloture with 97 senators duly chosen and sworn; (b) an override in the House when 420 members vote; (c) an override in the Senate when 100 senators vote.
4. Classify each measure as a bill, joint resolution, concurrent resolution, or simple resolution: (a) H.Res. setting debate rules for a bill; (b) a measure proposing a constitutional amendment; (c) a measure setting a joint adjournment of both chambers; (d) H.R. 1234 (illustrative number).
