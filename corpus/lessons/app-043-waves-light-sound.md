# APP-04.3 — Waves — Light & Sound

Drop a pebble in a pond and rings spread out across the water. A leaf floating on the pond bobs up and down as the rings pass, but it does not ride along to the shore. That is the key idea of a **wave**: a disturbance that travels from place to place and carries **energy**, while the material it travels through mostly stays where it was. Sound and light are both waves, and this lesson explains how each one travels, what we can measure about it, and how our ears and eyes detect it.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 4-6. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding the learner.

## Core ideas

### What waves carry

A wave starts with a **vibration**, something moving back and forth. The vibration pushes on the material next to it, which pushes on the next bit, and so on. The material it travels through is called the **medium**. Water, air, a rope, and the ground can all be media.

- In a **transverse wave**, the medium moves **across** the direction the wave travels. Flick a rope up and down: the bumps travel along the rope while each bit of rope moves up and down.
- In a **longitudinal wave**, the medium moves **back and forth along** the direction the wave travels. Push and pull one end of a stretched spring toy: bunched-up parts (**compressions**) and spread-out parts travel along it. Sound in air is a longitudinal wave.

### Measuring a wave

- **Wavelength** ($\lambda$, the Greek letter lambda): the distance from one crest (top) to the next crest, or from one compression to the next.
- **Amplitude:** how far the medium moves from its resting position. A bigger amplitude carries more energy.
- **Frequency** ($f$): how many waves pass a point each second, measured in **hertz** (Hz). 1 Hz is one wave per second.
- **Wave speed** ($v$): how fast the disturbance travels.

These are connected by one equation:

$$v = f \lambda$$

If the speed stays the same, a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength.

### Sound

Sound is a longitudinal wave made by vibrating objects. A guitar string, your vocal cords, and a speaker cone all vibrate and push on the air.

- **Pitch** is how high or low a sound is. It depends on **frequency**: faster vibrations make a higher pitch. A short, tight string vibrates faster than a long, loose one.
- **Loudness** depends mostly on **amplitude**. A harder pluck makes bigger vibrations and a louder sound at the same pitch.
- Sound needs a medium. It travels through gases, liquids, and solids, and it is usually faster in liquids and solids than in air. In air at 0 °C, sound travels about 331 meters per second; in fresh water it is about 1,480 m/s. In the near-empty space between planets there is almost nothing to carry sound.
- **Hearing:** sound waves travel down the ear canal and make the **eardrum** vibrate. Tiny bones in the middle ear pass the vibrations to the inner ear, which sends nerve signals to the brain.

### Light

Light is a transverse wave of a different kind: an **electromagnetic wave**. It does not need a medium. That is how sunlight reaches Earth across empty space. In a vacuum, light travels 299,792,458 meters per second, nearly a million times faster than sound in air.

Visible light is a small part of the **electromagnetic spectrum**. From longest to shortest wavelength: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, **visible light**, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays. Human eyes detect wavelengths from about 380 nanometers (violet) to about 700 nanometers (red).

When light reaches an object, it can be:

- **Reflected:** bounced off. On a smooth surface such as a mirror, the **law of reflection** holds: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, both measured from a line perpendicular to the surface.
- **Refracted:** bent as it passes from one material into another, such as from water into air.
- **Absorbed:** taken in, usually warming the object. A dark shirt absorbs more sunlight than a white one.
- **Transmitted:** passed through, as through clear glass.

**Seeing:** we see an object when light from it, usually reflected light, enters our eyes. Cells at the back of the eye respond to light and send signals to the brain. A red apple looks red because it reflects mostly red light and absorbs most other colors.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: How far away is the lightning?

You see a flash of lightning and count 10 seconds before the thunder.

1. Light arrives almost instantly. The delay is the time sound took to reach you.
2. The National Weather Service rule: sound takes about 5 seconds to travel a mile.
3. Distance ≈ 10 ÷ 5 = 2 miles.
4. Check with meters: 10 s × about 330 m/s ≈ 3,300 m, which is about 2 miles (1 mile is about 1,609 m). The two methods agree.
5. Safety boundary: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Go indoors; do not stay outside to count.

### Example 2: Finding a wavelength

*Illustrative example.* A tuning fork vibrates at 662 Hz in air at 0 °C, where sound travels at 331 m/s. What is the wavelength?

1. Start with $v = f\lambda$ and solve for wavelength: $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$.
2. Substitute with units: $\lambda = \dfrac{331\ \text{m/s}}{662\ \text{Hz}}$.
3. Since 1 Hz = 1 per second, the seconds cancel and the answer is in meters: $\lambda = 0.5\ \text{m}$.
4. Check: a fork at half this frequency (331 Hz) should give a wavelength twice as long, 1 m. It does: 331 ÷ 331 = 1.

### Example 3: A ray hitting a mirror

A flashlight beam hits a flat mirror at 35° from the perpendicular line (called the **normal**).

1. Draw the mirror and a dashed normal line where the beam hits.
2. Law of reflection: angle of reflection = angle of incidence.
3. So the reflected beam leaves at 35° on the **other side** of the normal.
4. Boundary: the angles are measured from the normal, not from the mirror's surface. Measured from the surface, both angles would be 90° − 35° = 55°.

### Example 4: The "broken" pencil

*Activity to try.* Put a pencil in a clear glass of water and look from the side.

1. The pencil seems to bend or break at the water's surface.
2. Light from the underwater part travels through water, then glass and air, before reaching your eye.
3. It bends (refracts) where it crosses from one material into another.
4. Your brain assumes light traveled in a straight line, so it places the underwater part in the wrong spot. The pencil itself is not bent; take it out to check.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Waves carry water (or air) along with them."** The medium mostly moves back and forth in place. The energy travels; the leaf on the pond stays put.
- **"Louder sounds are higher sounds."** Loudness depends on amplitude; pitch depends on frequency. A quiet whistle can be high, and a loud drum can be low.
- **"Sound is faster than light," or "we hear and see things at the same moment."** Light is far faster. That is why thunder comes after lightning and why fireworks are seen before they are heard.
- **"Space battles would be loud."** Sound needs a medium. In near-empty space there is nothing to carry sound, though light still crosses it.
- **"Our eyes send out rays to see."** Light travels **into** the eye from the object. In a completely dark room you cannot see anything.
- **"Light only travels in air."** Light also travels through water, glass, and empty space, at different speeds, which is why it refracts at a boundary.

## Practice

1. A stadium crowd does "the wave." Each person stands up and sits down in place. What travels around the stadium, and what does not?
2. Is each wave transverse or longitudinal? (a) a sound wave in air (b) a wave sent along a rope by moving the end up and down
3. Two sounds have the same pitch, but one is louder. What is different about the sound waves?
4. *Illustrative example:* A wave on a rope has a frequency of 4 Hz and a wavelength of 0.5 m. What is its speed?
5. *Illustrative example:* Sound in air at 0 °C (331 m/s) has a frequency of 331 Hz. What is the wavelength? What happens to the wavelength if the frequency doubles?
6. You count 15 seconds between lightning and thunder. About how far away was the strike?
7. Light hits a mirror at 50° from the normal. At what angle does it reflect?
8. Why does a black car in sunshine get hotter than a white car?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. The wave (the disturbance, and the energy) travels around the stadium. The people do not travel; each one stays at their seat.
2. (a) longitudinal (b) transverse
3. The louder sound has a larger amplitude. The frequency is the same, because the pitch is the same.
4. $v = f\lambda = 4\ \text{Hz} \times 0.5\ \text{m} = 2\ \text{m/s}$.
5. $\lambda = 331 \div 331 = 1\ \text{m}$. If the frequency doubles to 662 Hz at the same speed, the wavelength halves to 0.5 m.
6. About 15 ÷ 5 = 3 miles (about 5 km). You should already be heading indoors.
7. 50° from the normal, on the other side of it.
8. The black paint absorbs more of the light that hits it, and absorbed light becomes thermal energy. The white paint reflects more of it.

**Adult self-check:** In every calculation, ask the learner to say the units out loud (Hz × m gives m/s). For questions 2 and 3, ask them to show the motion with a spring toy or a rope.

</details>
