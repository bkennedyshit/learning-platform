# APP-02.2 — Addition & Subtraction

Addition puts amounts together. Subtraction takes an amount away, or finds how far apart two amounts are. In this lesson the learner first uses smart strategies for facts within 20, then uses tens and ones to add and subtract bigger numbers, up to 1,000.

The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-02.1 — Number Sense & Counting](/app-021-number-sense-counting): counting on from any number, knowing that 10 ones make 1 ten, and knowing the ways to make 10 (such as 7 and 3). For the three-digit part, the learner should be able to read a number like 365 as 3 hundreds, 6 tens, and 5 ones.

> **Adult note:** Useful materials are two ten frames with counters, a number line from 0 to 20, and base-ten blocks (unit cubes, ten-rods, hundred-flats). Paper drawings work too: a dot for a one, a stick for a ten, and a square for a hundred.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Tell what kind of story a word problem is and write an equation that matches it.
2. Solve addition and subtraction facts within 20 with a strategy, not only by counting by ones.
3. Add and subtract numbers up to 1,000, showing each regrouping with blocks or drawings and in writing.
4. Explain that the equal sign means "is the same amount as" and test equations for true or false.

## Core ideas

### Four kinds of stories

Most addition and subtraction problems are one of these:

| Kind of story | Illustrative example | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Add to | Mia has 5 shells and finds 3 more. How many now? | 5 + 3 = 8 |
| Take from | There are 9 birds. 4 fly away. How many are left? | 9 − 4 = 5 |
| Put together / take apart | 6 red apples and 2 green apples. How many apples? | 6 + 2 = 8 |
| Compare | Sam has 11 stickers. Jo has 7. How many more does Sam have? | 11 − 7 = 4 or 7 + 4 = 11 |

Any of the three numbers can be the unknown. "Mia had some shells, found 3 more, and now has 8" is still an add-to story, but the start is missing: __ + 3 = 8.

### Fact strategies within 20

- **Count on from the bigger number.** For 2 + 9, start at 9 and count "10, 11."
- **Make a ten.** For 8 + 5, move 2 from the 5 to fill the 8 up to 10, then add what is left: 8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3 = 10 + 3 = 13.
- **Doubles and near doubles.** 6 + 6 = 12, so 6 + 7 is one more: 13.
- **Think addition to subtract.** For 13 − 8, ask "8 plus what makes 13?" 8 + 2 = 10 and 10 + 3 = 13, so the answer is 2 + 3 = 5.

### Helpful properties

- **Order does not matter when adding** (commutative property): 3 + 9 = 9 + 3.
- **Grouping does not matter when adding** (associative property): (7 + 3) + 6 = 7 + (3 + 6). This is why you can look for a ten first.
- **Adding zero changes nothing** (identity property): 15 + 0 = 15.

Subtraction does **not** have the first two properties: 9 − 3 is 6, but 3 − 9 is not 6.

### The equal sign

The equal sign means **both sides are the same amount**. It does not mean "the answer comes next." So 7 = 3 + 4 is true, and 5 + 2 = 6 + 1 is true because both sides are 7.

### Bigger numbers: regrouping

When adding, if a place gets 10 or more, trade 10 of them for 1 of the next bigger place (10 ones → 1 ten; 10 tens → 1 hundred). When subtracting, if a place does not have enough, trade 1 of the next bigger place for 10 of the smaller one. This trading is called **regrouping**. It never changes the total amount; it only changes how it is shown.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: 47 + 38 with tens and ones

1. Show 47 as 4 ten-rods and 7 cubes; show 38 as 3 ten-rods and 8 cubes.
2. Put the ones together: 7 + 8 = 15 ones. That is more than 9, so trade 10 cubes for 1 ten-rod. Now there are 5 ones left and 1 new ten.
3. Put the tens together: 4 + 3 + 1 (the new ten) = 8 tens.
4. The total is 8 tens and 5 ones: 85.
5. Written method: write 5 in the ones place and a small 1 above the tens column; then 1 + 4 + 3 = 8 tens.
6. Check by estimating: 47 is close to 50 and 38 is close to 40; 50 + 40 = 90. The answer 85 is close, so it is reasonable.

### Example 2: 403 − 158, regrouping across a zero

1. Ones: 3 − 8 cannot be done with whole numbers, so we need more ones. There are 0 tens to trade.
2. Trade 1 hundred for 10 tens: now there are 3 hundreds and 10 tens.
3. Trade 1 of those tens for 10 ones: now 3 hundreds, 9 tens, and 13 ones. The amount is still 403 (300 + 90 + 13).
4. Subtract: ones 13 − 8 = 5; tens 9 − 5 = 4; hundreds 3 − 1 = 2.
5. The answer is 245.
6. Check by adding back: 245 + 158 = 403. Because addition undoes subtraction, the answer is correct.

### Example 3: A compare story

*Illustrative example.* "Ana has 12 marbles. That is 5 more than Ben has. How many marbles does Ben have?"

1. The word "more" is in the story, but Ana is the one with more. Ben has fewer.
2. Draw two bars: a long bar for Ana (12) and a shorter bar for Ben, with the 5-piece gap marked.
3. Ben's bar plus 5 equals Ana's bar: __ + 5 = 12.
4. Think addition: 7 + 5 = 12, so Ben has 7 marbles. Equivalently, 12 − 5 = 7.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Always take the smaller digit from the bigger one."** For 52 − 27 some learners compute 7 − 2 = 5 in the ones place and get 35. The ones place is 2 − 7, which needs regrouping: 4 tens and 12 ones, so 12 − 7 = 5 and 4 − 2 = 2, giving 25. Check: 25 + 27 = 52.
- **Reading the equal sign as "the answer is."** For 3 + 4 = __ + 2, writing 7 makes the sides 7 and 9. The blank must be 5 so both sides equal 7.
- **Key words decide the operation.** "More" does not always mean add (see Example 3). Ask the learner to picture or draw the story instead of hunting for a word.
- **Forgetting the traded ten.** In 47 + 38, writing 75 means the new ten from the ones was lost. Having the learner physically trade blocks makes the extra ten visible.
- **Switching the order in subtraction.** 3 − 9 does not equal 9 − 3. With whole numbers, "take 9 from 3" is not possible; later courses use negative numbers for this.

## Practice

1. Use make-a-ten to find 9 + 6. Show your two steps.
2. Find 14 − 6 by thinking "6 plus what makes 14?"
3. Is each equation true or false? (a) 8 = 5 + 3 (b) 4 + 4 = 5 + 2 (c) 10 + 2 = 6 + 6
4. Find 365 + 278. Show each regrouping.
5. Find 500 − 237. Check by adding.
6. *Illustrative story:* A class has 23 students. 9 are at art. How many are not at art? What kind of story is it?
7. *Illustrative story:* Leo read 15 pages on Monday and some more on Tuesday. He read 24 pages in all. How many did he read on Tuesday? Write the equation with a blank.

1. 9 + 1 = 10 (taking 1 from the 6 leaves 5), then 10 + 5 = 15.
2. 6 + 4 = 10, and 10 + 4 = 14, so 6 + 8 = 14 and 14 − 6 = 8.
3. (a) True. (b) False: 8 and 7. (c) True: both sides are 12.
4. Ones: 5 + 8 = 13, write 3, trade 10 ones for 1 ten. Tens: 6 + 7 + 1 = 14 tens, write 4, trade 10 tens for 1 hundred. Hundreds: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6. Answer: 643.
5. 500 is 4 hundreds, 9 tens, 10 ones after regrouping. Ones 10 − 7 = 3, tens 9 − 3 = 6, hundreds 4 − 2 = 2: 263. Check: 263 + 237 = 500.
6. Take apart (put together with one part unknown): 23 − 9 = 14 students. A take-from reading (23 students, 9 leave for art) gives the same equation.
7. 15 + __ = 24. Tuesday: 9 pages (15 + 5 = 20, 20 + 4 = 24, so 5 + 4 = 9).

**Adult self-check:** For every subtraction, have the learner add the answer to the number taken away; they should get the starting number. For every addition, a quick estimate (round to the nearest ten or hundred) should be close to the exact answer.
