# 07.5 — Free Will, Agency & Causation

> *"Man can do what he wills, but cannot will what he wills."* — Arthur Schopenhauer

> *"Free will is what you're doing when you're doing what you want."* — Compatibilism in one sentence

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

1. Distinguish **hard determinism**, **hard incompatibilism**, **compatibilism**, and **libertarianism** about free will.
2. Explain the **Libet experiment** and its interpretation (and its critiques).
3. Describe the **consequence argument** for incompatibilism.
4. State the compatibilist definition of freedom (acting according to reasons/desires without compulsion).
5. Compare **event causation** (Hume), **agent causation** (Reid), and **counterfactual analysis** (Lewis).
6. Apply the free will debate to questions about AI moral responsibility.

## 📚 1. The Free Will Landscape

### 1.1 The Determinist Challenge

**Causal determinism:** Every event, including every human decision, is causally necessitated by prior states of the world plus the laws of nature. The universe is a closed causal system. Given the state of the universe 1 second ago (or 1 billion years ago), every event today is determined.

**The threat:** If determinism is true, you couldn't have done otherwise than you did. Is anyone morally responsible for anything?

### 1.2 Hard Determinism

**Position:** Determinism is true, and determinism is incompatible with free will. Therefore free will does not exist. Moral responsibility based on desert is an illusion.

**Derk Pereboom, Alex Rosenberg** — "free will skeptics"

**Practical implication:** Criminal punishment based on desert is unjustified. Only forward-looking justifications (rehabilitation, deterrence, incapacitation) survive.

### 1.3 Compatibilism

**Position:** Free will and determinism are compatible. "Free will" properly understood is not freedom from causation but freedom from **compulsion, coercion, and internal dysfunction**.

**The classic compatibilist definition (Hume, Frankfurt, Dennett):**
You act freely when:
- Your action flows from your own desires and reasons
- Your desires are the kind you would endorse on reflection
- You are not coerced by external threats
- You are not compelled by an addiction or disorder that bypasses your deliberative capacities

**Frankfurt cases:** A neuroscientist has implanted a device that will force you to choose X if you seem about to choose Y. But you choose X freely (without the device activating). You couldn't have done otherwise, but it was still YOUR choice, driven by YOUR reasons.

### 1.4 Libertarianism (Agent Causation)

**Position:** Determinism is false (or fails to apply to human action), and therefore free will is possible.

**Agent causation (Reid, O'Connor):** Persons are a distinct type of causal agent — they initiate causal chains without being necessitated by prior states. Agent causation is irreducible to event causation.

**Quantum indeterminism:** Does quantum randomness help? Not obviously — random events are just as "not you" as determined events. Freedom requires more than indeterminism.

## 📚 2. The Libet Experiment and Its Interpretation

Benjamin Libet (1983) performed experiments showing that the **readiness potential (RP)** — an electrical build-up in motor cortex — begins approximately **550ms before a voluntary wrist movement**, while subjects reported conscious intention approximately **200ms** before the movement.

**The provocative interpretation:**
- The brain "decides" to move 550ms before the action
- Conscious awareness of the decision comes only 350ms later
- Consciousness appears to be an observer, not the initiator, of voluntary action

**Challenges to Libet's interpretation:**

1. **What does the RP measure?** Later research (Schurger 2012) suggests the RP reflects pre-movement neural noise accumulating to threshold, not a specific "decision." What Libet measured may be motor preparation noise, not a decision signal.

2. **The role of the veto:** Libet himself noted subjects can veto the movement after the RP begins. This "free won't" might be the relevant exercise of free will.

3. **The timing question:** The report of conscious intention is itself a memory reconstructed AFTER the movement. The 200ms figure may be an artefact of retrospective memory distortion.

4. **The action in question:** Moving a wrist at an arbitrary moment is a trivially simple "decision" with no deliberation. It may not generalise to genuine moral decision-making.

**Current view:** The Libet experiment is interesting but far from settling the free will debate. The neural "decision" it identifies may not be the deliberative process relevant to moral responsibility.

## 📚 3. The Consequence Argument (Van Inwagen)

The strongest argument for incompatibilism:

**Premise 1:** Our actions are consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past.
**Premise 2:** The laws of nature and events in the remote past are not up to us.
**Premise 3:** Therefore our actions are not up to us. Therefore we have no free will.

**Compatibilist response:** "Up to us" is equivocal. Our actions ARE "up to us" in the relevant sense (responsive to our reasons, expressions of our character) even if they're caused by prior states. The argument conflates metaphysical determination with practical control.

## 📚 4. Theories of Causation

### 4.1 Humean Event Causation

Hume (1748): Causation is **constant conjunction** — A causes B means A-type events are regularly followed by B-type events. There is no "necessary connection" in the objects — just observed regularity.

**Humean lesson:** We should not smuggle in mysterious causal powers. Causation is a descriptive, not explanatory, concept.

### 4.2 Counterfactual Analysis (Lewis)

A causes B if: "If A had not occurred, B would not have occurred" — in the closest possible world where A doesn't occur.

**Problem cases:** Overdetermination — two bullets hit simultaneously. Remove either one, and the target would still die. Does neither cause the death?

### 4.3 Agent Causation (Reid)

Persons are a distinct type of cause. When you freely act, you — as an agent — initiate a causal chain. This is not reducible to a prior event causing you to cause. You are the *source* of the action, not just a conduit.

**Problem:** How does agent causation fit into the physical causal closure of the world? If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, there's no "gap" for agent causation to fill.

## 📚 5. Free Will and AI Moral Agency

**Can AI systems be moral agents?**

**Under hard determinism:** Neither humans NOR AI systems have genuine moral responsibility. Determinism undermines all agency.

**Under compatibilism:** An AI system could qualify as a moral agent if:
- It acts according to its own goals/values
- Its behaviour is reasons-responsive (it can update on arguments/evidence)
- It is not compelled by external override

**Under libertarianism:** An AI system likely can't be a libertarian free agent (no agent causation in silicon). But libertarianism is the minority view.

**The alignment implication:** If AI systems are reasons-responsive agents under compatibilism, then they may have moral agency — and moral responsibility. This is relevant to debates about AI systems making harmful decisions. Under compatibilist reasoning: if a sufficiently sophisticated AI commits harm according to its own values and without external coercion, it could be morally responsible in the relevant sense.

## ⚠️ 6. Common Misconceptions

1. **"Compatibilism is just giving up on free will."** Compatibilists argue this is the ONLY defensible conception of free will. The libertarian requirement for freedom from causation sets an incoherent standard — "caused by nothing" is not freedom, it's randomness.

2. **"The Libet experiment proved free will doesn't exist."** Libet himself did not draw this conclusion. The experiment's interpretation is deeply contested, and its relevance to genuine deliberative action is unclear.

3. **"Determinism means the future is predictable."** Determinism (every event is causally necessitated) is compatible with radical unpredictability. Chaotic systems are deterministic but effectively unpredictable beyond short horizons.

4. **"Quantum randomness saves free will."** Random events are no more "up to you" than determined events. Quantum indeterminism introduces genuine chance — not control. Free will requires CONTROL, not randomness.

5. **"Compatibilism doesn't explain moral responsibility."** Compatibilists argue it explains the ONLY kind of moral responsibility worth caring about: whether the agent acted on their own reasons, whether they were responsive to moral argument, whether they could have been deterred. The metaphysical sense of "could have done otherwise" is irrelevant to these practical questions.

6. **"Hard determinism means we can't blame anyone."** Hard determinists don't abandon all reactive attitudes — they argue for a forward-looking revision of our practices. We can still restrain people who harm others, treat people as having preferences and reasons, and work to change behaviour through argument and consequences.
