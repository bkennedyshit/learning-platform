# APP-06.8 — Environmental Geography & Sustainability

People change the environment, and the environment shapes people. Farms replace forests, dams reshape rivers, cities spread across plains, and the gases released by burning fuels are warming the planet. These changes are not the same everywhere: some regions lose forests while others gain them, and some communities bear far more pollution than others. Environmental geography studies these patterns and asks how people can meet their needs without undermining the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12) and closes the Geography course. Its thinking skill is **reading environmental evidence carefully**: separating gross from net change, matching time periods before comparing numbers, and paying attention to the ranges and confidence levels scientists report. It is a nonpartisan lesson: it explains evidence and concepts and does not recommend particular policies.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain **human–environment interaction**, **carrying capacity**, and the **tragedy of the commons**, and critique **environmental determinism**.
2. Describe the causes and geography of **deforestation, desertification, and water scarcity**, and calculate deforestation, forest expansion, and net forest change from FAO data.
3. Distinguish **renewable** from **nonrenewable** energy resources and explain why environmental change and resources vary from region to region.
4. Explain **sustainable development**, the **Sustainable Development Goals**, and **environmental justice**, and evaluate an environmental claim using ranges and confidence levels.

## Core ideas

### People and environment

The open textbook *Introduction to Human Geography* (Dorrell and Henderson) calls the relationship between people, culture, and the physical landscape **human–environment interaction**, and stresses that it is **reciprocal**: "culture adapts to a particular place, and that place is changed by people."

An older idea, **environmental determinism**, claimed that climate and landscape fix what kind of people live in a place. Dorrell and Henderson give the example of the ancient Greek writer Hippocrates, who claimed that hot climates produced "lazy, lethargic people" and that his own region produced "the best kind of people." Geographers today reject determinism as "simplistic and reductionist": environments offer possibilities and limits, but people make choices.

**Carrying capacity**, in the OpenStax textbook *Introduction to Sociology*, is "the maximum amount of life that can be sustained within a given area." When a shared resource is open to everyone and no one is responsible for it, users can exceed its carrying capacity. Garrett Hardin called this the **tragedy of the commons** in a 1968 article; OpenStax notes that the economist William Forster Lloyd had described overgrazed common pastures in the 1800s.

### Forests and deforestation

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's *Global Forest Resources Assessment 2020* (FRA 2020) reports:

- The world has **4.06 billion hectares** of forest, **31 percent** of the land area, unevenly distributed.
- **Deforestation** (converting forest to other land uses such as agriculture and infrastructure) removed an estimated **420 million ha** since 1990. The annual rate was about **10 million ha** in 2015–2020, down from 12 million ha in 2010–2015.
- Forest area also **grows**, through planting on land that was not forest (**afforestation**) and through trees regrowing on abandoned land (**natural forest expansion**).
- **Net forest change** = gains (expansion) − losses (deforestation). The world's **net** loss since 1990 is **178 million ha**, "an area about the size of Libya." The net loss rate fell from 7.8 million ha per year in 1990–2000 to 5.2 million in 2000–2010 and 4.7 million in 2010–2020.
- The largest average annual net losses in 2010–2020 were in Brazil (about 1.5 million ha per year), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (about 1.1 million), and Indonesia (about 0.75 million).

FAO has since published FRA 2025; check it for updated figures before using these numbers in current work.

### Desertification and land degradation

The IPCC's *Special Report on Climate Change and Land* defines **desertification** as "land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas resulting from many factors, including climatic variations and human activities." It is not simply deserts spreading on their own. The report finds that:

- Soil erosion from farm fields is estimated at 10 to 20 times (with no tillage) to more than 100 times (with conventional tillage) the rate at which soil forms (medium confidence).
- In 2015, about **500 million** people (range 380–620 million) lived in areas that had experienced desertification between the 1980s and 2000s. The most affected regions include South and East Asia, the area around the Sahara, and the Middle East (low confidence for the regional pattern).

The UN Convention to Combat Desertification works toward a "land degradation neutral world," avoiding, reducing, and reversing degradation.

### Water

Recall from [APP-06.2](/app-062-physical-geography-landforms-bodies-of-water) that only about 2.5 percent of Earth's water is fresh, and most of that is ice or groundwater (USGS). Fresh water is also unevenly distributed in space and time, and it can be overused.

**Case study: the Aral Sea.** NASA's Earth Observatory explains that in the 1960s the Soviet Union diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton and other crops in the deserts of present-day Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan. The Aral Sea, once the fourth-largest lake in the world, shrank and split apart; in 2014 the eastern lobe of the South Aral Sea disappeared completely. Fisheries and the communities that depended on them collapsed; wind-blown salt and dust degraded farmland; and without the moderating influence of the lake, winters became colder and summers hotter and drier. Kazakhstan's Kok-Aral dam, finished in 2005, separated the North Aral, where fisheries have rebounded. In NASA's words, "irrigation made the desert bloom," but "it devastated the Aral Sea."

### Energy resources

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) divides energy sources into two kinds:

- **Nonrenewable** sources have supplies "limited to what we can mine or extract": petroleum, hydrocarbon gas liquids, natural gas, coal, and nuclear energy from uranium.
- **Renewable** sources "are constantly replenished by nature": solar, geothermal, wind, biomass, and hydropower. EIA adds that they are "virtually inexhaustible, but they are limited by availability"; solar energy, for example, is only available when the sun is shining.

Both kinds are unevenly distributed. Fossil fuels occur where geology produced them; sunlight, wind, river flow, and geothermal heat vary with climate, landforms, and plate boundaries. That is why energy is a central topic in economic and political geography.

### Climate change has a geography

The IPCC's *AR6 Synthesis Report* finds that global surface temperature in 2011–2020 was 1.09 °C above 1850–1900, with **larger increases over land (1.59 °C) than over the ocean (0.88 °C)**, and that contributions to greenhouse-gas emissions have been unequal across regions, between and within countries, and among individuals. Warming, rainfall changes, sea-level rise, and species shifts (see [APP-06.3](/app-063-climate-biomes)) all vary from place to place.

### Sustainable development and environmental justice

- In 1987, the UN's **Brundtland Commission** defined sustainability as "meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."
- At UN Headquarters on 25–27 September 2015, world leaders adopted the **2030 Agenda**, with **17 Sustainable Development Goals** and **169 targets**, covering poverty, hunger, health, education, water, energy, cities, climate, oceans, land, and more.
- **Environmental justice** asks whether environmental benefits and burdens are shared fairly. OpenStax defines **environmental racism** as the way neighborhoods populated mainly by people of color and low-income residents "are burdened with a disproportionate number of hazards," such as toxic-waste facilities and dumps. It reports that a twenty-year study led by sociologist Robert Bullard found race "more important than socioeconomic status in predicting the location of the nation's commercial hazardous waste facilities" (Bullard et al., 2007).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Gross and net forest change

**Problem:** Using FRA 2020 figures for 1990–2020, find how much forest was gained through afforestation and natural expansion, and check the decade-by-decade net loss rates for consistency.

1. **Write the relationship:** net change = gains − deforestation, so gains = net change + deforestation.
2. **Substitute:** net change = −178 Mha; deforestation = 420 Mha. Gains = −178 + 420 = **242 Mha** over 30 years, about 8 Mha per year.
3. **Check consistency:** a 178 Mha net loss over 30 years averages 178 ÷ 30 ≈ **5.9 Mha per year**. The three decade rates average (7.8 + 5.2 + 4.7) ÷ 3 ≈ **5.9**. They agree.
4. **Interpret:** the world lost far more forest than the net figure alone suggests; gains in some places offset part of the losses elsewhere. A planted forest or regrowth is also not the same ecosystem as the old-growth forest that was cleared.

### Example 2: Spotting a mismatched comparison

**Problem:** A student writes: "Deforestation was 10 million ha per year and net loss was 4.7 million ha per year, so forests expanded by 5.3 million ha per year." What is wrong?

1. **Check the periods:** the 10 Mha figure is for **2015–2020**. The 4.7 Mha figure is the average for **2010–2020**.
2. **Identify the error:** subtracting rates from different periods mixes two different time spans, so the result is not a valid measurement.
3. **Correct method:** use deforestation and net change for the **same** period, as in Example 1.
4. **General rule:** before combining or comparing two statistics, check that they share the same period, the same area, and the same definitions.

### Example 3: The tragedy of the commons in numbers

**Problem (hypothetical):** Ten herders share a pasture that sustainably supports 100 cattle, 10 each, and each cow is worth 100 units. If one herder adds an 11th cow, overgrazing lowers the value of every cow to 98. Who gains and who loses?

1. **The herder who added a cow:** before, 10 × 100 = 1,000. After, 11 × 98 = **1,078**. A gain of 78.
2. **Each other herder:** before, 1,000. After, 10 × 98 = **980**. A loss of 20 each, or 9 × 20 = 180 in total.
3. **The whole pasture:** before, 100 × 100 = 10,000. After, 101 × 98 = **9,898**. A net loss of 102.
4. **Interpret:** the individual gains while the cost is spread across everyone, so each herder is tempted to add cattle even though the group loses. OpenStax notes that because "no one was held responsible for the land," no one was willing to make sacrifices to protect it.
5. **Limit the claim:** real communities often manage shared resources through rules, customs, and monitoring. The model shows a risk, not an inevitable outcome.

### Example 4: Reading a finding with a range and confidence level

**Statement (IPCC):** "In 2015, about 500 (380–620) million people lived within areas which experienced desertification between the 1980s and 2000s." The regional pattern is given with low confidence.

1. **Identify the best estimate and the range:** best estimate 500 million; plausible range 380 to 620 million, a width of 620 − 380 = **240 million**.
2. **What you can say:** hundreds of millions of people live in areas that experienced desertification; the number is uncertain but large under any value in the range.
3. **What you should not say:** "exactly 500 million," or that the regional ranking is certain, since it carries low confidence.
4. **Write it accurately:** "The IPCC estimates that about 500 million people (range 380–620 million) lived in areas that experienced desertification between the 1980s and 2000s."

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Deforestation and net forest loss are the same."** Deforestation counts losses only; net change subtracts gains from expansion and planting. Between 1990 and 2020, deforestation (420 Mha) was more than twice the net loss (178 Mha).
- **"Desertification means deserts naturally spreading."** The IPCC definition is land degradation in drylands caused by many factors, including human activities as well as climate.
- **"Renewable means unlimited."** EIA calls renewables "virtually inexhaustible," but they are limited by availability in time and place, and building and siting them has environmental effects too.
- **"The environment determines how people live."** Environmental determinism has been rejected; environments set possibilities and limits, and people make choices.
- **"Climate change warms everywhere equally."** Land has warmed more than the ocean, and impacts vary by region.
- **"Environmental problems affect everyone equally."** Research on environmental justice documents unequal exposure to hazards across communities.
- **Boundary: solving one problem can create another.** Irrigation fed crops but devastated the Aral Sea; a dam saved the North Aral's fisheries while limiting flow to the south. Environmental decisions involve trade-offs.

## Practice

1. Define carrying capacity, and explain how the tragedy of the commons can lead a shared resource to exceed it.
2. A region deforests 2.0 Mha per year and gains 0.6 Mha per year through afforestation and natural expansion. Find its net forest change per year. What would the net change be if gains rose to 2.5 Mha per year?
3. Rewrite this inaccurate sentence: "The world lost 420 million hectares of forest between 1990 and 2020."
4. Classify each energy source as renewable or nonrenewable and name one geographic factor that limits it: (a) wind; (b) coal; (c) hydropower; (d) uranium; (e) solar.
5. Using the Aral Sea case, draw or describe a chain of at least four causes and effects, from the river diversions to the effects on people.
6. Quote the Brundtland definition of sustainability, and explain in your own words one tension it identifies.
7. The IPCC reports warming of 1.59 °C over land and 0.88 °C over the ocean (2011–2020 relative to 1850–1900). How many times larger is land warming? Suggest one reason land and ocean might differ.
8. **Suggested activity: local environmental issue brief (individual or pairs, 40–60 minutes).** Choose one environmental issue in your region, such as water supply, tree cover, air quality, or flooding. Using public sources (a government agency, a university, or a reputable news organization), write a one-page brief with: the issue and its location; two pieces of evidence with sources and dates; one trade-off; and one question that the evidence does not yet answer. Do not visit hazardous sites; work from published information and public places only. Present the evidence for different viewpoints fairly; you do not need to argue for a particular policy.
