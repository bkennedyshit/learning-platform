# APP-01.7 — Writing — Sentences to Paragraphs

Reading turns marks on a page into ideas. Writing goes the other way: it turns your ideas into marks on a page that someone else can understand without you there to explain. The basic building block is the **sentence**: a group of words that tells a complete thought. Sentences that work together on one idea make a **paragraph**.

This lesson grows step by step. Younger writers (kindergarten and grade 1) start with one clear sentence. By grades 2-3, writers put several sentences together into a paragraph that stays on one topic and moves smoothly from idea to idea. The course plan places this lesson across grades K-3. The main text talks to the writer. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

Writers should be able to spell simple words by listening for their sounds, as in [APP-01.2 — Phonics — Blending & Decoding](/app-012-phonics-blending-decoding), and know the story elements beginning, problem, and ending from [APP-01.5 — Reading Comprehension — Narrative Text](/app-015-reading-comprehension-narrative-text). Topic sentences and details work like the main idea and details in [APP-01.6 — Reading Comprehension — Informational Text](/app-016-reading-comprehension-informational-text).

> **Adult note:** For kindergartners and early first graders, let the child draw first, then say the sentence aloud, then write it. You may write down a longer sentence the child says (dictation) and have them copy or trace part of it. During first drafts, focus on ideas; spelling and neatness can be fixed while editing.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the writer should be able to:

1. Write complete sentences with a **subject** and a **predicate**, and fix **fragments** and **run-ons**.
2. Write and punctuate the four **sentence types**, and use capital letters correctly.
3. **Expand** and **combine** short sentences.
4. Write and revise a paragraph with a **topic sentence**, **supporting details**, **transitions**, and a **concluding sentence**.

## Core ideas

### What makes a sentence complete

A complete sentence has two parts and tells a whole thought:

| Subject: who or what | Predicate: what the subject does or is |
|---|---|
| *The dog* | *barked at the mail carrier.* |
| *My sister and I* | *built a fort.* |
| *Frogs* | *are amphibians.* |

A **fragment** is part of a sentence that is punctuated as if it were whole. *Ran to the park.* (Who ran?) *Because it was raining.* (What happened because it was raining?)

A **run-on** is two or more sentences jammed together without correct punctuation: *I like soccer my brother likes swimming.* Fix it with a period (*I like soccer. My brother likes swimming.*) or with a comma and a joining word like *and*, *but*, or *so* (*I like soccer, but my brother likes swimming.*).

### Four kinds of sentences

| Type | Job | End mark | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statement (declarative) | tells something | . | *The library opens at nine.* |
| Question (interrogative) | asks something | ? | *When does the library open?* |
| Exclamation (exclamatory) | shows strong feeling | ! | *The library is finally open!* |
| Command (imperative) | tells someone to do something | . or ! | *Return your books by Friday.* |

In a command, the subject is *you*, even though it is not written: *(You) Return your books.*

### Capital letters

Capitalize the first word of every sentence, the word *I*, and **proper nouns**, which are the names of specific people, places, and things: *Maya*, *Ohio*, *Main Street*. Days of the week, months, and holidays are capitalized (*Monday*, *July*, *Thanksgiving*); seasons usually are not (*summer*).

### Building better sentences

- **Expanding:** add words that tell *what kind*, *where*, *when*, or *how*. *The dog napped.* → *The brown dog napped on the couch.*
- **Combining:** join short, choppy sentences that repeat words. *My dog is brown. My dog is big.* → *My brown dog is big.*

### The parts of a paragraph

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Topic sentence | tells what the whole paragraph is about |
| Supporting details (usually 2-4 sentences) | give reasons, facts, examples, or events |
| Concluding sentence | wraps up, often by restating the topic in new words |

A strong paragraph has **unity**: every sentence is about the topic. It also has **coherence**: sentences connect in a sensible order, using **transitions** such as *first*, *next*, *also*, *for example*, *then*, *finally*, and *because*.

### Narrative paragraphs

A paragraph that tells a story follows a small arc: **beginning** (who and where), **middle** (a problem and what happened), and **end** (how it was solved or how the character felt). Time-order transitions such as *one day*, *then*, *after that*, and *at last* help the reader follow along.

### Writing is a process

Writers **plan** (think, talk, draw, or list ideas), **draft** (write it down), **share** (read it to someone), **revise** (improve the ideas and order), and **edit** (fix capitals, punctuation, and spelling). Writers often go back and forth between these steps.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Fixing a fragment and a run-on

Draft: *After lunch. We went outside it was sunny.*

1. Read each "sentence" and ask: "Does it have a subject and a predicate, and is it a complete thought?"
2. *After lunch.* No subject, no action. **Fragment.**
3. *We went outside it was sunny.* Two complete thoughts (*We went outside* / *it was sunny*) with no punctuation between. **Run-on.**
4. Fix the fragment by attaching it to a sentence: *After lunch, we went outside.*
5. Fix the run-on by splitting or joining: *After lunch, we went outside because it was sunny.*
6. Check: one subject (*we*), one main action (*went outside*), and the reason is attached with *because*.

### Example 2: Choosing the sentence type and punctuation

Ideas: a lost cat, asking where it is, telling someone to help, being happy to find it.

1. Telling a fact → statement: *Our cat, Pepper, is missing.*
2. Asking → question: *Have you seen a gray cat?*
3. Telling someone to act → command: *Please call us if you see her.*
4. Strong feeling → exclamation: *We found Pepper under the porch!*
5. Check capitals: *Our*, *Pepper* (a name), *Have*, *Please*, *We*. Check end marks: . ? . !

### Example 3: Expanding and combining

Start: *The bird sang. The bird was small. It sang in the morning.*

1. Notice the repetition: *The bird* is said twice, and *sang* is said twice.
2. Combine the describing word into the first sentence: *The small bird sang.*
3. Add *when*: *The small bird sang in the morning.*
4. Expand with *where*: *The small bird sang in the maple tree every morning.*
5. Decision: three choppy sentences (12 words) became one clear sentence (10 words) that tells the reader more: what kind of bird, where, and how often.

### Example 4: Revising a paragraph for unity and coherence

*Illustrative draft by a second grader:*

> Recess is the best part of my day. I get to play tag with my friends. My mom packs me a sandwich. I can climb on the monkey bars. Recess is fun.

Revision decisions:

1. **Topic sentence:** *Recess is the best part of my day* clearly states the topic. Keep it.
2. **Unity:** *My mom packs me a sandwich* is about lunch, not recess. Remove it.
3. **Development:** only two details remain. Add a third: *When it rains, we play board games in the gym.*
4. **Coherence:** add transitions so the details connect: *First*, *also*, *even when*.
5. **Conclusion:** *Recess is fun* is a little flat and repeats the topic. Restate it with a reason: *That is why I count the minutes until recess every day.*

Revised:

> Recess is the best part of my day. First, I get to play tag with my friends. I can also climb all the way across the monkey bars. Even when it rains, we play board games in the gym. That is why I count the minutes until recess every day.

### Example 5: Planning a short narrative paragraph

1. **Beginning:** *Last Saturday, my grandpa and I went fishing at the lake.*
2. **Middle (problem):** *For two hours, nothing bit, and I wanted to give up.*
3. **Middle (event):** *Then my line jerked so hard that I almost dropped the pole.*
4. **End:** *At last I reeled in a fish as long as my arm, and Grandpa took a picture.*

Each sentence moves the story forward in time, signaled by *Last Saturday*, *For two hours*, *Then*, and *At last*.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A long sentence is a run-on."** Length does not matter. *My cousin, who lives in Texas, sent me a postcard with a picture of a cactus on it* is long but correct. *I ran I fell* is short but a run-on.
- **"Every sentence that starts with *because* is wrong."** *Because it rained, we stayed inside* is a complete sentence. *Because it rained.* by itself is a fragment.
- **"A command is not a sentence because it has no subject."** The subject *you* is understood. *Sit down.* is a complete sentence.
- **"A paragraph is any five sentences."** A paragraph is defined by one main idea, not a number of sentences. Five sentences about five different topics are not a paragraph.
- **"Capitalize any important word."** Capital letters are for sentence beginnings, *I*, and proper nouns. *My Dog is the Best* should be *My dog is the best.* (Titles of books and stories follow different rules.)
- **"Revising means fixing spelling."** Revising is improving ideas, order, and word choice. Fixing spelling and punctuation is **editing**. Both matter, but revise first.

## Practice

1. Write **S** for a complete sentence or **F** for a fragment: (a) *The bus was late.* (b) *Running down the hall.* (c) *Because I was hungry.* (d) *Sit here.*
2. Fix this run-on two different ways: *The movie ended we went home.*
3. Add the correct end mark and name the type: (a) *Where is my backpack* (b) *Put on your coat* (c) *We won the game* (shouted with excitement)
4. Fix the capital letters: *on monday, sam and i went to the zoo in chicago.*
5. Combine into one sentence: *The kite was red. The kite was huge. The kite flew over the park.*
6. Which sentence does not belong in this paragraph? *Dogs make great pets. They are loyal to their owners. Many dogs love to play fetch. Cats like to sleep in the sun. Dogs can even be trained to help people.*
7. Write a topic sentence for a paragraph whose details are: *you can build sandcastles*; *the waves are fun to jump*; *you can look for shells*.
8. Write your own paragraph (4-6 sentences) about your favorite season or a time you learned something new. Score it with the rubric in the answer key.
