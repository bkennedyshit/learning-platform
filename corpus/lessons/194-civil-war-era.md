# 19.4 - Civil War Era

The Civil War (1861–1865) was the deadliest war in American history. An estimated 620,000 soldiers died, roughly 2 percent of the population. It ended slavery in the United States and rewrote the Constitution. Then, in Reconstruction (1865–1877), Americans fought a second struggle over what freedom and citizenship would mean for four million formerly enslaved people. This lesson follows the crisis from the arguments over western territories to the end of Reconstruction.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). It discusses slavery, war, and racial violence directly, because they are central to the history. Its main skill is **sourcing**: asking who created a document, why, and what that tells you.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain how four political decisions from 1820 to 1857 turned the **expansion of slavery** into a national crisis.
2. Use **secession** documents and **Lincoln's First Inaugural** to explain why the war began.
3. Analyze the **Emancipation Proclamation**'s scope and limits.
4. Evaluate **Reconstruction** and its end.

## Core ideas

### The sectional crisis: slavery in the territories

Each time the United States gained territory, Congress had to decide whether slavery would be allowed there. The question mattered because new states meant new senators, and the balance between slave and free states shaped national power.

| Year | Decision | What it did | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1820 | **Missouri Compromise** | Missouri admitted as a slave state and Maine as a free state; slavery banned north of 36°30' in the rest of the Louisiana Territory | Held for 34 years |
| 1850 | **Compromise of 1850** | California admitted as a free state; **popular sovereignty** (settlers vote on slavery) in new territories; slave trade banned in Washington, D.C.; Texas boundary settled; stricter **Fugitive Slave Act** | The fugitive slave law angered many Northerners because it required them to help return people who had escaped |
| 1854 | **Kansas-Nebraska Act** | Repealed the Missouri Compromise line and applied popular sovereignty to Kansas and Nebraska | Pro- and antislavery settlers rushed to Kansas; violence earned the name "Bleeding Kansas"; opponents founded the **Republican Party** to stop slavery's spread |
| 1857 | ***Dred Scott v. Sandford*** | Chief Justice Roger Taney's majority opinion said Black Americans were not citizens of the United States and that Congress had no authority to ban slavery in a federal territory | Declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional and seemed to block any national limit on slavery's expansion |

### Secession and war

Abraham Lincoln, a Republican who opposed slavery's expansion, won the presidency in November 1860. Before he took office, seven Deep South states seceded. Their own declarations explain why. Mississippi's declaration begins: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery-- the greatest material interest of the world." (See Example 1.)

In his First Inaugural Address (March 4, 1861), Lincoln quoted an earlier speech: "I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so." He also said secession was illegal and that he would hold federal property. Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter in April 1861, and four more slave states joined the Confederacy. Four slave states (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri) stayed in the Union and are called the **border states**.

### From a war for Union to a war for freedom

The National Archives explains that at first the North fought "to prevent the secession of the Southern states and preserve the Union," and that "ending slavery was not a goal of the war." Three forces changed that:

1. **Enslaved people's actions.** "From the first days of the Civil War, enslaved people had acted to secure their own liberty," escaping to Union lines.
2. **Military necessity.** After the Union victory at Antietam in September 1862, Lincoln issued the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22. The final **Emancipation Proclamation** took effect January 1, 1863. Lincoln hoped it would weaken the Confederacy and keep Britain and France from supporting it.
3. **Black soldiers.** The Proclamation announced that freed men "will be received into the armed service of the United States." The National Archives reports that roughly 179,000 Black men served in the U.S. Army (about 10 percent of it) and another 19,000 in the Navy; nearly 40,000 Black soldiers died during the war.

In the Gettysburg Address (November 1863), Lincoln described the nation as "dedicated to the proposition that 'all men are created equal'" and called for "a new birth of freedom." The Confederacy surrendered in April 1865.

### Reconstruction, 1865–1877

| Measure | Date | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Freedmen's Bureau | Created March 3, 1865 | A War Department agency to provide relief and help formerly enslaved people; most of its work was done from June 1865 to December 1868; abolished in 1872 |
| **Thirteenth Amendment** | Ratified December 6, 1865 | "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States" |
| Civil Rights Act of 1866 | Veto overridden April 9, 1866 | The first time Congress legislated on civil rights; the House overrode President Andrew Johnson's veto 122–41 |
| **Fourteenth Amendment** | Ratified July 9, 1868 | Citizenship for all persons born or naturalized in the U.S.; no state may deny "due process of law" or "the equal protection of the laws" |
| **Fifteenth Amendment** | Ratified February 3, 1870 | The right to vote "shall not be denied or abridged ... on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude" |

President Andrew Johnson favored a quick, lenient readmission of Southern states. Many Southern governments passed **Black Codes** that, according to *The American Yawp*, "granted some rights to African Americans, like the right to own property, to marry, or to make contracts," but used vagrancy laws and labor contracts to control Black workers. Congress responded with the Reconstruction Acts of 1867, which divided much of the South into five military districts and required new state constitutions that enfranchised Black men. The House impeached Johnson in 1868; the Senate acquitted him.

Black Southerners voted, built schools and churches, reunited families, and held office: "by the end of Reconstruction in 1877, more than two thousand African American men had served in offices ranging from local levee commissioner to U.S. senator." White supremacist groups such as the Ku Klux Klan used terror against Black Americans and Republicans.

### How Reconstruction ended

By the mid-1870s, economic depression and Northern fatigue weakened support for Reconstruction. Violence such as Mississippi's 1875 campaign to intimidate Black voters went unanswered. The 1876 election between Rutherford B. Hayes and Samuel Tilden was disputed; a special electoral commission voted 8–7 for Hayes. In what became known as the **Compromise of 1877**, Democrats accepted Hayes as president, and in April 1877 the remaining federal troops were ordered out of the South. White Democrats regained control of Southern governments, and the rights promised by the amendments went largely unenforced for decades (Lesson 19.5).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Sourcing a secession declaration

1. **Who and when:** the Mississippi secession convention, January 1861, explaining "the prominent reasons which have induced our course."
2. **Audience and purpose:** other states and the public; the convention wanted to justify secession.
3. **What it says:** "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery-- the greatest material interest of the world. Its labor supplies the product which constitutes by far the largest and most important portions of commerce of the earth."
4. **What it shows:** the people who made the decision to secede named slavery as the central reason. This is strong evidence because it comes from the secessionists themselves, at the time, explaining their own motives.
5. **Limits:** it shows the reasons stated by Mississippi's leaders. It does not tell you why each Confederate soldier fought; soldiers' letters and diaries are needed for that question.

**Takeaway:** when historians say slavery caused the war, they rely on evidence like this, written by the secessionists.

### Example 2: Reading what the Emancipation Proclamation actually covered

The Proclamation lists the areas "in rebellion": "Arkansas, Texas, Louisiana, (except the Parishes of St. Bernard, Plaquemines, ... and Orleans, including the City of New Orleans) Mississippi, Alabama, Florida, Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, and Virginia, (except the forty-eight counties designated as West Virginia, and also the counties of Berkley, Accomac, Northampton, ...)."

1. **Find what is listed:** ten Confederate states, with exceptions.
2. **Find what is missing:** Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri (Union border states) do not appear, and neither does Tennessee, a Confederate state largely under Union control by 1863.
3. **Find the exceptions:** parts of Louisiana and Virginia already held by the Union are "left precisely as if this proclamation were not issued."
4. **Reason:** Lincoln issued it as commander in chief, "upon military necessity," so it applied only to enemy territory. Freedom in those areas "depended upon Union military victory" (National Archives).
5. **Significance:** it did not free everyone, but after January 1, 1863, "every advance of federal troops expanded the domain of freedom," and it opened the army to Black men. Ending slavery everywhere required the Thirteenth Amendment.

### Example 3: Comparing the three Reconstruction amendments

| Amendment | Problem it addressed | Key words | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13th | Slavery | "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude" | Exception "as a punishment for crime," which Southern states later used in convict leasing |
| 14th | Citizenship and equal treatment by states | "equal protection of the laws" | Courts read it narrowly for decades (for example, *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 1896) |
| 15th | Voting | "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude" | Did not mention sex, and did not ban literacy tests or poll taxes, which states later used to block Black voters |

**Reasoning:** each amendment answered a specific problem, and each left a gap that opponents used. Understanding the exact wording helps explain why the struggle continued into the 1960s (Lesson 19.7).

### Example 4: Explaining why Reconstruction ended

Rank three causes, with evidence:

1. **Violence and intimidation** (most direct): the Klan and campaigns like Mississippi's in 1875 drove Black voters and Republicans from power.
2. **Declining Northern commitment:** after the Depression of 1873, "economic issues supplanted Reconstruction as the foremost issue on the national agenda," and the federal government "never committed the personnel required" to protect Black Southerners' rights (*American Yawp*).
3. **The political bargain of 1877:** removing the last troops ended federal enforcement.

**Claim:** Reconstruction was overthrown by organized violence, which succeeded because the national government stopped enforcing the new amendments; the Compromise of 1877 made that retreat official.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The war was mainly about states' rights, not slavery."** Secession documents name slavery as the central cause. The "right" the seceding states defended was the right to hold people in slavery and to take slavery into the territories.
- **"Lincoln was an abolitionist from the start."** Lincoln opposed slavery's expansion and said in 1861 that he had no lawful right to interfere with slavery in the states. His policy changed during the war.
- **"The Emancipation Proclamation freed all enslaved people."** It applied only to Confederate areas not under Union control. Slavery in the border states ended with state action and the Thirteenth Amendment (ratified December 1865).
- **"Enslaved people waited to be freed."** Many escaped to Union lines and about 179,000 Black men served in the Army, helping make emancipation a war aim.
- **"Reconstruction failed because Black officeholders were unprepared."** More than 2,000 Black men served in office, and Reconstruction governments created public schools. It was overthrown by violence and the federal government's retreat.
- **"The Thirteenth Amendment ended all forced labor."** It contains an exception for punishment for crime.
- **Boundary: casualty figures.** About 620,000 deaths is a long-standing estimate (American Battlefield Trust); some historians have argued for higher totals. Always name the source of a figure.

> **Note on sources and viewpoints:** This lesson quotes primary sources (Mississippi's secession declaration, Lincoln's First Inaugural and Gettysburg Address, the Emancipation Proclamation, and the constitutional amendments) and uses the National Archives, the U.S. House historian, the Senate, the American Battlefield Trust, and *The American Yawp* for context. The secession declaration contains racist claims; it is quoted as evidence of its authors' motives, which it states directly. The lesson treats slavery as the central cause of secession because the seceding states said so; it describes Reconstruction's end using the interpretation supported by current scholarship, while noting that historians debate how to weigh its causes.
