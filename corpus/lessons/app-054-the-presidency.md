# APP-05.4 — The Presidency

The **president** heads the executive branch, the part of government that carries out (executes) the laws. Article II of the Constitution begins: "The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America." Those few words leave much undefined, and Americans have argued about the size of presidential power since George Washington took office. This lesson separates what the Constitution says from what presidents have done in practice, and shows how Congress and the courts set limits.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). It explains offices and powers, not the record of any particular president. Office holders change; when you need a current name, check an official source such as [usa.gov](https://www.usa.gov/branches-of-government) or the White House website.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. State the president's **qualifications**, **term limits**, and **succession** rules with constitutional citations.
2. Distinguish **formal** from **informal** powers and classify examples.
3. Explain the limits on presidential power in ***Youngstown*** (1952), ***United States v. Nixon*** (1974), and the **War Powers Resolution**.
4. Describe how the **Cabinet**, the **Executive Office of the President**, and federal **agencies** carry out laws, including **rulemaking**.

## Core ideas

### Who can be president, and for how long

- **Qualifications (Article II, Section 1):** a "natural born Citizen," at least 35 years old, and "fourteen Years a Resident within the United States."
- **Term:** four years. The president is chosen by electors, not directly by a national popular vote (Lesson 5.7 explains the Electoral College).
- **Term limit (Twenty-second Amendment, ratified 1951):** "No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice," and a person who served more than two years of someone else's term may be elected only once.
- **Succession and disability (Twenty-fifth Amendment, ratified 1967):** the vice president becomes president if the president dies, resigns, or is removed. If the vice presidency is vacant, the president nominates a replacement, who takes office after confirmation by a majority of both houses of Congress. The amendment also sets procedures for when a president is unable to perform the duties of the office.

### Formal powers

Formal powers are written in the Constitution.

| Power | Text | Check on it |
|---|---|---|
| Commander in chief | "Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States" | Congress declares war, raises and funds the military (Article I, Section 8) |
| Treaties | "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties, provided two thirds of the Senators present concur" | Senate approval |
| Appointments | Nominates ambassadors, judges, "and all other Officers," with Senate advice and consent; may fill vacancies during a Senate recess with commissions that expire at the end of the next session | Senate confirmation |
| Pardons | "Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment" | Applies only to federal offenses; cannot block impeachment |
| Veto | Returns a bill with objections (Article I, Section 7) | Two-thirds override in each chamber |
| Legislative role | "from time to time give to the Congress Information of the State of the Union, and recommend ... such Measures as he shall judge necessary and expedient" | Congress decides whether to act |
| Executing the law | "shall take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" | Courts review whether actions follow the law |
| Opinions from department heads | "may require the Opinion, in writing, of the principal Officer in each of the executive Departments" | Not a power to make law |

### Informal powers

Informal powers are not listed in the Constitution but have developed from the formal ones and from practice.

- **Executive orders.** The National Archives defines them as "official documents, numbered consecutively, through which the President of the United States manages the operations of the Federal Government." They are published in the Federal Register. An executive order must rest on authority from the Constitution or a statute; it can be revoked by a later president, overridden by Congress through a law (subject to veto), or struck down by a court.
- **Executive agreements.** The Senate notes that presidents have "frequently entered the United States into international agreements without the advice and consent of the Senate." They are binding under international law but are not treaties.
- **Executive privilege.** The claim that some presidential communications may be kept confidential. Courts have recognized a limited version (see Example 3).
- **Agenda-setting and persuasion.** Speeches, press conferences, and meetings with members of Congress let a president push issues onto the national agenda. Theodore Roosevelt's phrase "bully pulpit" is often used for this power.
- **Veto threats.** Because overrides are rare (Lesson 5.3), a public statement that the president will veto a bill can change its content before it passes.

### The executive branch the president leads

- **The Cabinet.** USAGov describes it as advisers that "include the vice president, heads of executive departments, and other high-ranking government officials." The word *Cabinet* does not appear in the Constitution; the practice grew from the opinion clause.
- **The 15 executive departments**, listed in federal law (5 U.S.C. § 101): State, Treasury, Defense, Justice, Interior, Agriculture, Commerce, Labor, Health and Human Services, Housing and Urban Development, Transportation, Energy, Education, Veterans Affairs, and Homeland Security. A September 2025 executive order authorized "Department of War" as a "secondary title" for the Department of Defense; the order states that statutory references to the Department of Defense remain, and it directs a recommendation on the legislative and executive actions needed for a permanent change.
- **The Executive Office of the President (EOP)**, which includes offices such as the Office of Management and Budget (which prepares the president's budget) and the National Security Council staff.
- **Independent agencies and regulatory commissions** that are outside the 15 departments, such as the Environmental Protection Agency and the Federal Election Commission.

Agencies carry out laws partly by writing **regulations**. Under the Administrative Procedure Act (5 U.S.C. § 553), an agency generally must publish a "general notice of proposed rule making" in the Federal Register, "give interested persons an opportunity to participate ... through submission of written data, views, or arguments," and publish a final substantive rule "not less than 30 days before its effective date," with listed exceptions. Regulations must be based on authority Congress has given the agency.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Formal or informal?

| Action | Formal or informal | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| The president vetoes a spending bill | Formal | Article I, Section 7 |
| The president pardons a person convicted of a federal crime | Formal | Article II, Section 2 pardon clause |
| The president signs an executive order directing agencies to change a procurement practice | Informal (derived) | Not named in the Constitution; rests on executive power and statutes |
| The president gives a televised address urging Congress to pass a bill | Informal, built on a formal duty | The State of the Union and "recommend ... Measures" clauses support it, but persuasion through mass media is a developed practice |
| The president nominates a Supreme Court justice | Formal | Article II, Section 2 appointments clause |
| The president signs an agreement with another country without sending it to the Senate | Informal | Executive agreement, not a treaty |

**Test to apply:** can you quote a clause that grants the action? If yes, formal. If the action grew from practice or from a general grant such as "executive Power," classify it as informal and name the formal power it relies on.

### Example 2: *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* (1952)

**Facts:** During the Korean War, a steel strike threatened production. President Truman ordered the government to seize and operate the steel mills.

1. **Look for statutory authority.** Oyez summarizes the Court's finding: "there was no congressional statute that authorized the President to take possession of private property."
2. **Look for constitutional authority.** The government argued the commander-in-chief power applied. The Court held that power "did not extend to labor disputes" at home.
3. **Apply separation of powers.** The Court reasoned that "the President's power to see that the laws are faithfully executed refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker." Seizing private property was lawmaking, which belongs to Congress.
4. **Result:** 6–3 against the president.

**Takeaway:** when a president acts without statutory or clear constitutional authority, courts can block the action. In later separation-of-powers cases, judges often cite Justice Robert Jackson's concurrence in *Youngstown*, which says presidential power is strongest when backed by Congress and weakest when it contradicts Congress's will.

### Example 3: *United States v. Nixon* (1974)

**Facts:** During the Watergate investigation, a special prosecutor subpoenaed tape recordings of President Nixon's conversations. Nixon claimed executive privilege.

1. **The question** (Oyez): is executive privilege "entirely immune from judicial review"?
2. **The holding:** No. Neither separation of powers nor "the generalized need for confidentiality of high-level communications" can sustain "an absolute, unqualified" privilege. The Court recognized a limited privilege, especially in military or diplomatic matters, but gave preference to "the fundamental demands of due process of law in the fair administration of justice" in a criminal case.
3. **Result:** 8–0. The president had to produce the tapes. Nixon resigned shortly afterward.

**Takeaway:** executive privilege exists but is not absolute, and courts decide its limits.

### Example 4: Applying the War Powers Resolution's timeline

Congress passed the War Powers Resolution in 1973 over President Nixon's veto. Two provisions set deadlines:

- When armed forces are introduced into hostilities without a declaration of war, the president "shall submit within 48 hours" a written report to the Speaker of the House and the president pro tempore of the Senate (50 U.S.C. § 1543).
- Within "sixty calendar days" after the report is submitted or required, the president must end the use of forces unless Congress declares war, authorizes the action, or extends the period. The period can be extended by up to thirty more days if the president certifies that "unavoidable military necessity" requires it for safe withdrawal (50 U.S.C. § 1544).

**Illustrative timeline:** forces are introduced on March 1 and the report is submitted on March 3, within 48 hours.

1. 60 calendar days after March 3: 28 remaining days in March + 30 in April + 2 in May = 60, so the deadline is **May 2**.
2. If the president certifies a 30-day withdrawal period, the outer limit is **June 1** (May 2 + 29 days to May 31, + 1 = June 1).
3. If Congress passes an authorization before May 2, the deadline no longer applies to that action.

**Boundary:** presidents of both parties have questioned whether the resolution's limits are constitutional, and Congress and presidents have disputed whether particular operations counted as "hostilities." The statute's text is clear about the deadlines; how it applies to a given operation has often been contested.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Executive orders are laws."** They direct the executive branch and must rest on existing constitutional or statutory authority. A later president can revoke them, and courts can strike them down, as *Youngstown* shows.
- **"The president can declare war."** Congress has the power "To declare War" (Article I, Section 8). The president commands the forces once they are authorized, and has acted in emergencies, which is why Congress passed the War Powers Resolution.
- **"The Cabinet is set up in the Constitution."** The Constitution refers to "executive Departments" and their principal officers; the Cabinet developed as a practice, and its membership beyond department heads is the president's choice.
- **"The pardon power covers any crime."** It covers "Offences against the United States," meaning federal offenses, and does not apply to impeachment. State crimes are handled under state law.
- **"Executive privilege lets a president refuse any request."** *United States v. Nixon* rejected an absolute privilege.
- **"Treaties and executive agreements are the same."** A treaty needs two-thirds of senators present; an executive agreement does not go to the Senate for approval.
- **Boundary: the "unitary executive" debate.** Scholars, judges, and presidents disagree about how much control the president must have over agencies, including whether Congress may limit the president's power to remove some officials. Courts continue to decide cases on this question, so describe current law with the date you checked it.
