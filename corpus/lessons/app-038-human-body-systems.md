# APP-03.8 — Human Body Systems

When you run up a flight of stairs, your leg muscles need more energy. Your breathing speeds up to bring in more oxygen, your heart beats faster to deliver it, your digestive system has already supplied the sugar your muscles burn, your nervous system coordinates every step, and your skin starts to sweat to get rid of extra heat. No body system works alone. This lesson describes how the body is organized, takes a closer look at the digestive, circulatory, respiratory, and nervous systems, and explains how the body keeps conditions steady and defends itself against germs.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 7-8. This lesson is educational and is not medical advice.

## Core ideas

### Levels of organization

- **Cells** are the basic units (muscle cells, nerve cells, red blood cells).
- **Tissues** are groups of similar cells working together (muscle tissue).
- **Organs** are structures made of two or more kinds of tissue with a specific job (the heart, the stomach).
- **Organ systems** are groups of organs that work together (the circulatory system).
- The **organism** is the whole living person.

### The eleven organ systems

| System | Main organs | One main function |
|---|---|---|
| Integumentary | skin, hair, nails | protects the body; helps control temperature |
| Skeletal | bones, joints | supports the body; protects organs |
| Muscular | skeletal muscles | moves the body |
| Nervous | brain, spinal cord, nerves | senses and responds; controls the body quickly |
| Endocrine | glands such as the thyroid | sends chemical messages (hormones) |
| Cardiovascular (circulatory) | heart, blood vessels | carries oxygen, nutrients, and wastes |
| Lymphatic and immune | lymph nodes, white blood cells | returns fluid to the blood; fights infection |
| Respiratory | nose, trachea, lungs | brings in oxygen; removes carbon dioxide |
| Digestive | mouth, stomach, intestines, liver | breaks down food and absorbs nutrients |
| Urinary | kidneys, bladder | removes wastes; balances water |
| Reproductive | reproductive organs | produces offspring |

Sorting organs into systems is not perfectly neat: most organs contribute to more than one system.

### The digestive system

**Digestion** breaks food into parts small enough for the body to absorb: proteins into amino acids, fats into fatty acids and glycerol, and carbohydrates into simple sugars.

1. **Mouth:** chewing breaks food apart; saliva begins breaking down starches.
2. **Esophagus:** waves of muscle contraction (**peristalsis**) push food to the stomach.
3. **Stomach:** muscles mix food with stomach acid and digestive juices.
4. **Small intestine:** digestive juices from the pancreas, liver, and the intestine itself finish the job. The small intestine **absorbs most of the nutrients**, which the blood then carries around the body.
5. **Large intestine:** absorbs **water**; what remains becomes stool.

### The circulatory system

**Arteries** carry blood away from the heart. **Veins** carry blood back to the heart. Tiny **capillaries** connect them; their thin walls let oxygen, nutrients, and wastes pass between blood and cells. The heart has four chambers, two **atria** on top and two **ventricles** below, and valves that keep blood flowing one way.

Blood makes two loops:

1. **To the lungs:** oxygen-poor blood from the body enters the **right atrium**, goes to the **right ventricle**, and is pumped through the **pulmonary artery** to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen.
2. **To the body:** oxygen-rich blood returns through the **pulmonary veins** to the **left atrium**, goes to the **left ventricle**, and is pumped out through the **aorta** to the rest of the body.

### The respiratory system

Air travels through the nose or mouth, down the **trachea** (windpipe), into two **bronchi**, then through smaller and smaller **bronchioles**, and finally into tiny air sacs called **alveoli**; your lungs have about 150 million of them. In the alveoli, **oxygen** moves from the air into the blood in the capillaries, and **carbon dioxide** moves from the blood into the air to be breathed out. The **diaphragm**, a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs, helps you breathe.

Why do you need oxygen? Your cells use it in cellular respiration to release energy from glucose, and the carbon dioxide you breathe out is a waste product of that process.

### The nervous system

The **central nervous system (CNS)** is the brain and spinal cord. The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is everything else: the nerves that carry signals between the CNS and the rest of the body. Signals travel along nerve cells called **neurons**. A **reflex**, such as the knee jerk when a doctor taps just below the kneecap, is a fast, automatic response.

### Homeostasis and negative feedback

**Homeostasis** means keeping the body's internal conditions within a healthy range. For example, the set point for normal human body temperature is about **37 °C (98.6 °F)**. Values such as temperature naturally go a little above and below the set point.

The body mainly uses **negative feedback**, which *reverses* a change:

- **Too hot:** blood vessels in the skin widen so more heat leaves through the skin, and sweat glands release sweat, which carries heat away as it evaporates.
- **Too cold:** blood vessels in the skin narrow to keep heat in the core, and if heat loss is severe, the brain triggers **shivering**: muscle contractions that release heat.

### Defending the body

**Germs** (microbes) include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. Most microbes are harmless or even helpful; **pathogens** are the ones that cause disease.

- **Barriers:** the skin blocks most pathogens; tears and mucus contain substances that kill microbes.
- **Innate response:** fast, general defenses that act against any invader.
- **Adaptive response:** slower but specific. **White blood cells** make **antibodies**, proteins that identify and neutralize particular invaders. After an infection is cleared, a few of these white blood cells remain to keep watch, so the body is prepared if the same invader returns.
- **Vaccines** contain an **antigen** (for example, a weakened or killed germ, or a piece of one) that prompts the immune system to make antibodies. This helps the body learn to defend itself without the dangers of a full infection.
- **Antibiotics** fight infections caused by bacteria. They do not work against viruses.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Following an oxygen molecule

Trace one oxygen molecule from the air to a muscle cell in your leg.

1. **Respiratory:** nose → trachea → bronchus → bronchiole → alveolus.
2. **Gas exchange:** oxygen crosses into a capillary around the alveolus.
3. **Circulatory:** pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → arteries → a capillary in the leg muscle.
4. **Cell:** oxygen enters a muscle cell and is used in its mitochondria for cellular respiration.
5. **Return trip for the waste:** carbon dioxide made in the cell enters the blood → veins → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → breathed out.

Check: the trip used two systems plus the cell, and the blood passed through the heart twice.

### Example 2: Following a bite of bread

1. **Mouth:** teeth break the bread apart; saliva begins breaking down its starch.
2. **Esophagus:** peristalsis moves it to the stomach.
3. **Stomach:** it is churned with acid and digestive juices.
4. **Small intestine:** the starch is broken into simple sugars, which are absorbed into the blood.
5. **Circulatory system:** the blood carries the sugar (glucose) to cells.
6. **Cells:** mitochondria use the glucose, with oxygen, to release energy.
7. **Large intestine:** water from the leftover material is absorbed.

### Example 3: A negative-feedback loop on a hot day

You are playing soccer on a hot afternoon, and your body temperature starts to rise above its set point.

1. **Stimulus:** body temperature rises.
2. **Sensors:** temperature-sensing nerve cells detect the change.
3. **Control center:** the brain compares the reading with the set point.
4. **Effectors:** sweat glands and blood vessels in the skin.
5. **Response:** sweating increases and skin blood vessels widen, so more heat leaves the body.
6. **Feedback:** as temperature falls back toward the set point, the response slows down. The response *opposed* the change, which is what makes it **negative** feedback.

### Example 4: Pulse arithmetic and system teamwork

*Illustrative numbers, not a health measurement.* A student counts 18 pulse beats in 15 seconds while sitting, then 30 beats in 15 seconds right after jogging in place for one minute.

1. There are 60 ÷ 15 = 4 fifteen-second periods in a minute.
2. Resting rate: 18 × 4 = **72 beats per minute**.
3. After jogging: 30 × 4 = **120 beats per minute**.
4. Increase: 120 − 72 = **48 beats per minute**.
5. **Explain with systems:** working muscles (muscular) need more energy, so cells use more oxygen and glucose. The nervous system speeds up the heart (circulatory) and breathing (respiratory) to deliver oxygen and remove carbon dioxide faster. The skin (integumentary) sweats to remove the extra heat.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Arteries always carry oxygen-rich blood and veins always carry oxygen-poor blood."** Arteries are defined by carrying blood *away* from the heart. The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood to the lungs, and the pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood back to the heart.
- **"Most food is absorbed in the stomach."** The stomach mixes and breaks down food, but the small intestine absorbs most of the nutrients.
- **"Each organ belongs to only one system."** Most organs contribute to more than one. The pancreas, for example, makes digestive juices and also hormones.
- **"Homeostasis means nothing in the body ever changes."** Conditions such as body temperature constantly shift a little; homeostasis keeps them within a normal range around a set point.
- **"Antibiotics cure colds and the flu."** Colds and the flu are caused by viruses. Antibiotics work against bacteria, not viruses.
- **"All germs are harmful."** Most microbes are harmless or helpful, including many that live on your skin and in your intestines.

3. Where are most nutrients absorbed? Where is most water absorbed?
4. Starting in the right atrium, list the next three places blood goes.
5. What happens in the alveoli?
6. You step outside into freezing weather. Describe two responses your body can make, and explain why each is negative feedback.
7. *Illustrative numbers:* someone counts 21 beats in 15 seconds. What is the rate in beats per minute?
