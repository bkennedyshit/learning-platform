# 43.8 — Reading Latin: Caesar, Cicero, Virgil & Ovid

*Back to [Subject_Plan](Subject_Plan) | [← 43.7](43.7---Advanced-Syntax-—-Subordinate-Clauses-&-Rhetorical-Style)*

> **Chapter goal:** Apply all grammar knowledge to authentic classical Latin texts — develop a reading methodology, understand each author's style and register, learn to use Perseus and commentaries effectively, and begin scanning Latin poetry.

---

## 1. The Reading Methodology

Reading authentic Latin is a skill distinct from knowing Latin grammar. It requires a specific workflow:

### The Three-Pass Method

**Pass 1 — Orient:** Read the sentence all the way through without stopping. Identify the main verb (usually near the end). Identify the subject (nominative). Get a rough sense of the whole.

**Pass 2 — Parse:** Now go back and parse every ambiguous form. Use the case endings to assign grammatical roles. Look up any unknown vocabulary with Lewis & Short or Perseus.

**Pass 3 — Translate:** Construct a smooth English translation. Check that every Latin word is accounted for.

> **The main verb rule:** Latin sentences often end with the main verb. Train your eye to find the verb first, then work backward to the subject, then fill in the rest.

### Using Perseus Digital Library

Perseus (perseus.tufts.edu) is the essential tool for reading classical Latin:
1. Navigate to the text (e.g., Caesar *BG* I.1)
2. Click any word → morphological analysis appears
3. Every form is fully parsed: part of speech, declension/conjugation, case/tense/mood, etc.
4. Lewis & Short dictionary entry appears
5. Use this for every unknown form until parsing becomes automatic (~3–6 months of daily reading)

### Whitaker's Words
Paste any Latin word or passage → full morphological analysis. Available at archives.nd.edu/words.html and as a downloadable app.

## 2. Caesar: *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*

### Why Start with Caesar

*Bellum Gallicum* (58–50 BCE) is the **standard first authentic Latin text** for several reasons:
- **Prose, not poetry:** no scansion required
- **Short, declarative sentences:** low subordinate clause density compared to Cicero
- **Military/geographic context:** highly concrete vocabulary; less abstract
- **Excellent modern commentaries:** Dickinson, Kelsey, Allen & Greenough editions all freely available
- **Cultural value:** Caesar's campaigns shaped Western European geography and culture for centuries

### The Opening — *Bellum Gallicum* I.1

```
Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs, quārum ūnam incolunt Belgae,
aliam Aquītānī, tertiam quī ipsōrum linguā Celtae, nostrā Gallī appellantur.
```

**Parse and translate:**
- *Gallia* — nom. f. sg. (subject)
- *est omnis dīvīsa* — perfect passive periphrastic + *omnis* (adjective with *Gallia*): "is all divided"
- *in partēs trēs* — acc. pl. with *in* (motion-toward): "into three parts"
- *quārum ūnam* — gen. pl. (partitive genitive) + acc. sg.: "of which one"
- *incolunt* — 3rd pl. present active of *incolō* (inhabit): "they inhabit"
- *Belgae* — nom. pl. (subject of *incolunt*)
- *aliam...tertiam* — acc. sg. (parallel with *ūnam*): "(inhabit) another...the third"
- *quī...appellantur* — relative clause: "who...are called"
- *ipsōrum linguā* — abl. of means: "in their own language"
- *nostrā* — sc. *linguā*: "in our language"

**Translation:** "All of Gaul is divided into three parts, one of which the Belgae inhabit, another the Aquitani, the third those who are called Celts in their own language, Gauls in ours."

### Caesar Vocabulary Priorities
*imperō, dūcō, mittō, relinquō, capiō, moveō, premō, superō, proficīscor, perveniō, constituō, nūntiō, certior facio, iter facere, castra pōnere, in deditionem accipere*

### Caesar's Favourite Constructions
- Ablative absolute at sentence opening: *"Hīs rēbus cognitīs..."*
- Indirect statement reporting enemy movements: *"Hostēs nuntiāvērunt sē..."*
- *cum* + pluperfect subjunctive for background: *"Cum Caesar haec audiisset..."*
- Military technical vocab: *agmen* (column of march), *castra* (camp), *acies* (battle line), *legio* (legion), *cohors* (cohort), *centuria* (century)

## 3. Cicero: *In Catilinam* I

### Why Read Cicero After Caesar

Cicero's prose is **harder** than Caesar because of:
- **Long periodic sentences** — the main verb can come after 5+ subordinate clauses
- **Rhetorical vocabulary** — political, legal, moral, emotional register
- **Ellipsis** — Cicero omits words that can be inferred (especially forms of *esse*)
- **Emotional intensity** — *In Catilinam* is one of the most rhetorically charged texts in Western literature

But Cicero is **the most important prose stylist in Latin** — reading him transforms your understanding of the language.

### The Opening — *In Catilinam* I.1

```
Quō usque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā?
Quam diū etiam furor iste tuus nōs ēlūdet?
Quem ad fīnem sēsē effrēnāta iactābit audācia?
```

**Parse and translate:**
- *Quō usque tandem* — "How long, at last...?" (rhetorical adverbial phrase)
- *abūtēre* = *abūtēris* (future passive 2nd sg. of *abūtor*) — "will you abuse?"
- *Catilīna* — voc. sg. (direct address)
- *patientiā nostrā* — abl. of means/specification: "our patience"
- *Quam diū...furor iste tuus...ēlūdet* — "how long...will that madness of yours...elude?"
- *Quem ad fīnem...iactābit audācia* — "to what end will that unbridled audacity flaunt itself?"

**Translation:** "How long, Catiline, will you abuse our patience? How much longer will that madness of yours elude us? To what end will your unbridled audacity flaunt itself?"

### Reading Cicero: Practical Strategy
1. Find the **main verb** (often the last word of the period)
2. Find the **subject** in the nominative
3. Peel off **ablative absolutes** (they're grammatically independent — set them aside)
4. Identify each **subordinate clause** by its conjunction or relative pronoun
5. Read each subordinate clause separately before reassembling into the whole
6. Use the Dickinson *In Catilinam* commentary for vocabulary and grammatical notes

### Recommended Cicero Reading Progression
1. *In Catilinam* I (speeches against Catiline — dramatic, accessible)
2. *De Amīcitiā* (On Friendship — philosophical prose; moderate difficulty)
3. *De Senectūte* (On Old Age — positive, accessible tone)
4. Letters to Atticus (private letters — most informal Latin Cicero wrote)
5. *Prō Archiā* (speech defending a poet — beautiful meditation on literature)

## 4. Virgil: *Aeneid*

### Dactylic Hexameter — Scansion Basics

Latin poetry is **quantitative** — based on patterns of long (—) and short (˘) syllables, not stress. The *Aeneid* uses **dactylic hexameter**: each line has 6 feet; each foot is either a **dactyl** (— ˘ ˘) or a **spondee** (— —); the 5th foot is almost always a dactyl; the 6th is always a spondee or trochee.

**Syllable quantity rules:**
- A syllable is **long** if: it contains a naturally long vowel, a diphthong, or a short vowel followed by two consonants
- A syllable is **short** if: it contains a short vowel followed by at most one consonant

**Elision:** a vowel (or vowel + m) at the end of a word is dropped before a vowel or h at the start of the next word. The syllable is marked but not counted.

### *Aeneid* I.1 — The Opening Line

```
Ārmă vĭ | rumquĕ că | nō, Troi | ae qui | prīmŭs ăb | ōris
```

**Scan:**
- *Arma* — dactyl: Ār-mă-vĭ (arm + vi = spondee/dactyl?)
Full scan: **—˘˘ | —˘˘ | —— | —˘˘ | —˘˘ | ——**
(6 feet; 5th foot = dactyl *prīmŭs ăb*)

**Translation of I.1–7:**
```
Arma virumque canō, Troiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs
Ītaliam, fātō profugus, Lāvīniaque vēnit
lītora, multum ille et terrīs iactātus et altō
vī superum saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram;
multa quoque et bellō passus, dum conderet urbem,
inferretque deōs Latiō, genus unde Latīnum,
Albānīque patrēs, atque altae moenia Rōmae.
```

"I sing of arms and the man, who first came from the shores of Troy to Italy and the Lavinian coast, exiled by fate, much tossed on land and sea by the violence of the gods because of the memorable wrath of fierce Juno; much suffering also in war, until he could found a city and bring his gods into Latium, whence came the Latin race, the Alban fathers, and the walls of lofty Rome."

### Strategy for Reading Virgil
- Use a **commentary** (Pharr's *Aeneid* Books I–VI for learners; R.D. Williams for scholars)
- Identify the verb and subject first — **poetry scrambles word order far more than prose**
- Scan before translating — scanning reveals word emphasis and helps identify elision
- Learn the **epic vocabulary**: *pius* (dutiful/loyal), *fātum* (fate), *arma* (arms), *vīr* (man/hero), *superī* (gods above), *inferi* (underworld), *Iūnō* (the antagonist goddess), *furor* (rage — key Virgilian concept)

## 5. Ovid: *Metamorphoses*

### Why Ovid After Virgil

Ovid (43 BCE–17/18 CE) is often the **second Latin poet** learners tackle because:
- **Vocabulary is more varied and playful** than Virgil
- **Stories are immediately engaging** — the *Metamorphoses* is a narrative of transformation myths
- **Same meter** (dactylic hexameter) as Virgil — one system to learn serves both
- **Witty, ironic tone** — unlike Virgil's gravitas

### *Metamorphoses* I.1–4 — The Opening

```
In nova fert animus mūtātās dīcere fōrmās
corpora; dī, coeptīs (nam vōs mūtāstis et illa)
adspirate meīs prīmāque ab orīgine mundī
ad mea perpetuum dēdūcite tempora carmen.
```

**Translation:** "My spirit moves me to speak of forms changed into new bodies; gods — for you changed those too — breathe inspiration upon my undertaking and lead my song without interruption from the first origin of the world down to my own times."

### Ovid's Style vs. Virgil's
| Feature | Virgil | Ovid |
|---------|--------|------|
| **Tone** | Solemn, elevated, patriotic | Witty, ironic, playful |
| **Syntax** | Complex hypotaxis, rare vocabulary | Clearer syntax, witty enjambment |
| **Subject** | Epic heroism, Roman destiny | Metamorphosis myths, love, transformation |
| **Difficulty** | Harder for vocabulary/density | Easier register; harder to catch the wit |

### Best Entry Points in the *Metamorphoses*
- **Book I** — Creation of the world; the four ages; the flood (Deucalion and Pyrrha); Apollo and Daphne
- **Book III** — Narcissus and Echo (psychologically rich)
- **Book VIII** — Daedalus and Icarus (famous; compact story)
- **Book X** — Orpheus and Eurydice (lyrical; essential cultural touchstone)

## 6. Building a Latin Reading Practice

### Daily Reading Protocol
1. **5–10 lines per session** (quality over quantity for the first year)
2. **Three-pass method** (see §1): orient → parse → translate
3. **Keep a vocabulary notebook** — enter new words with their principal parts: *amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum*
4. **Read aloud with correct pronunciation** — the sound of Latin is part of the meaning, especially in poetry
5. **Use Perseus for every unfamiliar form** until parsing becomes reflexive

### Reading Progression

| Stage | Text | Grammar level needed | Commentary |
|-------|------|---------------------|-----------|
| Beginner | Adapted Caesar passages | 43.2–43.4 | Wheelock exercises |
| Beginner-Int | Nepos *Vitae* (lives of generals) | 43.4–43.5 | Bennett edition |
| Intermediate | Caesar *BG* I–II unadapted | 43.4–43.6 | Dickinson College |
| Int-Advanced | Cicero *In Catilinam* I | 43.5–43.7 | Dickinson College |
| Advanced prose | Cicero *De Amīcitiā*, Livy selections | 43.6–43.7 | Various commentaries |
| Poetry entry | Ovid *Met.* I (with scansion) | 43.6–43.8 | Pharr or Melville trans. |
| Advanced poetry | Virgil *Aeneid* I–II | 43.7–43.8 | Pharr's commentary |

## 7. Cultural Context — Why These Texts Matter

- **Caesar *BG***: Primary source for the history of Gaul, Britain, and Germanic tribes; Caesar's self-justification for an illegal war; shaped Western Europe's geography (Latin as a spoken language in Gaul → French)
- **Cicero *In Catilinam***: The defence of the Roman Republic; the template for political oratory for 2,000 years; Lincoln, Jefferson, and Churchill all studied Cicero
- **Virgil *Aeneid***: The national epic of Rome; foundational text of Western literature; Dante chose Virgil as his guide through Hell precisely because *the Aeneid* was the most important book in medieval education
- **Ovid *Metamorphoses***: The single most influential source for classical mythology in Western literature; Shakespeare, Milton, Chaucer, Keats, Bernini, Titian — all drew from Ovid
