# 19.3 - Expansion & Reform

Between 1800 and 1848 the United States more than tripled in size, from the Atlantic states to the Pacific coast. Canals, steamboats, factories, and the telegraph changed how Americans worked and traded. Voting rights expanded for white men, and reformers demanded more: an end to slavery, rights for women, and moral renewal. The same decades brought the forced removal of Native nations and a war that took half of Mexico's territory. This lesson asks you to hold both stories together.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). Its main skill is **evaluating significance**: deciding which changes mattered most, for whom, and why.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain how the **Louisiana Purchase**, the **War of 1812**, the **Monroe Doctrine**, **Texas annexation**, and the **Mexican-American War** expanded U.S. territory and influence, and identify who bore the costs.
2. Describe the **Market Revolution** and how it linked Northern industry to Southern cotton and slavery.
3. Analyze **Jacksonian democracy** with primary sources.
4. Compare **reform movements** and analyze the **Declaration of Sentiments**.

## Core ideas

### Expansion by purchase, war, and diplomacy

| Year | Event | What changed | Who bore the costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1803 | **Louisiana Purchase** from France | About 530 million acres for 15 million dollars (Office of the Historian) | Native nations living there; the National Archives notes the treaty "ignored the potential impact on Native Americans" |
| 1812–1815 | **War of 1812** with Britain | Fought over British interference with neutral trade and the **impressment** (forced recruitment) of American sailors; the Treaty of Ghent (signed December 24, 1814) restored prewar boundaries | Soldiers on both sides; Native nations allied with Britain lost a powerful partner |
| 1823 | **Monroe Doctrine** | Warned European powers against new colonization or puppet monarchs in the Western Hemisphere | Later used to justify U.S. intervention in Latin America (Lesson 19.5) |
| 1845 | **Texas annexation** by joint resolution of Congress | Texas, independent from Mexico since 1836, joins the Union as a slave state | Mexico, which never recognized Texas independence or the Rio Grande border |
| 1846 | **Oregon Treaty** with Britain | Divides the Oregon Country | Native nations of the Pacific Northwest |
| 1846–1848 | **Mexican-American War** | In the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (February 2, 1848), "Mexico ceded 55 percent of its territory" and recognized the Rio Grande border | Mexico; Mexican citizens and Native peoples in the ceded lands |

The war began in a disputed borderland. Texans claimed the border was the Rio Grande; Mexico said it was the Nueces River farther north. In July 1845, President James K. Polk, "elected on a platform of expansionism," ordered General Zachary Taylor into the land between the two rivers (Office of the Historian). Fighting there led to war in 1846.

Supporters described expansion with the phrase **Manifest Destiny**. Editor John O'Sullivan wrote of "our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions." The phrase was a justification, not a neutral description: it presented expansion as natural and divinely approved, and it ignored the people who already lived on the land.

### The Market Revolution

- **The cotton gin.** Eli Whitney patented his improved cotton gin on March 14, 1794. According to the National Archives, it was the first to clean short-staple cotton, "the yield of raw cotton doubled each decade after 1800," and "by mid-century, America was growing three-quarters of the world's supply of cotton," most of it shipped to mills in England or New England. Enslaved people grew and picked that cotton, so the gin made slavery more profitable, not less.
- **Transportation.** New York completed the **Erie Canal** in 1825, a 350-mile waterway linking the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and the Atlantic (*American Yawp*). Crops from the Great Lakes region could reach eastern cities by water, and manufactured goods could travel west.
- **Communication.** In 1843 Samuel Morse persuaded Congress to fund a 40-mile telegraph line from Washington to Baltimore; within a few years, news crossed the country in days instead of weeks.
- **Factories.** Textile mills in New England turned Southern cotton into cloth, hiring many young women as wage workers.

The result was a national economy with **regional specialties**: Northern industry and commerce, Western grain, and Southern cotton grown by enslaved labor. Those connections made the regions dependent on each other and also raised the stakes of every argument over slavery.

### Jacksonian democracy

By the 1820s most states had dropped property requirements for voting by white men, and party politics became more popular and more combative. Andrew Jackson, elected in 1828, presented himself as the champion of ordinary white Americans against "the rich and powerful."

- **The Bank War.** In his veto of the bill to renew the Second Bank of the United States (July 10, 1832), Jackson wrote: "It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes." He also wrote: "Equality of talents, of education, or of wealth can not be produced by human institutions."
- **Nullification.** Southerners opposed the protective Tariff of 1828, which they called the "Tariff of Abominations." Vice President John C. Calhoun secretly drafted the "South Carolina Exposition and Protest," arguing that the Union was a compact among the states and that a state could nullify (refuse to enforce) a federal law it judged unconstitutional (*American Yawp*). Jackson rejected nullification, and the crisis ended in 1833 with a compromise tariff.
- **Indian Removal.** Jackson signed the **Indian Removal Act** on May 28, 1830, "authorizing the president to grant lands west of the Mississippi in exchange for Indian lands within existing state borders" (Library of Congress).

### Removal and the Trail of Tears

The National Park Service explains that during the 1830s the Choctaw, Muscogee Creek, Seminole, and Chickasaw nations "agreed to be relocated" to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) after resisting the policy. The Cherokee Nation challenged Georgia's laws in court. In ***Worcester v. Georgia*** (1832), the Supreme Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice John Marshall, held 5–1 that Georgia's law "violated the Constitution, treaties, and laws of the United States" (Oyez). The ruling did not stop removal.

In late 1835 "a small, rump faction" of the Cherokee signed the **Treaty of New Echota**, which the Senate ratified in May 1836, although most Cherokees opposed it. In May 1838, U.S. troops and state militia "forcibly evicted more than 16,000 Cherokee Indian people" from their homelands (NPS). The Library of Congress estimates that "approximately 4,000 Cherokees died on this forced march, which became known as the 'Trail of Tears.'"

### Religion and reform

A wave of religious revivals, the **Second Great Awakening**, swept the country in the early 1800s; one of the largest, at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801, drew thousands of people (*American Yawp*). Revivals taught that individuals could choose salvation and improve the world, and many converts joined reform movements:

- **Abolition.** Reformers such as William Lloyd Garrison and Lucretia Mott demanded **immediatism**, "emancipation without delay." Free Black Americans were leaders of the movement from its beginning.
- **Temperance**, a campaign to limit or ban alcohol.
- **Women's rights.** At Seneca Falls, New York, in 1848, reformers including Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott signed the **Declaration of Sentiments** (Example 3).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Weighing significance

**Question:** Which development from 1800 to 1848 was most significant for the future of slavery?

1. **Set criteria.** Significance can mean how many people were affected, how deep the change was, and how long it lasted.
2. **Test candidates.** The cotton gin made slave-grown cotton enormously profitable (yields doubled each decade after 1800). The Louisiana Purchase and the Mexican Cession added huge territories where the question "slave or free?" had to be answered.
3. **Compare.** The gin raised the economic value of slavery; the new territories forced political conflict over its expansion.
4. **Claim:** The cotton gin was most significant economically because it tied the national economy to slavery, but territorial expansion was most significant politically because every new territory reopened the slavery question (Lesson 19.4).

A strong answer names its criteria. Two students can rank events differently and both be well reasoned if they use evidence for their criteria.

### Example 2: The Indian Removal Act and its enforcement

1. **The law's wording:** the act authorized the president to grant western lands "in exchange for" Native lands in existing states (Library of Congress summary).
2. **The legal challenge:** *Worcester v. Georgia* held Georgia's law over Cherokee lands invalid under federal treaties and laws.
3. **What happened:** a treaty signed by a minority faction was ratified anyway, and troops forced more than 16,000 Cherokees from their homes in 1838.
4. **Reasoning:** the gap between a law's wording ("exchange") and its enforcement (forced removal, about 4,000 deaths) is evidence. Historians use both kinds of source: the text of the law and records of how it was carried out.

**Takeaway:** do not judge a policy only by its official language. Compare the text with what happened to the people it affected.

### Example 3: Comparing two declarations

| Declaration of Independence (1776) | Declaration of Sentiments (1848) |
|---|---|
| "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal" | "We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal" |
| Lists the king's "repeated injuries and usurpations" | Lists "repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman" |
| "For imposing Taxes on us without our Consent" | "He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise" (the vote) |

**Reasoning:**

1. **Purpose:** borrowing the structure of the founding document claimed its authority. The authors argued that women were being denied the rights the nation already claimed to believe in.
2. **Evidence:** the 1848 declaration lists specific legal disabilities, for example that a married woman was "in the eye of the law, civilly dead" and had no right to "the wages she earns."
3. **Limits:** the document complains that women were denied rights "given to the most ignorant and degraded men — both natives and foreigners." That line shows the signers' own prejudices about immigrants and others; quote it as evidence of their views, not as fact.

### Example 4: Reading a political cause from a speech

Jackson's bank veto says the rich "bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes," but also that "[e]quality of talents, of education, or of wealth can not be produced by human institutions."

1. **Identify the audience:** Congress and the voting public in an election year (1832).
2. **Identify the argument:** government should not grant special privileges, such as a national bank charter, to a few; it should not try to equalize wealth either.
3. **Connect to context:** this matches Jacksonian appeals to ordinary white male voters against "elites."
4. **Limit:** the same democratic language did not extend to Native nations, enslaved people, or women.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Louisiana Purchase bought empty land."** France sold its claim to the territory. Native nations lived on and governed most of that land, and the United States later took it from them by treaty and force.
- **"The United States won the War of 1812."** The Treaty of Ghent restored the boundaries that existed before the war; neither side gained territory. Americans celebrated it as a victory partly because of battles won late in the war.
- **"Manifest Destiny was a law or official policy."** It was a phrase used by supporters of expansion to justify it.
- **"Industrialization in the North had nothing to do with slavery."** Northern mills processed Southern cotton grown by enslaved people, and Northern merchants and banks profited from the trade.
- **"Jacksonian democracy meant democracy for everyone."** Voting expanded for white men; women, most Black Americans, and Native nations were excluded, and Native nations were removed.
- **"The Supreme Court stopped Indian Removal."** *Worcester v. Georgia* ruled against Georgia, but the Cherokee were removed six years later under the Treaty of New Echota.
- **Boundary: casualty numbers.** About 4,000 deaths on the Trail of Tears is an estimate (Library of Congress); other estimates differ. State the source of any number you use.

3. Summarize the doctrine of nullification in one sentence, and explain why it was a threat to the Union.
