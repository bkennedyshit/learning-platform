# 59.4 - Voice & Style Development

**Voice** is the impression that a particular person, with particular habits of mind, wrote a text. It is built from observable choices: the words a writer reaches for, the shape and pace of the sentences, the examples and details the writer notices, what the writer leaves out, and how the writer addresses the reader. **Style** is the broader set of those choices in a given piece. **Tone** is the attitude a piece takes toward its subject and reader in a particular situation. You keep roughly the same voice across your work; you adjust tone constantly.

Voice is not decoration. Arthur Quiller-Couch, lecturing at Cambridge in 1914, told students that style "is not--can never be--extraneous Ornament." It is the way a writer's thinking shows on the page. This lesson treats voice as something you can analyze, practice, and develop, written for college students and working writers who have mastered clarity (Lesson 59.1) and want their prose to sound like someone in particular.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Describe** a passage's voice in terms of observable features.
2. Distinguish **voice** from **tone** and rate a passage on four tone dimensions.
3. Decide when **first person** and **personal experience** help a text.
4. Use **imitation exercises** to learn structure and rhythm without copying words.

## Core ideas

### What voice is made of

You can describe voice with evidence, the way you would describe any other feature of a text:

| Feature | Questions to ask | Example contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Diction (word choice) | Plain or Latinate? Concrete or abstract? Technical? | *use* versus *utilize*; *the river flooded* versus *a hydrological event occurred* |
| Sentence patterns | Long or short? Cumulative or periodic? Questions? Fragments? | a run of short declaratives versus long, qualified sentences |
| Selection | Which details, examples, and comparisons does the writer choose? | a statistic versus a scene versus a personal anecdote |
| Omission | What does the writer refuse to explain or decline to say? | trusting the reader to infer versus spelling out every step |
| Stance | How does the writer address the reader: expert to novice, peer to peer, *you*, *we*, or no pronouns? | *You'll notice...* versus *It may be observed...* |

The selection and omission rows matter most and are the hardest to fake. Two writers using identical vocabulary will still sound different if one notices the cracked tile in the lab and the other notices the budget line.

### Voice and tone

The Mailchimp Content Style Guide puts the distinction simply: "You have the same voice all the time, but your tone changes," and "Our voice doesn't change much from day to day, but our tone changes all the time." You use one tone at dinner with friends and another in a meeting with your boss, and you change tone again when the person you are addressing is upset.

Nielsen Norman Group offers a vocabulary for analyzing tone. Kate Moran's article on tone of voice identifies four dimensions, each a spectrum: **formal vs. casual**, **serious vs. funny**, **respectful vs. irreverent**, and **matter-of-fact vs. enthusiastic**. Purdue OWL adds that the right level of formality "should be determined by the expectations of your audience and your purpose": formal for an application to an unknown reader, semi-formal for a known colleague, informal only where the venue expects it.

### The persona trap

Many writers, asked to sound academic or professional, adopt a borrowed voice: long abstract nouns, stacked hedges, no actors. The UNC Writing Center warns that when students imitate the complicated style of their textbooks they may "sacrifice communicating and being understood," and it calls the habit of reaching for impressive words "ostentatious erudition." Its advice: "Never use a word you can't clearly define." A credible professional voice is usually plainer than the persona writers imagine.

### First person and personal experience

UNC's handout *Should I Use "I"?* separates two questions: whether to use first-person pronouns and whether to include personal experience. Both depend on field and purpose:

- **Use "I" or "we"** when it adds clarity or names agency: *In our study ... we explored* is clearer than *the question ... was explored*. UNC lists assertiveness, clarity, and positioning your work among others' as benefits.
- **Avoid "I"** when it makes an observable or reproducible phenomenon sound merely subjective, and avoid empty frames such as *I think* or *I believe* that stand in for evidence.
- **Personal experience** helps when it serves the argument (an anecdote that illustrates a principle, a researcher's position relative to the subject) and distracts when it becomes the topic. UNC notes that conventions differ: sciences tend to minimize it; some humanities and social-science work invites it; always check with your instructor or publication.

### Developing voice: read, write, imitate

Voice develops through volume and attention, not through a decision to "have a voice." The oldest deliberate method is **imitation**. In his *Autobiography*, Benjamin Franklin describes teaching himself to write from an odd volume of the *Spectator*: he took "short hints of the sentiment in each sentence," laid them aside for a few days, then, "without looking at the book," tried to rewrite the papers in his own words and compared his version with the original to discover "some of my faults." He also scrambled his notes and later tried to reassemble them "to teach me method in the arrangement of thoughts," and turned prose into verse and back again to build his vocabulary.

The method works because it isolates what a model writer does differently from you, which a quick reading cannot reveal. It is a practice exercise. What you learn is **structure, rhythm, and selection**; the words you submit must be your own (see the integrity note).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Describing voice with evidence

Two illustrative versions of the same update from a lab manager:

**Version A:** *It has been determined that, due to the unforeseen unavailability of the primary centrifuge, the scheduling of experimental procedures requiring its utilization will be subject to modification until further notice.*

**Version B:** *The main centrifuge is down. If your experiment needs it, check the shared calendar before Friday; I'm moving bookings to the backup unit in Room 214 and will post when the repair is done.*

| Feature | A | B |
|---|---|---|
| Diction | Latinate and abstract (*unavailability*, *utilization*, *modification*) | plain and concrete (*down*, *check*, *moving*) |
| Sentence pattern | one long passive sentence | a four-word statement, then a longer instruction |
| Selection | no concrete detail | room number, deadline, backup plan |
| Stance | no person in sight (*It has been determined*) | an *I* who is taking responsibility; *you* addressed directly |
| Tone (NN/g dimensions) | formal, serious, respectful, matter-of-fact | semi-casual, serious, respectful, matter-of-fact |

Reasoning: the two differ less in "formality" than in **selection and stance**. B sounds like a specific person because it contains specific information and someone accepts responsibility for the next step.

### Example 2: Franklin's method on a public-domain model

**Model** (William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style*, rule 13): *Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts.*

1. **Take hints.** *vigorous = concise* / *sentence: no extra words; paragraph: no extra sentences* / *same reason: drawing, lines; machine, parts*.
2. **Wait, then reconstruct without looking.** *(Illustrative attempt)* *Strong writing is brief. Sentences should not carry extra words, nor paragraphs extra sentences, just as a good drawing has no extra lines and a machine no extra parts.*
3. **Compare and record the differences.**
   - Strunk repeats *unnecessary* four times; the attempt switched to *extra*. The repetition is the rhythm: the same word hammers the point.
   - Strunk writes *for the same reason that*, which makes the analogy an argument (drawings and machines are *why*). *Just as* only compares.
   - Strunk's second clause omits the verb (*a paragraph no unnecessary sentences*). The attempt kept that compression, which is worth noticing as a technique.
4. **Imitate the structure with new content (no borrowed words beyond function words):** *Good documentation is specific. A step should contain no vague verbs, a page no undefined terms, for the same reason that a map should have no unlabeled roads and a recipe no unmeasured ingredients.*

Reasoning: step 3 is where learning happens. The comparison exposed three deliberate choices (repetition, causal framing, and verb omission) that a casual reading misses.

### Example 3: One voice, two tones

An illustrative software company has a direct, plain-spoken voice. Two messages:

- **New feature (enthusiastic, casual):** *You can now export reports straight to a spreadsheet. Open any report, choose **Export**, and you're done.*
- **Data incident (matter-of-fact, formal, respectful):** *On March 3, an error exposed some customers' report names to other users for about two hours. We fixed the error and notified affected customers. Here is what happened and what we are changing.*

Reasoning: both are short, concrete, and use *you* and *we*: that is the voice. The incident message drops enthusiasm and casualness because the reader may be worried; Mailchimp's guide makes the same point about adjusting tone to "the emotional state of the person you're addressing."

### Example 4: Deciding on "I"

| Draft | Decision |
|---|---|
| *In this paper, it will be argued that the 1890 census undercounted urban renters.* | Use *I*: *I argue that...* is shorter and names the agent. Check your field's convention. |
| *As I watched the reaction, I noticed the solution turned blue.* (lab report) | Remove *I*: the observation should read as reproducible: *The solution turned blue after 30 seconds.* |
| *I think Aristotle's argument applies to modern cases.* | Delete *I think* and argue the point; UNC notes the reader already assumes the ideas in your paper are yours. |
| *When I tutored at the county jail, three students asked for the same book.* (education essay) | Keep if the anecdote illustrates the argument and is brief; cut if it becomes the subject. |

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Voice means being informal or quirky."** A formal legal brief can have a strong voice. Voice comes from selection, precision, and stance, not from slang or jokes.
- **"Voice is innate; you either have it or you don't."** Franklin's account is a record of building a style deliberately through imitation and comparison.
- **"Imitation is plagiarism."** Imitation as private practice, with the model acknowledged, is a learning exercise. Submitting a model's words as your own is plagiarism. The line is what you submit and how you credit it.
- **"Academic writing has no voice."** Academic prose varies widely; UNC notes that humanities writers often value assertiveness and agency. What academic writing usually lacks is the borrowed, inflated persona, which is a voice too, just a poor one.
- **"Never use 'I' in academic writing."** UNC lists this among overly strict rules; conventions differ by field and are changing. Ask, and use it where it clarifies agency.
- **"A thesaurus makes prose sophisticated."** UNC warns that synonyms carry different connotations and that repeating key terms is often correct.
- **Boundary: organizational voice.** At work you often write in an institutional voice defined by a style guide. Your personal voice then shows mainly in selection and clarity, within those limits.
- **Boundary: generated text.** If you use a writing tool that generates prose, the specific observations, examples, and judgments still have to come from you; generic text has no particular person behind it. Follow your institution's or employer's policy on such tools (Lesson 59.8 discusses disclosure).

## Practice

1. Rate this passage on NN/g's four tone dimensions and justify each rating with a word or phrase: *Heads up! We've totally revamped the dashboard, and honestly, it's kind of amazing. Poke around and tell us what you think.*
2. Using the feature table, describe the voice of a paragraph from a writer you read regularly (a columnist, scholar, or technical blogger). Give one quoted example for each of the five features.
3. Rewrite this sentence in a semi-formal voice for a known colleague, without losing information: *Pursuant to our prior communication, the undersigned wishes to ascertain whether the draft has been reviewed.*
4. For each, decide whether to use *I* (or *we*) and explain: (a) a methods section describing how samples were collected; (b) the introduction to a philosophy paper stating your position; (c) a reflective essay for a teaching portfolio; (d) a résumé bullet.
5. **Suggested activity (imitation).** Choose a paragraph of 60-120 words by a writer you admire, ideally a public-domain text (for example, from Project Gutenberg). Follow Franklin's steps: take hints, wait at least a day, reconstruct without looking, compare, and list three differences you learned from. Keep the model and your reconstruction in a private notebook, not in submitted work.
6. **Extended task.** Write two versions (150-200 words each) of the same message, one to a supervisor and one to a peer team, keeping a consistent voice but changing the tone. Then write a short note explaining the changes in terms of the four tone dimensions. Score it with the rubric.

<summary>Answer key, rubric, and self-check</summary>

1. Casual (*Heads up!*, contractions); somewhat funny or playful (*honestly, it's kind of amazing*); respectful but slightly irreverent toward its own product (self-aware *kind of*); strongly enthusiastic (*totally revamped*, *amazing*). Reasonable ratings with evidence are acceptable.
2. Answers vary. Check that each feature has a quoted example and an explanation, not just a label.
3. Sample: *Following up on my last email: have you had a chance to review the draft?* Any version that is polite, direct, and keeps the request is correct.
4. (a) Usually no *I*, unless the journal or instructor prefers first-person active voice; UNC notes conventions are changing. (b) Usually *I* is appropriate and clear: *I argue that...* (c) Yes: personal reflection is the purpose. (d) No: UNC advises describing experience without a personal pronoun (*Volunteered as a peer counselor*).
5. Answers vary. Strong reflections name specific structural or rhythmic choices (repetition, sentence order, verb choice, what the model omits) rather than general impressions.
6. Use the rubric.

**Voice and tone rubric** (score each row 1-4; 16 points total):

| Row | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consistent voice | both versions recognizably the same writer (selection, diction, stance) | mostly consistent | inconsistent | no recognizable voice |
| Tone adjustment | tone fits each reader; changes are deliberate | fits, with one mismatch | little difference | inappropriate tone |
| Specificity | concrete details and examples a particular writer would choose | some specifics | mostly generic | generic throughout |
| Analysis note | explains changes using the four dimensions with examples | explains most changes | vague | missing |

**Self-check:** Cover the names on both versions and ask a classmate whether the same person wrote them and who each was written for. If they can tell both, you succeeded.

> **Integrity note:** Imitation exercises are for practice. In work you submit, put any words you take from a model in quotation marks with a citation, and do not present another writer's structure so closely that it amounts to a paraphrase without credit. If an assignment or employer restricts writing tools, follow that policy and disclose any permitted use as required.
