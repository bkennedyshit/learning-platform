# 07.4 — Perception, Qualia & Phenomenology

> *"Every experience is an experience of something."* — Edmund Husserl

## 📚 1. Qualia — The Subjective Feel

**Qualia** (singular: quale) are the subjective, experiential properties of mental states. The redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the what-it's-like-ness of tasting coffee.

**Ned Block's terminology:**
- **Phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness):** What it is like — qualia
- **Access consciousness (A-consciousness):** Information that is available for reasoning, report, control

The hard problem from [07.1](07.1---The-Hard-Problem-of-Consciousness) is a problem about P-consciousness. Most functionalists try to eliminate qualia or replace them with functional properties.

### 1.1 Properties of Qualia (Dennett's "Four properties" — that he argues we're wrong about)

**Intrinsic:** Qualia have their character independently of any relation to anything else. The redness of red is how it is, regardless of its role.

**Private:** Qualia are accessible only from the inside. No one else can directly access my qualia.

**Immediately apprehensible:** We know our own qualia directly and certainly.

**Ineffable:** Qualia cannot be fully described in words. Describing redness to a congenitally blind person is impossible.

Dennett's **illusionism** denies that any mental states have these properties — he argues this is a systematic confusion.

## 📚 2. The Inverted Spectrum

**Thought experiment:** Imagine two people, A and B, who are functionally identical — they both say "red" for red things, "green" for green things, and their colour behaviour is indistinguishable. But internally, the subjective experience of red for A is what the subjective experience of green is for B, and vice versa.

**The challenge for functionalism:** If A and B are functionally identical yet have different qualia, then qualia are not functionally defined. Functionalism about qualia is incomplete.

**Replies:**
1. **The coherence reply:** An inverted spectrum is impossible because the phenomenal and functional properties of colour experience are too tightly linked. You can't invert one without affecting the other.
2. **The phenomenal concepts reply:** We have special "phenomenal concepts" that refer to qualia directly — not via their functional role. The inverted spectrum thought experiment exploits this.
3. **The phenomenological continuity reply:** Our emotional responses to colours (red = danger/excitement, green = calm/nature) would be different for inverted-spectrum people, so they wouldn't be functionally identical after all.

## 📚 3. The Knowledge Argument (Mary's Room — Jackson)

**Frank Jackson's thought experiment (1982):**

> Mary is a brilliant colour scientist who has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room. She studies colour vision exhaustively through black-and-white TV and textbooks. She knows every physical fact about colour: the wavelengths, the neural responses in V4, the functional states, everything.
>
> Then Mary is released from the room and sees red for the first time.
> **Does she learn anything new?**

**Jackson's intuition:** Yes, she learns what it's LIKE to see red. She learns a new fact — a phenomenal fact about the subjective character of seeing red. This fact wasn't in her complete physical knowledge.

**The argument:**
1. Mary knows all the physical facts about colour vision before leaving the room
2. She learns something new when she sees red
3. Therefore, there are non-physical facts (phenomenal facts about qualia)
4. Therefore, physicalism is false

### 3.1 Physicalist Replies

**The ability hypothesis (Lewis/Nemirow):** Mary doesn't gain new *propositional* knowledge (a new fact). She gains new *abilities*: the ability to recognise, imagine, and remember red. Abilities are not knowledge-that.

**Criticism:** The ability hypothesis seems to mischaracterise the experience. When Mary says "Now I know what it's like," she seems to be reporting new information, not just new abilities.

**The phenomenal concepts strategy:** Mary gains a new *concept* for something she already knew under a physical description. "The neural state N" and "what red looks like" refer to the same thing via different modes of presentation. No new fact — just a new way to conceptualise the same fact.

**The old fact/new mode reply:** All the facts were in Mary's physical knowledge, but she lacked the phenomenal mode of presentation to grasp them as phenomenal. Learning involves concept acquisition, not fact acquisition.

**Jackson's recantation:** Jackson himself later abandoned the knowledge argument, adopting a representationalist physicalist view. He came to believe the knowledge argument relies on a confusion about phenomenal concepts.

## 📚 4. Phenomenology — The Structure of Experience

Phenomenology is the philosophical study of the *structure* of conscious experience — not its neural or functional basis, but how experience presents itself to the experiencing subject.

### 4.1 Husserl — Intentionality

Edmund Husserl's central insight: **every conscious act is intentional** — it is always directed at an object.

- I see *the cup*
- I fear *the falling*
- I imagine *Napoleon*
- I love *music*

There is no such thing as consciousness in the abstract — free-floating awareness with no object. Consciousness is always "consciousness OF."

**Noesis / Noema:**
- **Noesis:** The mental act itself (seeing, fearing, remembering)
- **Noema:** The intentional content — the object as experienced (the cup as seen from here, now, in this light)

The same object can be given through multiple noemata. The cup seen from the left, from the right, in different lights — all are different noemata of the same cup.

### 4.2 Heidegger — Being-in-the-World

Heidegger's radical departure: consciousness is not a "mind in a body in a world" (Cartesian picture). Being is fundamentally **Being-in-the-world** — we are always already embedded, engaged, oriented.

**Ready-to-hand vs present-at-hand:**
- A hammer being used is *ready-to-hand* — we don't notice it as an object; it's transparent in our engaged activity
- When the hammer breaks, it becomes *present-at-hand* — suddenly an object of attention

**Implication:** The Cartesian model of detached subject contemplating external objects is derivative of our more fundamental engaged involvement with the world.

### 4.3 Merleau-Ponty — Embodiment

**Phenomenology of Perception (1945):** The body is not an object we have but a subject we are.

The **body schema** — the pre-reflective sense of where our limbs are, what we can do — is not a mental representation. It is constitutive of how we experience and move in the world.

**The phantom limb:** An amputee continues to experience a limb that isn't there — not because they believe it's there, but because their body schema hasn't updated. The body schema is primary; beliefs about the body are secondary.

**Skill and embodied cognition:** Expert abilities (driving, typing, piano playing) are not stored as mental representations and retrieved — they're inscribed in the body, enacted without reflection.

**AI implication:** If cognition is fundamentally embodied, then a disembodied AI (a language model without sensorimotor coupling to the world) may be missing something essential about mental life.

## 📚 5. Theories of Perception

| Theory | What we perceive | Mediation |
|--------|-----------------|----------|
| **Naive/direct realism** | External objects directly | None |
| **Scientific direct realism** | Real objects as they are | Causal but direct |
| **Indirect realism** | Sense-data (private representations) | Mediated by sense-data |
| **Idealism (Berkeley)** | Objects ARE ideas | Reality is mental |
| **Constructivism (Helmholtz)** | Unconscious inferences | Brain constructs percept |

**Predictive Processing** (from [07.3](07.3---Computational-Theories-of-Mind)) is a sophisticated form of constructivism — perception is a controlled hallucination shaped by top-down priors and bottom-up prediction error.

## ⚠️ 6. Common Misconceptions

1. **"Qualia are private objects inside the mind."** This is one view (Cartesian). Other views: qualia are representational contents (representationalism), qualia are functional states (functionalism), qualia are structural features of the world (Russellian monism). The nature of qualia is precisely what's at issue.

2. **"Phenomenology is just introspection."** Husserl distinguished phenomenology from psychology. Phenomenology analyses the essential structures of experience, not contingent psychological facts. It's closer to conceptual analysis than self-observation.

3. **"Mary's argument proves dualism."** Jackson himself later rejected the dualist interpretation. The argument is widely debated and has multiple physicalist responses that are not obviously wrong.

4. **"Embodied cognition means minds are in bodies."** That's just Cartesian dualism with a twist. Embodied cognition (Merleau-Ponty, Varela) says the boundary between mind and body/world is not sharp — cognition is constitutively enacted through body-environment coupling.

5. **"Intentionality requires consciousness."** Brentano introduced intentionality as the mark of the mental. But many argue there's unconscious intentionality (unconscious beliefs and desires). Whether intentionality requires phenomenal consciousness is debated.

6. **"The knowledge argument is decisive."** Jackson's own abandonment of it shows it's not. The phenomenal concepts strategy, if successful, accommodates Mary's learning while preserving physicalism. The debate continues.
