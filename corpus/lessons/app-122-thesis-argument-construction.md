# APP-12.2 — Thesis & Argument Construction

A **thesis** is the one-sentence (sometimes two-sentence) answer to the question your essay addresses. It is not the topic and it is not a fact everyone already accepts. The UNC Writing Center describes a thesis as an interpretation of a subject, "not the subject itself," a road map for the paper, and a claim that "others might dispute." An **argument** is the thesis plus the reasons and evidence that make it worth believing, arranged so a skeptical reader can follow them.

This lesson shows how to write and test a thesis, how to see the hidden structure of an argument with the Toulmin model, and how to avoid the reasoning errors called **fallacies**. It is written for high school students; the same tools are used in first-year college writing.

## Prerequisites

[APP-12.1 — The Writing Process](/app-121-the-writing-process): you should know what a working thesis is and expect to revise it. Some familiarity with persuasive techniques from [38.5 — Argumentation & Rhetoric](/385-argumentation-rhetoric) helps but is not required.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Tell a thesis from a topic, a fact, or an announcement, and test a working thesis with five checks.
2. Classify claims by what they assert: what is (or what caused something), what something means, how good it is, or what should be done.
3. Map an argument with **Toulmin's** six parts and add a qualifier and rebuttal to your own argument.
4. Identify and repair common **fallacies**.

## Core ideas

### Topic, fact, announcement, thesis

| Statement | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Mary Shelley's* Frankenstein | Topic | Names a subject; makes no claim |
| *Frankenstein was first published in 1818.* | Fact | True and checkable; no one would argue against it |
| *This essay will discuss responsibility in* Frankenstein. | Announcement | Says what the essay is about, not what it argues |
| *In* Frankenstein, *Victor's abandonment of the creature, more than the act of creating it, causes the novel's tragedy.* | Thesis | An interpretation a reader could dispute and the essay can support with evidence |

Purdue OWL distinguishes three common kinds of papers by their thesis. An **analytical** thesis breaks an issue into parts and evaluates them; an **expository** thesis explains something; an **argumentative** thesis makes a claim and justifies it with evidence. All three need a specific thesis that covers only what the paper discusses. Purdue OWL also notes that the thesis usually appears at the end of the first paragraph, and that your topic may change as you write, so the thesis should be revised to match the finished paper.

### Kinds of claims

Purdue OWL lists the claims an argumentative thesis might make: "an opinion, a policy proposal, an evaluation, a cause-and-effect statement, or an interpretation." A useful way to sort them is by the question each answers:

| Claim about... | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| What is, or what caused it | Is it true? Why did it happen? | *Fact checkers judged website credibility more accurately than historians in one study because they left the site to check it.* |
| What it means (interpretation) | How should we read this? | *Victor's "breathless horror" at his creation reveals that he loved the idea of discovery more than its consequences.* |
| How good it is (evaluation) | Is it effective, fair, or valuable? | *The district's new phone policy is fairer than the old one but harder to enforce.* |
| What should be done (policy) | What action should we take? | *Ninth-grade English classes should teach lateral reading.* |

A factual statement can be a thesis only when reasonable people dispute it, usually because it is about causes, trends, or evidence that requires interpretation. "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level" is not arguable; "The new bus schedule caused the rise in late arrivals" is.

### Five checks for a working thesis

Adapted from the UNC Writing Center's questions:

1. **Does it answer the question?** Reread the prompt. If you rephrased it as a question (Lesson 12.1), your thesis should answer that question directly.
2. **Could someone disagree?** If no one could, you are summarizing, not arguing.
3. **Is it specific?** Vague words like *good*, *bad*, *important*, or *successful* hide the actual claim. Say what makes something good.
4. **Does it pass the "how and why?" test?** If a reader's first response is "How?" or "Why?", add the reasoning.
5. **Does it pass the "so what?" test?** Connect the claim to something that matters to your reader.

### Toulmin's model

The philosopher Stephen Toulmin broke arguments into six parts. Purdue OWL explains that every argument starts with three: the **claim**, the **grounds**, and the **warrant**. The other three are added as needed.

| Part | What it is | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | The assertion you want the audience to accept | What are you arguing? |
| **Grounds** | The evidence and facts that support the claim | What do you have to go on? |
| **Warrant** | The assumption, stated or implied, that connects the grounds to the claim | Why does that evidence support this claim? |
| **Backing** | Additional support for the warrant | Why should I accept your assumption? |
| **Qualifier** | Words that limit the claim (*usually*, *in most cases*, *likely*) | How sure are you, and when does it apply? |
| **Rebuttal** | Acknowledgment of a valid opposing view or exception, and your response | What about the other side? |

Purdue OWL points out that a qualifier or rebuttal builds your **credibility**: it shows you are "a careful, unbiased thinker" rather than someone "blindly pushing" one interpretation. Most weak arguments fail at the warrant, because the writer assumes the reader shares a belief that the reader does not.

### Fallacies

A **fallacy** is an error in reasoning that makes an argument look stronger than it is. The examples below are written for this lesson.

| Fallacy | What goes wrong | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | Attacks the person instead of the argument | *Ignore her proposal for longer lunches; she is always late to class.* |
| **Straw man** | Distorts the opponent's position into a weaker one, then attacks that | *My opponent wants a phone policy, so he must want to ban all technology from school.* |
| **False dichotomy (either/or)** | Presents two options as if they were the only ones | *Either we cancel the field trip or we accept that students will be unsafe.* |
| **Slippery slope** | Claims one step will lead to an extreme outcome through an unsupported chain | *If we allow one retake, soon no one will study for any test.* |
| **Hasty generalization** | Draws a broad conclusion from too little evidence | *Two seniors said the new schedule is terrible, so the whole school hates it.* |
| **Post hoc** | Assumes that because B followed A, A caused B | *Scores rose after we painted the hallways, so the paint improved learning.* |
| **Appeal to authority (misplaced)** | Relies on a famous name or a "supposed authority who really isn't much of an expert" (UNC) | *A famous actor says this diet works, so it must.* |
| **Bandwagon** | Argues that something is right because many people believe it | *Everyone is signing the petition, so you should too.* |
| **Circular reasoning** | The conclusion is just restated as the evidence | *The rule is fair because it treats people justly.* |
| **Red herring** | Changes the subject to avoid the issue | *Maybe the cafeteria food is unhealthy, but the cafeteria staff work very hard.* |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Strengthening a literary thesis

**Prompt:** *How does Mary Shelley's* Frankenstein *present responsibility?*

**Working thesis:** *Frankenstein is a novel about responsibility.*

1. **Answers the question?** Only restates it. It names responsibility but says nothing about *how* the novel presents it.
2. **Debatable?** No. Almost any reader would agree.
3. **Specific?** No. Whose responsibility, for what?
4. **How and why?** The text gives the writer evidence: in Chapter 5, shortly after the creature comes to life, Victor writes, "Unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room." In Chapter 10, the creature tells Victor, "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend."
5. **So what?** The novel invites the question of whether the crime is making the creature or abandoning it, which matters to anyone thinking about the duties creators owe to what they make.

**Revised thesis:** *In* Frankenstein, *Shelley locates Victor's real failure not in creating life but in abandoning the being he created, suggesting that responsibility for a creation begins, rather than ends, at the moment it exists.*

Reasoning: the revision answers *how* the novel presents responsibility, makes a claim another reader could challenge (someone might argue the act of creation itself is the transgression), and points to evidence the essay can analyze.

### Example 2: Mapping a policy argument with Toulmin

**Claim:** Ninth-grade English classes should teach **lateral reading**, the practice of leaving an unfamiliar website to find out what other sources say about it.

1. **Grounds:** In a study by Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew (2019), 10 historians, 10 professional fact checkers, and 25 Stanford undergraduates evaluated live websites. According to the published abstract, historians and students "often fell victim to easily manipulated features of websites, such as official-looking logos and domain names," while fact checkers read laterally and "arrived at more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time."
2. **Warrant:** If a skill clearly improves how people judge online information and even highly educated readers lack it, schools should teach it explicitly.
3. **Backing:** The study's historians had doctorates and still read "vertically," staying on the page, so general education alone did not produce the skill.
4. **Qualifier:** The study compared skilled groups; it did not test a classroom lesson. So the claim should say lateral reading is *likely* to help students and is *worth teaching*, not that one unit will make students as skilled as professional fact checkers.
5. **Rebuttal:** *Objection:* the ninth-grade English curriculum is already full. *Response:* lateral reading can be taught inside the research unit students already complete, using sources they are already evaluating.

Reasoning: writing out the warrant exposes the assumption a skeptical teacher might reject, and the qualifier keeps the claim within what one study of 45 people can support. Lesson 12.4 teaches the lateral-reading moves themselves.

### Example 3: Diagnosing and repairing fallacies

**Argument A:** *We should not listen to the student council's proposal for a later start time. They just want to sleep in.*

- **Fallacy:** ad hominem (it attacks motives instead of the proposal).
- **Repair:** respond to the proposal's actual reasons: *The council's proposal does not address how bus routes shared with the middle school would change, so the board needs a transportation plan before voting.*

**Argument B:** *Either the library stays open until 6 p.m. or students will have nowhere to study.*

- **Fallacy:** false dichotomy (other options exist: classroom study halls, a 5 p.m. closing, or a public library).
- **Repair:** *Keeping the library open until 6 p.m. would give students without quiet space at home a reliable place to study; other options, such as a study hall, would reach fewer students after school.* (The second clause is now a claim the writer must support with evidence.)

**Argument C:** *Our robotics team won after switching to the new software, so the software made us win.*

- **Fallacy:** post hoc (the team may also have practiced more, recruited new members, or faced weaker opponents).
- **Repair:** qualify and look for better evidence: *The new software may have helped; our build-time logs show the team finished the robot two weeks earlier than last year, which gave us more time to practice.*

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"A thesis lists three points."** Many theses preview reasons, but a list of topics (*This essay covers A, B, and C*) is an announcement. The thesis must make a claim about A, B, and C.
- **"A strong thesis sounds absolutely certain."** Overstated claims are easier to refute. Qualifiers such as *often*, *likely*, and *in most cases* make a claim more defensible, not weaker.
- **"Citing an expert is always a fallacy."** Citing a qualified expert on a question in their field is good evidence. The fallacy is relying on a famous name or someone without relevant expertise, or treating one expert's opinion as the end of the debate.
- **"If an argument contains a fallacy, its conclusion is false."** A fallacy means the reasoning does not support the conclusion. The conclusion may still be true for other reasons; you just have not shown it.
- **"Acknowledging the other side weakens my essay."** A rebuttal answers the reader's strongest objection before they raise it, which strengthens your credibility.
- **"Slippery slope is always a fallacy."** Some chains of cause and effect are real. It becomes a fallacy when each step is not supported by evidence.
- **Boundary: thesis placement.** The end of the first paragraph is a convention, not a law. Some genres, such as certain lab reports and narratives, place the main point elsewhere; follow the expectations of your assignment and discipline (Lesson 12.7).

> **Academic integrity note:** Argue in good faith. Represent opposing views as their best advocates would, quote opponents accurately and in context, and never invent evidence to fill a gap in your argument. If you cannot find support for a claim, qualify it or drop it.
