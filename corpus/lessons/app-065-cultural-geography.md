# APP-06.5 — Cultural Geography

Walk down a street in any city and you can read culture in the landscape: the languages on signs, the shapes of houses of worship, the foods for sale, the statues in the square, even how the streets are laid out. Cultural geographers study how these patterns vary from place to place, how they spread, and how people shape places and are shaped by them.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its thinking skill is **describing culture without judging it**: you will practice separating what you can observe from what you infer, and recognizing when a description slips into ranking one culture above another. Cultural and religious identity can be personal. Nothing in this lesson asks you to share your own beliefs or background.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Define **culture** and its material and nonmaterial elements, and distinguish **formal** and **functional** culture regions.
2. Classify examples of **relocation, contagious, and hierarchical diffusion**, and explain how religions and languages spread.
3. Describe how languages are grouped into **families** and why the line between a language and a dialect is partly political, and summarize the current global distribution of **religious groups** using Pew Research Center data.
4. Read a **cultural landscape** by separating what can be observed from what can be inferred, and avoid ethnocentric judgments.

## Core ideas

### What culture is

The OpenStax textbook *Introduction to Sociology* distinguishes two kinds of culture:

- **Material culture** is the physical things people make and use: tools, buildings, clothing, transit passes, buses.
- **Nonmaterial culture** is intangible: values, beliefs, norms (rules of behavior), symbols, and language.

The two are linked. A metro pass is a physical object, but it represents nonmaterial ideas, such as accepting that people pay for transportation. Geographers add a spatial question: **where** do particular culture traits occur, and why there?

**Ethnocentrism** is judging another culture by the standards of one's own, often assuming one's own is superior. Geographers try to describe and explain cultural differences rather than rank them.

### Culture regions

A **region** is an area that shares some characteristic. The open textbook *Introduction to Human Geography* (Dorrell and Henderson) describes two major types:

- **Formal regions** share a uniform characteristic, physical or human, such as a climate type, a majority language, or a dominant religion.
- **Functional (nodal) regions** are organized around a center and linked by connections, such as the area served by a city's newspaper, airport, or transit system.

Many geographers add a third type, **perceptual (vernacular) regions**, which exist in people's minds, such as "the Midwest" or "the South." Their boundaries are fuzzy and differ from person to person.

### Cultural diffusion

**Diffusion** is "the spread of ideas, objects, inventions, and other practices from place to place" (Dorrell and Henderson). The main types:

| Type | How it spreads | Example from the sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Relocation diffusion** | People move and carry culture with them | Migrants bring languages, foods, and religions to new places; missionary work can create "great leaps forward" to distant footholds |
| **Expansion diffusion: contagious** | Spreads outward from person to person, like a contagious disease | Direct person-to-person proselytizing |
| **Expansion diffusion: hierarchical** | Spreads down a hierarchy, from leaders to followers or from large cities to smaller ones | The conversion of a king or emperor, who then influences others |

Dorrell and Henderson note that religions use nearly all these forms, and that today's religious map is a "snapshot" of expansions that happened at different times.

### Cultural landscape

The **cultural landscape** is the visible imprint of a culture on a place. Dorrell and Henderson write that "landscapes are cultural byproducts": architecture, economic activities, clothing, and entertainment are visible to anyone who looks. The relationship runs both ways: culture adapts to a place, and people change the place. They also note that "power is written into the landscape." Who gets a statue, a street name, or a prime location reflects who has had influence.

### The geography of language

- **How many languages?** Estimates range from about 5,000 to 7,000. The exact number depends partly on the "arbitrary distinction between languages and dialects" (Dorrell and Henderson).
- **Language versus dialect.** Swedish, Danish, and Norwegian speakers can usually understand each other, yet each is treated as a separate language for political and historical reasons; Hindi and Urdu are another example. The opposite also happens: spoken Chinese varieties that speakers do not mutually understand are often regarded as one language.
- **Language families.** Languages descended from a common ancestor (a **proto-language**) form a **family**, and families divide into branches and groups. The **Indo-European** family, the largest, is spoken by nearly half of the world's population, across most of Europe, large parts of Asia (including Russia and India), the Americas, Australia, and New Zealand. It spread first across Europe and Asia by a mechanism "still being debated," and later through European colonial expansion, which carried English, French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Russian far from Europe.
- **Endangered languages.** The UN, launching the International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032), reports that even optimistic estimates suggest at least 50 percent of today's spoken languages will be extinct or seriously endangered by 2100; most are Indigenous languages.

### The geography of religion

Dorrell and Henderson divide religions into two types:

- **Universalizing religions** seek to convert others and are open to anyone. Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism are the largest.
- **Ethnic religions** are tied to one group of people and make little or no effort to convert others. **Hinduism** is the largest; **Judaism** is another. Through migration, both have spread around much of the world but remain closely tied to their communities.

Where religions began is often not where most followers live now. Buddhism originated near today's Nepal–India border and spread mainly south and east. Christianity began in the eastern Mediterranean and grew first within the Roman Empire.

**The world in 2020 (Pew Research Center, published 2025).** Based on more than 2,700 censuses and surveys:

| Group | Number, 2020 | Share of world population | Change in share, 2010–2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Christians | 2.3 billion | 28.8% | −1.8 points |
| Muslims | (grew by 347 million) | 25.6% | +1.8 points |
| Religiously unaffiliated ("nones") | 1.9 billion | 24.2% | nearly +1 point |
| Hindus | 1.2 billion | 14.9% | steady |
| Buddhists | 324 million | 4.1% | −0.8 points |
| Jews | 14.8 million | about 0.2% | steady |
| All other religions combined | | 2.2% | steady |

Pew also reports a geographic shift: in 2020, **30.7 percent of the world's Christians lived in sub-Saharan Africa**, compared with 22.3 percent in Europe, making sub-Saharan Africa home to the largest number of Christians.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Classifying diffusion

**Problem:** Classify each case and explain.

- (a) A family moves from one country to another and opens a restaurant serving dishes from home.
- (b) A new slang word spreads among students at one school, then to neighboring schools, as friends talk to friends.
- (c) A clothing style appears first in the largest world cities, then in regional cities, and only later in small towns.
- (d) Around the third century BCE, the Mauryan emperor Ashoka promoted Buddhism after his conquest of Kalinga, and Buddhism spread through his empire. (See [18.1 - Foundations of Civilization](/181-foundations-of-civilization).)

1. **(a) Relocation:** the culture moved because the people moved.
2. **(b) Contagious expansion:** it spread person to person to nearby places, without skipping over anyone.
3. **(c) Hierarchical expansion:** it moved down a hierarchy of places, skipping smaller ones at first.
4. **(d) Hierarchical:** a ruler's support helped spread the religion among his subjects. If followers then carried it to distant lands, that would add relocation diffusion.
5. **Check:** ask two questions. Did people move (relocation), or did the idea move (expansion)? If the idea moved, did it spread to neighbors (contagious) or down a ladder of importance (hierarchical)?

### Example 2: Numbers versus shares in religious data

**Problem:** Pew reports that the number of Christians rose by 122 million from 2010 to 2020, yet their share of the world's population fell 1.8 points. How can both be true? Check the data for consistency.

1. **Distinguish counts from shares:** a group can grow in number but grow more slowly than the total population, so its share falls.
2. **Compare:** Muslims grew by 347 million, almost three times as many (347 ÷ 122 ≈ 2.8), so their share rose. Pew explains that global change in religious groups results from two primary mechanisms: natural increase (births minus deaths) and religious "switching."
3. **Check consistency:** if 2.3 billion Christians are 28.8 percent of the world, the world's population in 2020 was about 2.3 ÷ 0.288 ≈ **8.0 billion**. If 1.9 billion "nones" are 24.2 percent, it was about 1.9 ÷ 0.242 ≈ **7.9 billion**. The two estimates agree within rounding.
4. **Check the total:** 28.8 + 25.6 + 24.2 + 14.9 + 4.1 + 0.2 + 2.2 = **100.0 percent**.
5. **Conclusion:** both statements are true. Reporting a count without the share, or a share without the count, can mislead.

### Example 3: Reading a cultural landscape

**Scenario (hypothetical):** On a city's main street you observe a shop sign in two scripts, a domed building with a crescent on top next to a church with a steeple, a statue of a man on horseback in the central square, and a market selling fresh tortillas and halal meat.

1. **Observations:** two scripts on a sign; two buildings that look like a mosque and a church; an equestrian statue; specific foods.
2. **Possible inferences:** the city has more than one language community; Muslim and Christian communities both have places of worship; someone thought the rider important enough for a statue; the market serves diverse customers.
3. **Limits:** you cannot tell from the landscape alone how many people belong to each group, how long they have lived there, or how they relate to each other. A building's style does not tell you how many people use it.
4. **Questions to research:** Who chose the statue, and when? Which communities arrived by relocation diffusion, and when? This is where "power is written into the landscape" becomes a research question, not an assumption.

### Example 4: Is it a language or a dialect?

**Problem:** A student says, "Two speech varieties are separate languages only if their speakers can't understand each other." Test this rule with Dorrell and Henderson's examples.

1. **Case 1:** Swedish, Danish, and Norwegian speakers can usually communicate, but each is officially a separate language. The rule predicts "one language"; reality says "three."
2. **Case 2:** speakers of different spoken Chinese varieties may not understand each other, yet they are often called one language. The rule predicts "several languages"; common usage says "one."
3. **Conclusion:** mutual understanding is one criterion, but national identity, writing systems, religion, and history also shape what people call a language. The rule is incomplete.
4. **Geographic point:** language boundaries on maps often follow political boundaries, not only linguistic ones.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Culture is only art, music, and food."** It includes nonmaterial elements such as values, norms, and language.
- **"Diffusion always spreads outward from a single center."** Relocation diffusion can jump great distances, and ideas often travel along trade routes and migration paths.
- **"A religion is strongest where it began."** Buddhism and Christianity spread mostly away from their places of origin, and today the largest number of Christians lives in sub-Saharan Africa.
- **"Dialects are 'incorrect' versions of a language."** Dialects are regional or social varieties with their own consistent rules; calling one "correct" is usually a social judgment.
- **"Each country has one culture."** Most states contain several language and religious communities, and culture regions often cross borders.
- **"A culture region has a sharp boundary."** Most culture regions grade into each other, and perceptual regions differ from person to person.
- **Boundary: religious statistics are estimates.** Pew's figures rely on censuses and surveys that ask about identity; they count affiliation, not depth of belief or practice, and some 2020 data were delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic.

3. Classify each example of diffusion and explain: (a) a video dance trend that spreads from friend to friend; (b) a cuisine that arrives with immigrants; (c) a new technology adopted first in capital cities, then in provincial towns.
4. Use the Pew table: (a) Which three groups made up the largest shares in 2020? (b) Which group declined in number? (c) What fraction of the world's people identified with any religion?
