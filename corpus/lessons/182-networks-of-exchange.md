# 18.2 - Networks of Exchange

Between about 1200 and 1450 CE, more people, goods, and ideas moved across Afro-Eurasia than ever before. Mongol armies built the largest land empire the world had yet seen and then protected the merchants who crossed it. Ships timed to the monsoon winds carried pepper, porcelain, and gold between East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China. Camel caravans carried salt south across the Sahara and gold north. These same networks also carried a disease, the plague, that killed a large share of the people in many of the regions they connected.

This lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its historical-thinking skill is **causation**: explaining how one development (connected trade routes) produced several effects, some intended (wealth, cultural exchange) and some not (pandemic disease).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain how the **Mongol Empire** and the **Pax Mongolica** promoted Eurasian trade, citing specific institutions such as the kurultai and the yam relay stations.
2. Describe how **monsoon winds**, the **dhow**, and port cities such as Kilwa, Calicut, and Malacca organized **Indian Ocean trade**, and identify at least four goods that moved through it.
3. Explain how **trans-Saharan trade** in gold and salt enriched the **Mali Empire**, using Mansa Musa's 1324–1325 pilgrimage and Ibn Battuta's account as evidence.
4. Trace how the **Black Death** spread along trade routes and analyze its short- and long-term effects, including conflicting interpretations of its origins.

## Core ideas

### What a "network of exchange" is

A **network of exchange** is a set of routes, places, and people that regularly move goods, ideas, technologies, and diseases between regions. Three networks dominated this period:

| Network | Main route | Key goods | Who carried them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Silk Roads** | Overland across Central Asia, linking China, Persia, and the Black Sea | Silk, porcelain, horses, metals, spices | Caravan merchants, protected by the Mongols after the 1200s |
| **Indian Ocean** | Sea routes from East Africa and Arabia to India, Southeast Asia, and China | Gold, ivory, iron, copper, pepper, gems, silk, porcelain, glass beads | Arab, Persian, Indian, Swahili, Malay, and Chinese merchants |
| **Trans-Saharan** | Camel caravans across the Sahara between North and West Africa | Gold, salt, copper, kola nuts, ivory, enslaved captives | North African and West African merchants |

Each network moved more than goods. Islam spread with Muslim merchants along all three; Buddhism had earlier traveled the Silk Roads; and sailing knowledge passed between sailors from different regions.

### The Mongols and the Pax Mongolica

**Temujin** united the steppe peoples and in **1206** called a **kurultai**, a great assembly whose attendance signaled loyalty, to confirm his rule as **Chinggis (Genghis) Khan**. Mongol armies then conquered northern China, Central Asia, Persia, and the lands of the Rus. The conquests were extremely violent: Hulagu Khan's armies destroyed Baghdad in 1258 and killed the caliph.

Yet the Mongols also valued trade. In a message to the ruler of Khwarazmia, in Central Asia, Chinggis wrote, "We have an equal interest in fostering trade between our subjects." The Mongols built **yam**, relay stations "at one-day intervals" with rest areas and supply depots. Chinggis's successor Ogedei extended the yam to merchants and diplomats and created what historians call the **Pax Mongolica** ("Mongol peace"): a period when one power protected travel across much of Eurasia.

The empire soon divided into four parts: the Chagatai Khanate, the Golden Horde, the Il-Khanate, and the **Yuan dynasty** in China, which Kublai Khan established in the 1270s. The rulers of three of the four (the Chagatai Khanate, the Golden Horde, and the Il-Khanate) eventually converted to Islam, but a shared religion did not stop them from fighting one another.

### The Indian Ocean: trade on the wind

Indian Ocean trade depended on the **monsoon**: seasonal winds that, from May through September, pushed ships from East Africa and Arabia toward western India, then reversed in the winter. Sailors learned to time voyages to these winds. The **dhow**, with its triangular **lateen sail**, could sail "both with and into the wind."

Along the East African coast, Arab and Persian merchants who settled from the eighth century often married into Bantu-speaking families, and port cities such as Mogadishu, Mombasa, Zanzibar, and **Kilwa** grew wealthy. Their people came to speak **Swahili**. Gold from the Kingdom of Mutapa flowed through the port of Sofala, which Kilwa controlled from the 1180s. Swahili elites wore Chinese silks, used Indian glass beads, and ate from Chinese porcelain; some set pieces of porcelain into the walls of their homes. Enslaved people were also traded in East African markets.

Farther east, **Malacca**, on the strait between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, controlled the main sea passage between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Its population included Hindus, Buddhists, and Muslims.

Between **1405** and the early 1430s, China's Ming dynasty sent Admiral **Zheng He** on seven voyages across the Indian Ocean. The first fleet had **317 ships and 27,000 people**. It traded silks and porcelain and collected pearls, pepper, and gems at Calicut on India's coast; later voyages reached Persia, Arabia, and East Africa. Their purpose was "to demonstrate the wealth and power of China" and to bring rulers into a tribute relationship. After the seventh voyage, court officials worried about cost and foreign influence, and the voyages ended.

### West Africa: gold, salt, and the Mali Empire

West Africa had gold; the Sahara had salt. Trade between them built a series of empires: Ghana, then **Mali**, then Songhai. At the salt-mining center of **Taghaza**, the traveler Ibn Battuta reported, even the houses and mosque were built "of rock and salt," and "a camel carries two slabs of it."

The most famous ruler of Mali, **Mansa Musa**, made the Muslim pilgrimage (hajj) to Mecca in **1324–1325**. According to the accounts summarized by OpenStax, his caravan reached Cairo with "sixty thousand soldiers, five hundred captives, and a hundred camel-loads of gold," and he gave away so many gifts that "the value of gold in Cairo fell and did not recover for twelve years." Mali's cities, including **Timbuktu**, became centers of Islamic scholarship as well as trade.

### The Black Death

The **Black Death** was a pandemic of plague, caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*. Its bubonic form, spread mainly by fleas carried by rats and other rodents, had a mortality rate of "30 to 80 percent"; the pneumonic form, spread person to person by coughing, killed 95 to 100 percent of those infected.

Trade networks spread it. Black rats were "an especially inconspicuous stowaway on trade caravans and merchant ships," and the routes protected by the Pax Mongolica carried the disease "further and in new directions." Plague reached the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, and from there Europe, in 1347–1348.

| Region | Evidence of impact (OpenStax) |
|---|---|
| China | An epidemic in 1331–1334; nineteenth-century accounts suggest about thirteen million deaths; disasters were read as a sign the Yuan was losing the Mandate of Heaven |
| Mamluk Egypt and Syria | A population loss of roughly one-third; industries declined while survivors built many madrasas, mosques, and tombs |
| Europe | Severe losses, especially among peasants and laborers; labor shortages followed |
| The Americas and Australia | Entirely spared because of geographic isolation |

**Long-term effects in Europe:** because so many workers died, surviving peasants could demand better terms. Rulers tried to stop this: an English law of 1349 required laborers to accept the wages of 1346, and the **Statute of Labourers** followed in 1351. Over time, labor shortages and social mobility weakened **feudalism**, the system in which serfs owed labor to hereditary landowners.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A causal chain from trade to pandemic

**Question:** How did the Mongol Empire contribute to the spread of the Black Death?

1. **Starting condition:** Mongol rule linked China, Central Asia, Persia, and the Black Sea under protected routes (the Pax Mongolica).
2. **Mechanism:** caravans and ships carried rats and fleas along with goods; famine and environmental change also pushed people to migrate.
3. **Effect:** the plague moved faster and farther than a disease could in a disconnected world, reaching the Mediterranean by 1347–1348.
4. **Qualify it:** the Mongols did not intend this, and trade was one cause among several. Climate crises and grain shortages, for example, may explain why the outbreak was so intense.
5. **Claim:** The connected trade routes the Mongols protected were a **necessary condition** for the plague's rapid spread across Afro-Eurasia, though not its only cause.

### Example 2: Comparing two interpretations of the plague's origins

OpenStax places two historians side by side.

| | Philip Ziegler, *The Black Death* | Monica H. Green, "The Four Black Deaths" |
|---|---|---|
| Main claim | Central Asia, near Lake Issyk-Kul, was "likely ... the cradle of the Black Death," based on an abnormally high death rate in 1338–1339 | The 1340s outbreak was one of four "explosive proliferations" of the bacterium; it was preceded by plague during Mongol sieges in the thirteenth century |
| Main evidence | Memorial stones that attribute deaths to plague | Genetic evidence: over three dozen complete *Yersinia pestis* genomes from historical victims |
| Certainty | "Impossible to be categorical" | Calls for historians to track the humans who spread plague, "not about assigning 'blame'" |

1. **Find the disagreement:** when and where the pandemic began, and how simple the story is.
2. **Find the evidence each uses:** inscriptions (Ziegler) versus genetics combined with documents (Green).
3. **Explain why they differ:** Green writes later, using genetic research that was not available to earlier historians.
4. **Conclusion:** new kinds of evidence can change an interpretation. A careful answer reports both views and explains what evidence supports each.

### Example 3: Sourcing a traveler's account of Mali

Ibn Battuta, a Muslim scholar from Morocco, visited Mali in the 1350s during the reign of Mansa Musa's brother, Mansa Suleyman. He wrote:

> "The people [of Mali] possess some admirable qualities. They are seldom unjust and have a greater abhorrence of injustice than any other people. ... Neither traveler nor inhabitant in it has anything to fear from robbers or men of violence."

1. **Author and audience:** a widely traveled outsider writing for readers in the Islamic world.
2. **What it shows:** a visitor found Mali's roads safe and its ruler strict about justice. That fits a strong state that protected trade.
3. **Point of view:** as a Muslim scholar and an outsider, Ibn Battuta judged Mali by the standards of his own education and travels. Outsiders tend to notice what is unfamiliar or what matters to travelers (here, safety on the roads) and may miss what local people considered important.
4. **Corroborate:** another Arabic writer, al-Umari, described Mali's rulers collecting gold as tribute, which supports the picture of a rich, well-organized state.
5. **Conclusion:** the passage is good evidence of a traveler's experience of security, but a single traveler's impression cannot prove that all of Mali was free of crime.

### Example 4: Continuity and change in the Indian Ocean

**Question:** What changed and what stayed the same in Indian Ocean trade between 1200 and 1450?

1. **Continuity:** monsoon winds still set the sailing calendar; merchants from many religions still traded in shared ports; luxury goods such as pepper, gold, ivory, silk, and porcelain remained central.
2. **Change:** new port states rose, such as Kilwa (controlling Sofala's gold from the 1180s) and Malacca (founded around 1400 on a key strait); Islam spread through merchant communities; China sent large state fleets between 1405 and the 1430s.
3. **Claim:** The basic mechanism of Indian Ocean trade (wind-driven, multi-ethnic, port-based) continued, while **which** ports and states controlled it changed.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Silk Road was a single road."** It was a web of overland routes, and sea routes in the Indian Ocean carried large volumes of goods too. Historians often say "Silk Roads."
- **"The Mongols only destroyed."** Their conquests were extremely violent, including the destruction of Baghdad in 1258, but they also protected merchants, built relay stations, and promoted trade. Both statements are true.
- **"Africa was isolated before Europeans arrived."** West Africa was tied to North Africa and the Middle East by the trans-Saharan trade, and East Africa's Swahili cities traded across the Indian Ocean with Arabia, India, and China.
- **"Mansa Musa's gold crashed Cairo's economy forever."** OpenStax reports the value of gold in Cairo fell and "did not recover for twelve years." That was a significant effect, but a temporary one. Numbers in the caravan accounts may also be exaggerated.
- **"The Black Death was only a European event."** China, the Mamluk lands of Egypt and Syria, and other regions suffered severe losses. Only regions without contact, such as the Americas and Australia, were spared.
- **"The plague spread from person to person everywhere."** The bubonic form spread mainly through fleas. Only the pneumonic form spread directly between people.
- **Boundary: medieval numbers are estimates.** Mortality rates and caravan sizes come from incomplete records. Use words like "roughly" and "about" and name the source.
