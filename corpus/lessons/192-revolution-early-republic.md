# 19.2 - Revolution & Early Republic

In 1763 most colonists were proud to be British subjects; Britain had just won a world war against France. Thirteen years later, the Continental Congress declared independence. This lesson explains how that happened, how the new nation won its war, and how its first leaders quarreled so sharply in the 1790s that they formed the first political parties. The Articles of Confederation and the writing of the Constitution are covered in detail in the Civics course; this lesson links to those lessons rather than repeating them.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). Its main skill is **causation**: explaining how one event led to another, and how strongly.

## Prerequisites

- [19.1 - Colonial America](/191-colonial-america): colonial assemblies, the Navigation Acts, and the Albany Plan.
- Helpful: [APP-05.1 — Foundations of American Democracy](/app-051-foundations-of-american-democracy) for Locke's ideas of natural rights and consent of the governed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Trace the **chain of causes** from the French and Indian War to independence.
2. Analyze the **Declaration of Independence** as a political argument and compare it with the realities of 1776.
3. Explain how **Saratoga, the French alliance, Yorktown, and the Treaty of Paris** secured independence.
4. Explain how disputes in the **1790s** produced the first **political parties** and shaped the election of 1800.

## Core ideas

### From victory to crisis, 1754–1774

The **French and Indian War** (1754–1763) was the North American part of the Seven Years' War between Britain and France. The State Department's Office of the Historian summarizes its result: it "provided Great Britain enormous territorial gains in North America, but disputes over subsequent frontier policy and paying the war's expenses led to colonial discontent, and ultimately to the American Revolution."

| Year | British action | Colonial response |
|---|---|---|
| 1763 | **Royal Proclamation** forbids colonial settlement beyond the Appalachian Mountains, after Pontiac's War with Native nations | Largely ignored; angers settlers and land speculators |
| 1763–1764 | Prime Minister George Grenville lowers duties on sugar and molasses but enforces them strictly; the 1764 **Currency Act** forbids colonial paper money | Letters of protest from New York and Massachusetts |
| 1765 | **Stamp Act** requires stamped paper for legal documents and other paper goods | Virginia's House of Burgesses denies Parliament's authority to tax; riots in Boston; delegates from nine colonies meet (the Stamp Act Congress); boycott of British goods |
| 1766 | Stamp Act repealed; **Declaratory Act** asserts Parliament's power to bind the colonies "in all cases whatsoever" | Celebration of repeal; the principle is left unresolved |
| 1767 | **Townshend Acts** impose new taxes | Renewed resistance, including boycotts |
| 1773 | **Tea Act** gives the East India Company a monopoly on the tax-free transport of tea | December 16: colonists dump tea into Boston Harbor |
| 1774 | Port of Boston closed; laws place Massachusetts under tighter British control (colonists call them the **Intolerable Acts**); General Thomas Gage named military governor | First Continental Congress meets in Philadelphia |

Fighting began at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. On July 2, 1776, Congress adopted Richard Henry Lee's resolution declaring the colonies independent, and on July 4 it adopted the Declaration of Independence explaining why.

### The core argument: representation

The colonists' central complaint was about **who had the right to tax them**, not just the amount. The Stamp Act Congress resolved in October 1765 "that no taxes be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives," and that the colonies "are not, and from their local circumstances cannot be, represented in the House of Commons." Parliament's answer, in the Declaratory Act, was that it could make laws for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." Those two positions could not both be true, which is why repealing one tax did not end the dispute.

### Winning the war, 1775–1783

- **Saratoga (1777):** the surrender of a British army in New York. News of it reached French Foreign Minister Vergennes in December 1777 and helped persuade France to ally with the United States.
- **The French alliance (February 6, 1778):** the Treaty of Alliance and the Treaty of Amity and Commerce. The Office of the Historian calls the French link "the single most important diplomatic success of the colonists."
- **Yorktown (1781):** the French navy "fought off a British fleet, and protected Washington's forces in Virginia," and French assistance "was crucial in securing the British surrender at Yorktown."
- **Treaty of Paris (September 3, 1783):** Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, and John Jay negotiated terms in which Britain accepted American independence and boundaries, settled fishing rights and prewar debts, promised restitution of property lost by Americans loyal to Britain, and agreed to evacuate British forces.

### The new government and the first parties

After independence, the states governed under the Articles of Confederation and then wrote the Constitution (see [APP-05.1](/app-051-foundations-of-american-democracy) and [APP-05.2 — The Constitution — Structure & Principles](/app-052-the-constitution-structure-principles)). George Washington became the first president in 1789. Within a few years his own cabinet split:

- **Alexander Hamilton** (Treasury) and his allies, the **Federalists**, favored a strong central government, close trade ties with Britain, and an active national financial policy.
- **Thomas Jefferson** (State) and James Madison led the **Democratic-Republicans**, who favored a more limited national government and were more sympathetic to revolutionary France.

Foreign policy sharpened the split. When Britain and France went to war in 1793, Washington sided with Hamilton's approach and sent John Jay to London. **Jay's Treaty** (signed November 19, 1794) "proved unpopular with the American public but did accomplish the goal of maintaining peace" with Britain. France, angered by the treaty, began seizing American merchant ships. In the **XYZ Affair** (1797–1798), French intermediaries demanded a loan and a bribe before French officials would negotiate with American envoys. The result was the **Quasi-War**, an undeclared naval war with France, ended by the Convention of 1800.

In 1798 a Federalist Congress passed the **Alien and Sedition Acts**. The National Archives explains that these four laws "raised the residency requirements for citizenship from 5 to 14 years," let the president deport non-citizens, and made it a crime to "print, utter, or publish...any false, scandalous, and malicious writing" about the government. "The only journalists prosecuted under the Sedition Act were editors of Democratic-Republican newspapers." Jefferson and Madison answered with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798–1799). The controversy "contributed to [the Federalists'] defeat in the election of 1800," when Jefferson won the presidency and power passed peacefully from one party to another.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Building a causal chain

**Question:** How did winning the French and Indian War help cause the American Revolution?

1. **Start with the condition.** Britain "emerged from the Seven Years' War burdened by heavy debts" (Office of the Historian).
2. **Link to policy.** To raise revenue and control the frontier, Parliament enforced trade duties, passed the Stamp Act, and issued the Proclamation of 1763.
3. **Link to reaction.** Colonists argued that only their own assemblies could tax them; protests and boycotts followed.
4. **Link to escalation.** Each new British law (Townshend, Tea, Coercive Acts) answered colonial resistance with more control, and each colonial response (boycotts, the Tea Party, the Continental Congress) drew more colonists into organized opposition.
5. **Weigh the causes.** The war's debt was a **long-term** cause; the constitutional dispute over representation was the **central** cause; the Coercive Acts and the fighting of 1775 were **immediate** causes.

**Sample claim:** Britain's victory in 1763 created debts and a larger empire to govern, and Parliament's efforts to tax and control the colonies raised a question neither side would concede: whether Parliament could bind the colonies "in all cases whatsoever."

### Example 2: The Declaration as an argument

Treat the Declaration like an essay with a thesis, evidence, and a conclusion.

| Part | Text | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Principles | People have unalienable rights; governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed"; people may alter or abolish a government that destroys those ends | The major premise |
| Grievances | "For imposing Taxes on us without our Consent"; "For cutting off our Trade with all parts of the world"; "He has kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures" | Evidence that the king's government destroyed those ends |
| Conclusion | The colonies "are, and of Right ought to be Free and Independent States" | The resolution, echoing the Lee Resolution of July 2 |

**Reasoning:** the grievances are aimed at the king, not Parliament, because the argument claims the colonists owed allegiance only to the Crown. Many grievances refer to specific laws in the timeline above (for example, taxes without consent refers to the Stamp and Townshend duties).

**Perspective check:** one grievance accuses the king of trying "to bring on the inhabitants of our frontiers, the merciless Indian Savages." The phrase is evidence of how the authors viewed Native nations, many of whom had reasons to side with Britain after the Proclamation of 1763 limited settlement on their lands.

### Example 3: Comparing principles with practice

In a letter written between March 31 and April 5, 1776, Abigail Adams wrote to her husband John, who was in Congress:

> "in the new Code of Laws which I suppose it will be necessary for you to make I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands."

1. **Source:** a private letter from a well-educated woman to a member of Congress.
2. **Claim it supports:** some Americans in 1776 saw that the language of rights could be applied to groups the leaders were not including.
3. **Limits:** one letter does not show what most women thought. Combine it with other evidence, such as the fact that slavery remained legal in all thirteen states in 1776.

**Takeaway:** the Declaration stated principles that its own authors did not apply to everyone. Later reformers, including the women at Seneca Falls in 1848 (Lesson 19.3) and abolitionists, used its words to demand that they be applied more fully.

### Example 4: Evaluating the Alien and Sedition Acts

**Question:** Were the Sedition Act prosecutions about national security or about partisan politics?

1. **Evidence for security:** in 1798 the country "stood on the brink of war with France," and Federalists feared non-citizens would sympathize with France (National Archives).
2. **Evidence for partisanship:** the only journalists prosecuted were Democratic-Republican editors, and the law punished criticism of the government that Federalists controlled.
3. **Weigh it:** both motives can be true at once. The pattern of prosecutions is strong evidence that the law was used against political opponents.
4. **Consequence:** the backlash helped the Democratic-Republicans win in 1800, and the controversy became one of "the first tests of the limits of freedom of speech and press."

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The colonists rebelled because British taxes were very high."** The dispute centered on the right to tax. After 1763, Prime Minister Grenville actually reduced the duties on sugar and molasses but enforced them more strictly, and the Tea Act made East India Company tea tax-free to transport. Colonists objected to taxation by a Parliament in which they were not represented.
- **"Everyone supported independence."** The Office of the Historian notes that "by 1774, opinion among the colonists was mixed." Many colonists, called Loyalists, sided with Britain; the Treaty of Paris included terms about their property.
- **"Independence was declared only on July 4."** Congress voted for the Lee Resolution on July 2, 1776; the Declaration, adopted July 4, explained the decision to the world.
- **"The Americans won the war alone."** French money, troops, and especially naval power were decisive at Yorktown.
- **"The Founders planned to have political parties."** Washington's Farewell Address (1796) warned "in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally." Parties formed anyway, over real disagreements about government power and foreign policy.
- **"'All men are created equal' meant all people in 1776."** In practice it did not include enslaved people, and women and many men without property could not vote. The phrase became a standard later reformers used.
- **Boundary: "causes" are not equally strong.** A good causal argument ranks causes (long-term, central, immediate) instead of listing them.

## Practice

1. Put these events in order and explain one link between any two of them: Tea Act; Declaratory Act; Proclamation of 1763; Coercive (Intolerable) Acts; Stamp Act.
2. Quote the Stamp Act Congress and the Declaratory Act. In two sentences, explain why repealing the Stamp Act did not settle the dispute.
3. Identify the principle, one grievance, and the conclusion of the Declaration of Independence. Then connect one grievance to a specific British law.
4. Explain why Saratoga mattered for the French alliance, and why the French alliance mattered at Yorktown.
5. Hamilton or Jefferson? Assign each position and explain: (a) stay at peace with Britain through Jay's Treaty; (b) sympathy for revolutionary France; (c) a strong national government with an active financial policy; (d) the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions against the Sedition Act.
6. **Suggested activity: two-sided letter (individual, 30 minutes).** Write two short letters dated 1775: one from a colonist who supports resistance and one from a Loyalist neighbor. Each letter must cite at least one real law or event from this lesson. This is a historical role-play; you are describing views people held then, not your own opinions.
