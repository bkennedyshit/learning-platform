# APP-02.5 — Fractions — Advanced Operations

In the last lesson, fractions with the same denominator were added by counting pieces of the same size. This lesson handles fractions whose pieces are **different sizes**, then multiplies fractions, and finally divides with unit fractions using pictures. Every rule here comes from one idea: you can change how a fraction is cut up without changing how much it is.

The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-02.4 — Fractions — Concepts & Operations](/app-024-fractions-concepts-operations): equivalent fractions (such as $\frac{2}{3} = \frac{8}{12}$), mixed numbers, and adding fractions with the same denominator. From [APP-02.3 — Multiplication & Division](/app-023-multiplication-division): multiplication facts, arrays, and the two meanings of division.

> **Adult note:** Fraction strips still help. For multiplication, square grid paper and two colors of pencil are enough to draw area models. For decimals at the end, a hundreds grid (10 × 10 squares) is useful.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Add and subtract fractions and mixed numbers with different denominators.
2. Multiply a fraction by a whole number or by another fraction, and draw an area model of the product.
3. Say before calculating whether a product will be bigger or smaller than the starting number.
4. Divide a unit fraction by a whole number, and a whole number by a unit fraction, with a drawing; write a fraction as a division and as a decimal.

## Core ideas

### Unlike denominators: make the pieces the same size

$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}$ cannot be found by counting pieces, because halves and thirds are different sizes. Cut both into a size that fits both: sixths. $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{6}$ and $\frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{6}$, so

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{5}{6}.$$

A **common denominator** is any number that both denominators divide into evenly. Multiplying the two denominators always works (2 × 3 = 6). A smaller one, when there is one, keeps the numbers smaller: for $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6}$, 12 works as well as 24.

### Multiplying a fraction by a whole number

$3 \times \frac{2}{5}$ means 3 groups of $\frac{2}{5}$: $\frac{2}{5} + \frac{2}{5} + \frac{2}{5} = \frac{6}{5}$. In general, multiply the whole number by the numerator: $3 \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3 \times 2}{5} = \frac{6}{5} = 1\tfrac{1}{5}$.

### Multiplying a fraction by a fraction

$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4}$ means "$\frac{2}{3}$ **of** $\frac{3}{4}$." On a square:

1. Split it into 4 columns and shade 3 of them: that is $\frac{3}{4}$.
2. Split it into 3 rows and mark 2 of them: that is $\frac{2}{3}$ of what was shaded.
3. The square now has 3 × 4 = 12 small rectangles, and the double-marked region is 2 × 3 = 6 of them.

So $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{2 \times 3}{3 \times 4} = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}$. Multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators. The picture explains why: the denominators count all the small pieces, and the numerators count the pieces in the overlap.

### Multiplying as resizing

Multiplying by a fraction **less than 1** makes a number smaller: $\frac{1}{2} \times 8 = 4$. Multiplying by a fraction **greater than 1** makes it bigger: $\frac{3}{2} \times 8 = 12$. Multiplying by a fraction **equal to 1**, such as $\frac{4}{4}$, leaves the number unchanged. This is also why equivalent fractions work: multiplying by $\frac{2}{2}$ is multiplying by 1.

### Dividing with unit fractions

- **Unit fraction ÷ whole number.** $\frac{1}{2} \div 4$: half of a pan of cornbread is shared equally by 4 people. Cut the half into 4 equal pieces. The whole pan would have 8 such pieces, so each person gets $\frac{1}{8}$ of the pan.
- **Whole number ÷ unit fraction.** $3 \div \frac{1}{4}$: how many quarter-cups are in 3 cups? Each cup holds 4 quarter-cups, so 3 cups hold 3 × 4 = 12. The answer is 12, bigger than 3, because we are counting small pieces.

Notice that $3 \div \frac{1}{4} = 3 \times 4$ and $\frac{1}{2} \div 4 = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{4}$. Dividing by a number gives the same result as multiplying by its flip (reciprocal). In the Common Core State Standards, dividing a fraction by any fraction is a grade 6 topic; here we only preview the pattern.

### Fractions are divisions, and some are easy decimals

$\frac{3}{4}$ also means $3 \div 4$. Share 3 pizzas equally among 4 people: cut each pizza into fourths, and each person gets one fourth from each pizza, so $\frac{3}{4}$ of a pizza.

When a fraction can be rewritten with a denominator of 10 or 100, it is easy to write as a decimal: $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{25}{100} = 0.25$ and $\frac{3}{5} = \frac{6}{10} = 0.6$. Decimals are the subject of [APP-02.6 — Decimals & Place Value](/app-026-decimals-place-value).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: $\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{4}$

1. Thirds and fourths are different sizes. List multiples of each denominator: 3, 6, 9, **12**; 4, 8, **12**. Twelfths fit both.
2. $\frac{2}{3} = \frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4} = \frac{8}{12}$.
3. $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1 \times 3}{4 \times 3} = \frac{3}{12}$.
4. Add the twelfths: $\frac{8}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{11}{12}$.
5. Check size: $\frac{2}{3}$ is a bit more than half and $\frac{1}{4}$ is a quarter, so the sum should be a bit less than 1. $\frac{11}{12}$ is just under 1, so it is reasonable.

### Example 2: $3\tfrac{1}{2} - 1\tfrac{3}{4}$

1. Rewrite both as fractions: $3\tfrac{1}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$ and $1\tfrac{3}{4} = \frac{7}{4}$.
2. Common denominator 4: $\frac{7}{2} = \frac{14}{4}$.
3. Subtract: $\frac{14}{4} - \frac{7}{4} = \frac{7}{4}$.
4. Rewrite as a mixed number: $\frac{7}{4} = 1\tfrac{3}{4}$.
5. Check by adding back: $1\tfrac{3}{4} + 1\tfrac{3}{4} = 2\tfrac{6}{4} = 3\tfrac{2}{4} = 3\tfrac{1}{2}$. Correct.

### Example 3: Area of a garden bed with fraction sides

*Illustrative story.* A garden bed is $\frac{3}{4}$ meter long and $\frac{1}{2}$ meter wide. What is its area?

1. Area of a rectangle is length × width: $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{2}$.
2. Draw a 1 meter by 1 meter square. Split the length into 4 parts and the width into 2 parts, making 8 equal small rectangles, each $\frac{1}{8}$ square meter.
3. The bed covers 3 columns in 1 row: 3 small rectangles.
4. So the area is $\frac{3}{8}$ square meter, matching $\frac{3 \times 1}{4 \times 2} = \frac{3}{8}$.
5. Check: both sides are less than 1 meter, so the area must be less than 1 square meter.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **Adding tops and bottoms.** $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}$ is not $\frac{2}{5}$. Quick test: $\frac{2}{5}$ is less than $\frac{1}{2}$, but adding $\frac{1}{3}$ to $\frac{1}{2}$ should give more than $\frac{1}{2}$.
- **"Multiplying always makes bigger, dividing always makes smaller."** $\frac{1}{2} \times 8 = 4$ is smaller than 8, and $3 \div \frac{1}{4} = 12$ is bigger than 3.
- **Thinking multiplication needs a common denominator.** It does not: $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4}$ is found directly. Common denominators are needed for adding and subtracting.
- **Losing the whole in a mixed-number subtraction.** In $3\tfrac{1}{2} - 1\tfrac{3}{4}$, subtracting $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{4}$ alone is not possible with these numbers; rename a whole as fourths first, or convert to fractions as in Example 2.
- **Dividing $\frac{1}{2} \div 4$ and getting 2.** That answers "how many 4s fit into one half," which is the reverse question. A picture of half a pan cut into 4 shows each piece is $\frac{1}{8}$.

## Practice

1. Find $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{5}$.
2. Find $\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{4}$.
3. Find $2\tfrac{2}{3} + 1\tfrac{1}{2}$.
4. Find $4 \times \frac{3}{8}$ and write the answer as a mixed number.
5. Draw an area model for $\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{3}{5}$ and give the product.
6. Without calculating, is $\frac{5}{6} \times 30$ more or less than 30? Then calculate it.
7. *Illustrative story:* A ribbon $\frac{1}{3}$ yard long is cut into 2 equal pieces. How long is each piece?
8. *Illustrative story:* How many $\frac{1}{5}$-pound servings are in 4 pounds of rice?
9. Write $\frac{7}{20}$ as a decimal.
