# 4.5 — Hamiltonian Mechanics: Canonical Equations

> *"The Hamiltonian formulation is the bridge between classical and quantum mechanics."* — Leonard Susskind

Hamiltonian mechanics recasts dynamics in **phase space** $(q, p)$ using first-order differential equations. The Legendre transform converts the Lagrangian's velocity-based description into a momentum-based one, revealing the symplectic geometry underlying all of mechanics and providing the natural language for quantum mechanics and statistical physics.

---

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 4.5.1 — The Hamiltonian

Given a Lagrangian $L(q,\dot{q},t)$ with conjugate momenta $p_k = \partial L/\partial\dot{q}_k$, the **Hamiltonian** is defined via the Legendre transform:

$$
H(q, p, t) = \sum_{k=1}^n p_k \dot{q}_k - L(q, \dot{q}, t),
$$

where $\dot{q}_k$ is expressed in terms of $(q, p)$ by inverting $p_k = \partial L/\partial\dot{q}_k$.

### Definition 4.5.2 — Phase Space

The **phase space** $\Gamma$ is the $2n$-dimensional space with coordinates $(q_1,\ldots,q_n, p_1,\ldots,p_n)$. Each point represents a complete instantaneous state of the system.

### Definition 4.5.3 — Canonical Coordinates

A set of coordinates $(q_k, p_k)$ is **canonical** if Hamilton's equations hold in their standard form. The fundamental Poisson bracket relations $\{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$ characterize canonical coordinates.

### Definition 4.5.4 — Hamiltonian Flow

The **Hamiltonian flow** is the one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms $\phi_t: \Gamma \to \Gamma$ generated by Hamilton's equations. It maps initial conditions to their time-evolved states.

---

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 4.5.A1 — Hamilton's Equations as Fundamental

The dynamics of a mechanical system are completely determined by the Hamiltonian $H(q,p,t)$ through:

$$
\dot{q}_k = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}, \quad \dot{p}_k = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}, \quad k = 1,\ldots,n.
$$

### Axiom 4.5.A2 — Symplectic Structure

Phase space carries a natural symplectic 2-form $\omega = \sum_k dp_k \wedge dq_k$ that is preserved by Hamiltonian evolution.

---

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 4.5.1 — Time Evolution of the Hamiltonian

$$
\frac{dH}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}.
$$

If $H$ has no explicit time dependence, $H$ is conserved.

**Proof.** By the chain rule: $\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}\dot{q}_k + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}\dot{p}_k\right) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}$. Substitute Hamilton's equations: $= \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}\left(-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}\right)\right) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}$. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 4.5.2 — Hamiltonian Equals Energy (Conditions)

$H = T + U = E$ when: (1) the constraints are scleronomic, (2) $T$ is a homogeneous quadratic in $\dot{q}$, and (3) $U$ is velocity-independent.

---

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 4.5.1 — Liouville's Theorem

The phase-space volume element is preserved under Hamiltonian flow:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)} d^nq\,d^np = 0.
$$

Equivalently, the phase-space "fluid" is incompressible: $\nabla_\Gamma \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial\dot{q}_k}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial\dot{p}_k}{\partial p_k}\right) = 0$.

### Theorem 4.5.2 — Equivalence of Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Formulations

Hamilton's canonical equations are equivalent to the Euler-Lagrange equations via the Legendre transform, provided the Hessian $\det\left(\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial\dot{q}_i\partial\dot{q}_j}\right) \neq 0$.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Derivation of Hamilton's Equations from the Legendre Transform

**Step 1:** Start with $H = \sum_k p_k\dot{q}_k - L$. Take the total differential:

$$
dH = \sum_k(\dot{q}_k\,dp_k + p_k\,d\dot{q}_k) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}dq_k - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_k}d\dot{q}_k - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt.
$$

**Step 2:** Since $p_k = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_k}$, the $d\dot{q}_k$ terms cancel: $p_k\,d\dot{q}_k - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_k}d\dot{q}_k = 0$.

**Step 3:** Remaining:

$$
dH = \sum_k\left(\dot{q}_k\,dp_k - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}dq_k\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt.
$$

**Step 4:** But $dH = \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}dq_k + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}dp_k\right) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}dt$. Comparing coefficients:

$$
\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k} = \dot{q}_k, \quad \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_k} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} = -\dot{p}_k, \quad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.2 Proof of Liouville's Theorem

**Proof.** The divergence of the phase-space velocity field $(\dot{q}_1,\ldots,\dot{q}_n,\dot{p}_1,\ldots,\dot{p}_n)$:

$$
\nabla_\Gamma\cdot\mathbf{v} = \sum_{k=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial\dot{q}_k}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial\dot{p}_k}{\partial p_k}\right) = \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_k\partial p_k} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_k\partial q_k}\right) = 0,
$$

by equality of mixed partial derivatives (Schwarz's theorem). By the continuity equation for the phase-space density $\rho$: $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla_\Gamma\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}) = 0$. Since $\nabla_\Gamma\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, this becomes $\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = 0$ — the density is constant along flow lines. $\blacksquare$

---

### 5.3 Derivation — Hamiltonian for a Charged Particle in EM Field

**Step 1:** The Lagrangian is $L = \frac{1}{2}m|\dot{\mathbf{r}}|^2 + q\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A} - q\phi$.

**Step 2:** Canonical momentum: $p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_i} = m\dot{x}_i + qA_i$. Note: $\mathbf{p} \neq m\mathbf{v}$ (the canonical momentum includes the vector potential contribution).

**Step 3:** Solve for velocity: $\dot{x}_i = (p_i - qA_i)/m$, so $|\dot{\mathbf{r}}|^2 = |\mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A}|^2/m^2$.

**Step 4:** Legendre transform:

$$
H = \mathbf{p}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}} - L = \sum_i p_i\frac{p_i - qA_i}{m} - \frac{1}{2m}|\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A}|^2 - \frac{q}{m}(\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{A} + q\phi.
$$

After careful algebra (expanding and collecting terms):

$$
H = \frac{|\mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A}|^2}{2m} + q\phi.
$$

This is the Hamiltonian for a charged particle. The kinetic momentum is $m\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A}$.

### 5.4 Phase Space Volume — Geometric Interpretation

Consider a small rectangle in 2D phase space with corners at $(q, p)$, $(q+\delta q, p)$, $(q, p+\delta p)$, $(q+\delta q, p+\delta p)$. After time $dt$, each corner evolves via Hamilton's equations.

The new area element: $\delta q' \cdot \delta p' = \delta q\,\delta p\left(1 + \frac{\partial\dot{q}}{\partial q}dt\right)\left(1 + \frac{\partial\dot{p}}{\partial p}dt\right) + O(dt^2)$.

To first order: $\delta q'\delta p' = \delta q\,\delta p\left(1 + \left(\frac{\partial\dot{q}}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial\dot{p}}{\partial p}\right)dt\right) = \delta q\,\delta p$ since the divergence vanishes.

This is Liouville's theorem in action: the Jacobian determinant of the time-evolution map equals 1.

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 4.5.1 — Simple Harmonic Oscillator in Phase Space

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

$L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx^2$. Conjugate momentum: $p = m\dot{x}$, so $\dot{x} = p/m$.

$$
H = p\dot{x} - L = p\cdot\frac{p}{m} - \frac{1}{2}m\frac{p^2}{m^2} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2.
$$

Hamilton's equations: $\dot{x} = \partial H/\partial p = p/m$, $\dot{p} = -\partial H/\partial x = -kx$.

Combine: $\ddot{x} = \dot{p}/m = -kx/m$, recovering $\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$.

**Solution in phase space:** $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi_0)$, $p(t) = -mA\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi_0)$.

Phase trajectories: $H = E = \text{const}$ gives $\frac{p^2}{2mE} + \frac{kx^2}{2E} = 1$ — ellipses in $(x,p)$ space with semi-axes $x_{\max} = \sqrt{2E/k}$ and $p_{\max} = \sqrt{2mE}$.

**Area enclosed:** $\oint p\,dq = 2\pi E/\omega = 2\pi E\sqrt{m/k}$ (this is the action variable $J$).

</details>

### Example 4.5.3 — Hamiltonian for Particle on a Cone

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

A particle of mass $m$ moves on the surface of a cone $z = \alpha r$ (half-angle $\beta = \arctan(1/\alpha)$) under gravity.

**Step 1:** Constraint reduces to 2 DOF. Use $(r, \theta)$ on the cone surface. The position: $\rho = r$, $z = \alpha r$. Speed: $\dot{\rho}^2 + \rho^2\dot\theta^2 + \dot{z}^2 = (1+\alpha^2)\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2$.

**Step 2:** $L = \frac{1}{2}m[(1+\alpha^2)\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2] - mg\alpha r$.

**Step 3:** Momenta: $p_r = m(1+\alpha^2)\dot{r}$, $p_\theta = mr^2\dot\theta$.

**Step 4:** Hamiltonian:

$$
H = \frac{p_r^2}{2m(1+\alpha^2)} + \frac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2} + mg\alpha r.
$$

$\theta$ is cyclic → $p_\theta$ is conserved (angular momentum about the cone axis).

</details>

### Example 4.5.2 — Particle in Central Force (Hamiltonian in Polar Coordinates)

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

$L = \frac{1}{2}\mu(\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) - U(r)$. Momenta: $p_r = \mu\dot{r}$, $p_\theta = \mu r^2\dot\theta = L$ (angular momentum).

$$
H = \frac{p_r^2}{2\mu} + \frac{p_\theta^2}{2\mu r^2} + U(r).
$$

Since $\theta$ is cyclic: $\dot{p}_\theta = -\partial H/\partial\theta = 0$, confirming $p_\theta = L = \text{const}$.

Radial equation: $\dot{p}_r = -\partial H/\partial r = \frac{p_\theta^2}{\mu r^3} - U'(r)$, which is $\mu\ddot{r} = L^2/(\mu r^3) - U'(r)$.

</details>

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Hamiltonian for the Spherical Pendulum

**Problem:** A mass $m$ is constrained to move on the surface of a sphere of radius $\ell$ (a spherical pendulum). Derive the Hamiltonian, identify cyclic coordinates, and reduce the problem to an effective one-dimensional system.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Lagrangian in spherical coordinates

On a sphere of radius $\ell$, the generalized coordinates are $(\theta, \phi)$ where $\theta$ is the polar angle from the downward vertical and $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle.

Position: $x = \ell\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = \ell\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = -\ell\cos\theta$.

Kinetic energy:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}m\ell^2(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2).
$$

Potential energy (measuring from the pivot):

$$
V = -mg\ell\cos\theta.
$$

Lagrangian:

$$
L = \frac{1}{2}m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2 + mg\ell\cos\theta.
$$

#### Step 2: Conjugate momenta

$$
p_\theta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta} = m\ell^2\dot\theta.
$$

$$
p_\phi = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\phi} = m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi.
$$

Note: $p_\phi$ is the $z$-component of angular momentum ($L_z$).

#### Step 3: Invert for velocities

$$
\dot\theta = \frac{p_\theta}{m\ell^2}, \quad \dot\phi = \frac{p_\phi}{m\ell^2\sin^2\theta}.
$$

#### Step 4: Hamiltonian via Legendre transform

$$
H = p_\theta\dot\theta + p_\phi\dot\phi - L = \frac{p_\theta^2}{m\ell^2} + \frac{p_\phi^2}{m\ell^2\sin^2\theta} - \frac{1}{2}m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 - \frac{1}{2}m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2 - mg\ell\cos\theta.
$$

Substituting the expressions for $\dot\theta$ and $\dot\phi$:

$$
H = \frac{p_\theta^2}{2m\ell^2} + \frac{p_\phi^2}{2m\ell^2\sin^2\theta} - mg\ell\cos\theta.
$$

#### Step 5: Identify cyclic coordinate and conservation law

$\phi$ is cyclic (does not appear in $H$), so:

$$
\dot{p}_\phi = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\phi} = 0 \implies p_\phi = L_z = \text{const}.
$$

#### Step 6: Effective one-dimensional problem

With $p_\phi = L_z$ constant, the $\theta$-dynamics is governed by:

$$
H = \frac{p_\theta^2}{2m\ell^2} + V_{\text{eff}}(\theta), \quad \text{where } V_{\text{eff}}(\theta) = \frac{L_z^2}{2m\ell^2\sin^2\theta} - mg\ell\cos\theta.
$$

The first term is a "centrifugal barrier" that prevents the pendulum from reaching the poles ($\theta = 0$ or $\pi$) when $L_z \neq 0$. The motion in $\theta$ oscillates between turning points where $E = V_{\text{eff}}(\theta)$.

#### Step 7: Hamilton's equations

$$
\dot\theta = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_\theta} = \frac{p_\theta}{m\ell^2}.
$$

$$
\dot{p}_\theta = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta} = \frac{L_z^2\cos\theta}{m\ell^2\sin^3\theta} - mg\ell\sin\theta.
$$

$$
\dot\phi = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_\phi} = \frac{L_z}{m\ell^2\sin^2\theta}, \quad \dot{p}_\phi = 0.
$$

**Final Answer:**

$$
H = \frac{p_\theta^2}{2m\ell^2} + \frac{p_\phi^2}{2m\ell^2\sin^2\theta} - mg\ell\cos\theta
$$

with $p_\phi = L_z$ conserved and effective potential $V_{\text{eff}}(\theta) = \frac{L_z^2}{2m\ell^2\sin^2\theta} - mg\ell\cos\theta$.

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Charged Particle in Electromagnetic Field

**Problem:** Derive the Hamiltonian for a particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ in an electromagnetic field described by scalar potential $\phi$ and vector potential $\mathbf{A}$. Show that Hamilton's equations reproduce the Lorentz force law.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: The Lagrangian for a charged particle

From [4.3 - Lagrangian Mechanics - Euler-Lagrange](4.3---Lagrangian-Mechanics---Euler-Lagrange), the Lagrangian is:

$$
L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 + q\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A} - q\phi.
$$

In component form ($i = x, y, z$):

$$
L = \frac{1}{2}m\sum_i\dot{x}_i^2 + q\sum_i\dot{x}_i A_i - q\phi.
$$

#### Step 2: Canonical momentum

$$
p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_i} = m\dot{x}_i + qA_i.
$$

This is the **canonical momentum**, which differs from the mechanical (kinetic) momentum $m\dot{x}_i$ by the term $qA_i$. In vector form:

$$
\mathbf{p} = m\dot{\mathbf{r}} + q\mathbf{A} \implies \dot{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{\mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A}}{m}.
$$

#### Step 3: Hamiltonian via Legendre transform

$$
H = \mathbf{p}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}} - L = \sum_i p_i\dot{x}_i - L.
$$

Substitute $\dot{x}_i = (p_i - qA_i)/m$:

$$
H = \sum_i p_i\cdot\frac{p_i - qA_i}{m} - \frac{1}{2m}\sum_i(p_i-qA_i)^2 - q\sum_i\frac{p_i-qA_i}{m}A_i + q\phi.
$$

Wait — let's be more systematic. $\mathbf{p}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \sum_i p_i(p_i-qA_i)/m = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i(p_i^2 - p_i qA_i)$.

$L = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_i(p_i-qA_i)^2 + q\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A} - q\phi = \frac{|\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A}|^2}{2m} + \frac{q}{m}(\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{A} - q\phi$.

Therefore:

$$
H = \frac{1}{m}(\mathbf{p}^2 - q\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{A}) - \frac{|\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A}|^2}{2m} - \frac{q}{m}(\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{A} + q\phi.
$$

This simplifies. More directly: since $T = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 = \frac{|\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A}|^2}{2m}$:

$$
H = T + q\phi = \frac{(\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A})^2}{2m} + q\phi.
$$

This can be verified: $H = \mathbf{p}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}} - L = (m\dot{\mathbf{r}}+q\mathbf{A})\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}} - \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 - q\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A} + q\phi = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 + q\phi$. ✓

#### Step 4: Hamilton's equations

$$
\dot{x}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = \frac{p_i - qA_i}{m}.
$$

$$
\dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_i} = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_j(p_j-qA_j)\left(-q\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x_i}\right) - q\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i} = \frac{q}{m}\sum_j(p_j-qA_j)\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x_i} - q\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}.
$$

Since $m\dot{x}_j = p_j - qA_j$:

$$
\dot{p}_i = q\sum_j\dot{x}_j\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x_i} - q\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}.
$$

#### Step 5: Recover the Lorentz force

The mechanical force is $F_i = m\ddot{x}_i = \dot{p}_i - q\dot{A}_i$ (since $p_i = m\dot{x}_i + qA_i$).

$$
\dot{A}_i = \frac{\partial A_i}{\partial t} + \sum_j\dot{x}_j\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial x_j}.
$$

$$
F_i = q\sum_j\dot{x}_j\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x_i} - q\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i} - q\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial t} - q\sum_j\dot{x}_j\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial x_j}.
$$

$$
= q\left(-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial A_i}{\partial t}\right) + q\sum_j\dot{x}_j\left(\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial A_i}{\partial x_j}\right).
$$

The first parenthesis is $E_i$ (electric field). The second is $(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})_i$ since $B_k = \varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_i A_j$. Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}).
$$

This is the **Lorentz force**. ✓

**Final Answer:**

$$
H = \frac{(\mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A})^2}{2m} + q\phi
$$

</details>



### Example 8.3 — Action-Angle Variables for the Simple Harmonic Oscillator

**Problem:** For a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2$, compute the action variable $J$, express $H$ in terms of $J$, and find the angle variable $\theta$ and its time evolution.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Define the action variable

The action variable is defined as the phase-space area enclosed by the orbit divided by $2\pi$:

$$
J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dq,
$$

where the integral is over one complete period of the motion.

#### Step 2: Express $p$ in terms of $q$ and $E$

From $H = E$: $p = \pm\sqrt{2m\left(E - \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2\right)} = \pm\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2 q^2}$.

The orbit in phase space is an ellipse: $\frac{p^2}{2mE} + \frac{q^2}{2E/(m\omega^2)} = 1$.

Semi-axes: $p_{\max} = \sqrt{2mE}$, $q_{\max} = \sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}$.

#### Step 3: Compute the action integral

The area of an ellipse with semi-axes $a, b$ is $\pi ab$. Therefore:

$$
\oint p\,dq = \pi\cdot p_{\max}\cdot q_{\max} = \pi\sqrt{2mE}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}} = \pi\cdot\frac{2E}{\omega}.
$$

$$
J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi E}{\omega} = \frac{E}{\omega}.
$$

#### Step 4: Hamiltonian in action-angle form

Inverting: $E = J\omega$, so:

$$
H(J) = \omega J.
$$

This is remarkably simple — the Hamiltonian is linear in $J$.

#### Step 5: Hamilton's equations in action-angle variables

$$
\dot\theta = \frac{\partial H}{\partial J} = \omega, \quad \dot{J} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta} = 0.
$$

Therefore $J = \text{const}$ (as expected — it's the action) and $\theta = \omega t + \theta_0$ (the angle increases uniformly at the oscillation frequency).

#### Step 6: Physical interpretation

The action variable $J$ is proportional to the energy: $J = E/\omega$. In quantum mechanics, the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rule $J = n\hbar$ gives $E_n = n\hbar\omega$ — the energy levels of the quantum harmonic oscillator (up to the zero-point energy $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$).

The angle variable $\theta$ is the phase of the oscillation. The transformation $(q, p) \to (\theta, J)$ is canonical and maps the elliptical phase-space orbits to horizontal lines $J = \text{const}$ in the $(\theta, J)$ plane — the simplest possible dynamics.

#### Step 7: Explicit transformation formulas

From $q = q_{\max}\sin\theta = \sqrt{2J/(m\omega)}\sin\theta$ and $p = p_{\max}\cos\theta = \sqrt{2mJ\omega}\cos\theta$:

$$
q = \sqrt{\frac{2J}{m\omega}}\sin\theta, \quad p = \sqrt{2mJ\omega}\cos\theta.
$$

Verify: $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2 = J\omega\cos^2\theta + J\omega\sin^2\theta = J\omega$. ✓

**Final Answer:**

$$
J = \frac{E}{\omega}, \quad H(J) = \omega J, \quad \theta(t) = \omega t + \theta_0
$$

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 Derivation of Hamilton's Canonical Equations from the Legendre Transform

The Hamiltonian formulation arises from a Legendre transform of the Lagrangian. Here we derive Hamilton's equations from scratch, showing why the Legendre transform is the natural mathematical operation.

**Step 1: The Lagrangian and its differential.** The Lagrangian $L(q_i, \dot{q}_i, t)$ has the total differential:

$$
dL = \sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}dq_i + \sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}d\dot{q}_i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt.
$$

Using the Euler-Lagrange equation $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \dot{p}_i$ and the definition $p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}$:

$$
dL = \sum_i\dot{p}_i\,dq_i + \sum_i p_i\,d\dot{q}_i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt.
$$

**Step 2: Motivation for the Legendre transform.** We want a function of $(q_i, p_i, t)$ instead of $(q_i, \dot{q}_i, t)$ — replacing the velocities $\dot{q}_i$ with the momenta $p_i$ as independent variables. The Legendre transform achieves this.

**Step 3: Define the Hamiltonian.**

$$
H(q_i, p_i, t) = \sum_i p_i\dot{q}_i - L(q_i, \dot{q}_i, t),
$$

where $\dot{q}_i$ on the right is understood as a function of $(q, p, t)$ obtained by inverting $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot{q}_i$.

**Step 4: Compute $dH$.**

$$
dH = \sum_i\dot{q}_i\,dp_i + \sum_i p_i\,d\dot{q}_i - dL.
$$

Substitute the expression for $dL$:

$$
dH = \sum_i\dot{q}_i\,dp_i + \sum_i p_i\,d\dot{q}_i - \sum_i\dot{p}_i\,dq_i - \sum_i p_i\,d\dot{q}_i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt.
$$

The $p_i\,d\dot{q}_i$ terms cancel:

$$
dH = \sum_i\dot{q}_i\,dp_i - \sum_i\dot{p}_i\,dq_i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt.
$$

**Step 5: Read off Hamilton's equations.** Since $H = H(q_i, p_i, t)$, we also have:

$$
dH = \sum_i\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}dq_i + \sum_i\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}dp_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}dt.
$$

Comparing coefficients of $dp_i$, $dq_i$, and $dt$:

$$
\dot{q}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \quad \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}, \quad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.
$$

The first two are **Hamilton's canonical equations**. The third shows that if $L$ has no explicit time dependence, neither does $H$, and $H$ is conserved (it equals the total energy for natural systems).

**Step 6: Symplectic structure.** Hamilton's equations can be written compactly as:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbb{J}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} H, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{z} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}\\\mathbf{p}\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbb{J} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The matrix $\mathbb{J}$ is the **symplectic matrix**, and this structure is the foundation of all of Hamiltonian mechanics — see [4.6 - Poisson Brackets & Canonical Transformations](4.6---Poisson-Brackets-&-Canonical-Transformations).

*References: Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, Ch. 8; Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §3; Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §40.*

### 9.2 When Does $H = E$? The Distinction Between Hamiltonian and Energy

A common source of confusion: the Hamiltonian $H$ is not always equal to the total energy $E = T + V$. Here we clarify precisely when they coincide.

**Theorem:** $H = E$ if and only if:
1. The kinetic energy $T$ is a homogeneous quadratic function of the generalized velocities (i.e., the transformation from Cartesian to generalized coordinates does not depend explicitly on time), AND
2. The potential $V$ does not depend on the velocities $\dot{q}_i$.

**Proof of the general relation:** By definition, $H = \sum_i p_i\dot{q}_i - L$. For a natural system, $T = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}M_{ij}(q)\dot{q}_i\dot{q}_j$ (homogeneous quadratic in $\dot{q}$). By Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions:

$$
\sum_i\dot{q}_i\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{q}_i} = 2T.
$$

If $V$ is independent of $\dot{q}_i$, then $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot{q}_i = \partial T/\partial\dot{q}_i$, so:

$$
H = \sum_i p_i\dot{q}_i - L = 2T - (T-V) = T + V = E.
$$

**When $H \neq E$:** Consider a bead on a rotating wire (Example 8.2 of [4.3 - Lagrangian Mechanics - Euler-Lagrange](4.3---Lagrangian-Mechanics---Euler-Lagrange)). The transformation to the rotating frame introduces explicit time dependence: $x = q\sin\alpha\cos(\omega t)$, etc. The kinetic energy acquires linear terms in $\dot{q}$:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2\sin^2\alpha.
$$

Here $T$ is not purely quadratic in $\dot{q}$ when viewed from the lab frame (the $\omega^2 q^2$ term comes from the time-dependent transformation). The Hamiltonian $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2\sin^2\alpha + mgq\cos\alpha$ is conserved but is NOT equal to $T + V$ — it's the **Jacobi integral** (energy in the rotating frame).

**Another example:** For a charged particle in an EM field, $H = \frac{(\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A})^2}{2m} + q\phi = T + q\phi = E$. Here $H = E$ because the velocity-dependent term $q\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A}$ in $L$ does not contribute to the energy (the magnetic force does no work).

*References: Goldstein, §8.1–8.2; Susskind, The Theoretical Minimum, Lecture 8; MIT 8.223 (Classical Mechanics II).*

### 9.3 Phase Space, Canonical Coordinates, and the Symplectic Structure

The Hamiltonian formulation elevates phase space from a convenient visualization tool to the fundamental arena of mechanics. Here we develop the geometric perspective.

**Phase space:** For a system with $n$ degrees of freedom, the phase space is the $2n$-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M}$ with coordinates $\mathbf{z} = (q_1, \ldots, q_n, p_1, \ldots, p_n)$.

**The symplectic 2-form:** The fundamental geometric object on phase space is the **symplectic form**:

$$
\omega = \sum_{i=1}^n dp_i \wedge dq_i.
$$

This is a closed, non-degenerate 2-form. It defines the "geometry" of phase space in the same way that the metric tensor defines the geometry of spacetime in general relativity.

**Hamilton's equations in geometric form:** Given a Hamiltonian $H$, the equations of motion are:

$$
\iota_{\mathbf{X}_H}\omega = dH,
$$

where $\mathbf{X}_H$ is the Hamiltonian vector field and $\iota$ denotes interior product. In coordinates, this reproduces $\dot{q}_i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot{p}_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$.

**Canonical transformations preserve $\omega$:** A transformation $(q, p) \to (Q, P)$ is canonical if and only if it preserves the symplectic form:

$$
\sum_i dP_i \wedge dQ_i = \sum_i dp_i \wedge dq_i.
$$

This is equivalent to the matrix condition $M^T\mathbb{J}M = \mathbb{J}$ (see Example 8.3 of [4.6 - Poisson Brackets & Canonical Transformations](4.6---Poisson-Brackets-&-Canonical-Transformations)).

**Darboux's theorem:** Any symplectic manifold locally looks like $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with the standard symplectic form. This means there always exist canonical coordinates — the symplectic structure has no local invariants (unlike Riemannian geometry, which has curvature).

**Physical consequences:**
1. **Volume preservation** (Liouville): $\omega^n = \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega$ ($n$ times) is the phase-space volume form, preserved by Hamiltonian flow.
2. **Poisson brackets** are defined by $\omega$: $\{f, g\} = \omega(\mathbf{X}_f, \mathbf{X}_g)$.
3. **Quantization:** The symplectic form determines the commutation relations $[\hat{q}_i, \hat{p}_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ — the passage from classical to quantum mechanics is fundamentally a deformation of the symplectic structure.

*References: Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §8; Abraham & Marsden, Foundations of Mechanics; Goldstein, §9.5.*
