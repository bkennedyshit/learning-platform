# 19.5 - Gilded Age & Progressivism

Mark Twain called the late 1800s the "Gilded Age": a thin layer of gold over serious problems. Between 1877 and 1920 the United States became a leading industrial power. Railroads crossed the continent, millions of immigrants arrived, and cities grew rapidly. Working conditions were often dangerous, a few companies dominated whole industries, and Southern states built a legal system of racial segregation. Reformers called **Progressives** used journalism, new laws, and constitutional amendments to respond. By 1920 the United States had also acquired overseas territories and fought in a world war.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). Its main skill is **continuity and change**: deciding what changed, what stayed the same, and for whom.

## Core ideas

### Big business and its regulation

A **trust** was a way to combine competing companies under one control. When the **Standard Oil Trust** was formed on January 2, 1882, stockholders handed their shares to nine trustees and received trust certificates; "the nine trustees ran all the component companies," which "allowed Standard Oil to function as a monopoly" (National Archives).

The federal government responded slowly:

| Year | Law or case | What it did | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1887 | **Interstate Commerce Act** | Created the Interstate Commerce Commission; the railroads became "the first industry subject to Federal regulation" | Early enforcement was weak |
| 1890 | **Sherman Anti-Trust Act** | The "first Federal act that outlawed monopolistic business practices"; passed the Senate 51–1 and the House 242–0 | Based on Congress's power to regulate interstate commerce |
| 1895 | *United States v. E. C. Knight* | The Supreme Court held that a company controlling "about 98% of all sugar refining" had not violated the law, because control of manufacturing was not control of trade | "Seemed to end any government regulation of trusts" |
| 1904 | *Northern Securities Co. v. United States* | The Court upheld Theodore Roosevelt's suit to break up a railroad holding company | Start of "trust-busting" success |
| 1911 | Standard Oil and American Tobacco cases | The Taft administration used the Sherman Act against both companies | Large trusts broken up |

### Workers and immigrants

- **Labor organizing.** After the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, workers joined unions such as the **Knights of Labor**, which welcomed skilled and unskilled workers. A national strike for an eight-hour day began on May 1, 1886. At a protest in Chicago's **Haymarket Square**, a bomb killed seven police officers, and police fire killed four people. Eight anarchists were convicted of conspiracy "despite no direct evidence implicating them in the bombing" (*American Yawp*). Many Americans began to associate unions with radicalism, and the more cautious **American Federation of Labor** grew instead.
- **Immigration.** Ellis Island opened on January 1, 1892, and "over 12 million immigrants were processed at Ellis Island during the peak years of 1892-1924" (National Park Service). Many came from southern and eastern Europe.
- **Exclusion.** The **Chinese Exclusion Act** (May 6, 1882) was "the first significant law restricting immigration into the United States." It banned Chinese laborers for ten years; Congress extended it in 1892, made it permanent in 1902, and did not repeal the exclusion acts until 1943 (National Archives).
- **Workplace danger.** On March 25, 1911, fire broke out at the Triangle Waist Company in New York; 146 people died, most of them young immigrant women. New York's Factory Investigating Commission, created afterward, led to new safety laws; Frances Perkins, who worked on its investigations, later became U.S. Secretary of Labor (Department of Labor).

### Jim Crow

After 1877, Southern states passed laws that segregated public life ("Jim Crow" laws) and kept most Black men from voting. The National Archives traces the steps: the Supreme Court struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875 in 1883, ruling that the Fourteenth Amendment did not let Congress prevent discrimination by private individuals; beginning with an 1887 Florida law, states required separate railroad cars. In ***Plessy v. Ferguson*** (1896), the Court upheld Louisiana's law requiring "equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races" by a vote of 7–1. Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented. The ruling stood until *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954).

### Progressive reform

Progressives believed experts, journalists, and government could fix problems caused by industry and cities.

| Reform | Date | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Food and Drugs Act and Meat Inspection Act | 1906 | Upton Sinclair's novel *The Jungle* exposed meatpacking conditions and was "the final precipitating force behind both a meat inspection law and a comprehensive food and drug law" (FDA) |
| Keating-Owen Child Labor Act | 1916 | Banned interstate sale of goods from factories employing children under 14 or mines employing children under 16; struck down in *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918) |
| **Sixteenth Amendment** | Ratified February 3, 1913 | Federal income tax |
| **Seventeenth Amendment** | Ratified April 8, 1913 | Direct election of senators by voters instead of state legislatures |
| **Eighteenth Amendment** | Ratified January 16, 1919 | Prohibition of alcohol (repealed by the 21st Amendment in 1933) |
| **Nineteenth Amendment** | Ratified August 18, 1920 | The right to vote "shall not be denied or abridged ... on account of sex" |

### Empire and World War I

- **1898.** After the battleship *Maine* exploded in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, the United States went to war with Spain. The peace treaty made Spain give up its claim to Cuba and cede Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines to the United States, and the United States annexed Hawaii during the war (Office of the Historian). The United States then fought a war against Filipino independence forces, the Philippine-American War (1899–1902).
- **Panama Canal (1903–1914).** President Theodore Roosevelt "oversaw the realization of a long-term United States goal—a trans-isthmian canal."
- **World War I.** The United States stayed neutral from 1914, and Woodrow Wilson was reelected in 1916 with the slogan "He kept us out of war." In 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, and the **Zimmermann Telegram** (Example 3) became public on March 1. Wilson asked Congress for war on April 2, 1917, saying "The world must be made safe for democracy." After the war, Wilson's **Fourteen Points** (January 8, 1918) proposed a League of Nations, but the Senate defeated the Treaty of Versailles 49–35 in March 1920 (falling short of the two-thirds needed), and the United States never joined the League.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Why the Sherman Act changed little at first

1. **Text:** the act declared illegal any combination "in restraint of trade or commerce among the several states, or with foreign nations," relying on Congress's commerce power.
2. **Interpretation:** in *E. C. Knight* (1895), the Court said manufacturing was not commerce, so a sugar trust with 98 percent of refining was legal.
3. **Change:** after 1901, presidents chose to bring suits, and the Court upheld breaking up Northern Securities (1904) and Standard Oil (1911).
4. **Reasoning:** a law's effect depends on how courts interpret it and whether officials enforce it. The same text produced weak results in the 1890s and strong ones in the 1900s.

### Example 2: *Plessy* was a planned test case

The National Archives explains that in 1891 Black residents of New Orleans formed the "Citizens' Committee to Test the Constitutionality of the Separate Car Law." With the railroad's cooperation, on June 7, 1892, Homer Plessy sat in a white compartment and was arrested.

1. **What this shows:** Black citizens organized to challenge segregation in court; they were not passive.
2. **The majority's reasoning (Justice Henry Brown):** "We consider the underlying fallacy of the plaintiff's argument to consist in the assumption that the enforced separation of the two races stamps the colored race with a badge of inferiority."
3. **Evaluate it:** the majority claimed the law treated races equally. Evidence of how segregated facilities were actually funded and maintained, and the fact that Black Louisianans protested the law in 1890, shows the "equal" claim did not match reality.
4. **Continuity and change:** the Fourteenth Amendment's text had not changed since 1868; what changed was the Court's reading of it, and that reading lasted until 1954.

### Example 3: Reading the Zimmermann Telegram

The decoded text says Germany would "begin on the first of February unrestricted submarine warfare" and proposes that if the United States entered the war, Germany and Mexico "make war together, make peace together," with "an understanding on our part that Mexico is to reconquer the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona."

1. **Source:** a coded German diplomatic message, intercepted and decoded by British cryptographers in January 1917.
2. **Why Britain waited:** the National Archives notes the British held it "to protect their intelligence from detection and to capitalize on growing anti-German sentiment," giving it to Wilson on February 24.
3. **Effect:** published widely on March 1, it "inflamed American public opinion against Germany."
4. **Weigh causes of entry:** submarine warfare (direct attacks on American ships), economic ties (U.S. munitions shipments to the Allies grew from about 6 million dollars in 1914 to almost 500 million in 1917), and the telegram (a direct threat). The telegram changed public opinion; submarine warfare was the immediate trigger.

### Example 4: Continuity and change for Black Americans, 1870–1920

| Area | 1870 | 1920 | Change or continuity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voting rights in the Constitution | 15th Amendment ratified | Still in the Constitution; women added by the 19th | Change on paper |
| Voting in practice in the South | Black men voting and holding office | Most Black Southerners blocked from voting by state laws and violence | Change for the worse |
| Legal segregation | Being challenged under Reconstruction laws | Upheld by *Plessy*; required by Jim Crow laws | Change for the worse |
| Organizing for rights | Black political activism | Legal challenges and organizations continued | Continuity |

**Claim:** between 1870 and 1920 the constitutional promises stayed the same, but their enforcement collapsed in the South, while Black Americans continued to organize against segregation.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Business leaders were simply 'robber barons' (or simply heroes)."** Both labels oversimplify. Figures such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller built efficient companies and gave large sums to charity, while using practices (such as trusts and union-breaking) that critics and courts challenged. Judge specific actions with evidence.
- **"The Sherman Act ended monopolies in 1890."** Early court rulings such as *E. C. Knight* limited it; major enforcement came after 1901.
- **"Every immigrant came through Ellis Island."** Most immigrants entered through the Port of New York, but there were other ports of entry, including Boston, Philadelphia, Baltimore, San Francisco, and New Orleans (Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation). Chinese immigrants arriving on the West Coast also faced the exclusion laws described above.
- **"Segregation was just custom."** It was required by state laws and upheld by the Supreme Court.
- **"Progressives were all on the same side."** Progressives disagreed on many issues, and some Progressive Era measures, such as literacy tests and segregation in federal offices under Wilson, restricted rights.
- **"The 19th Amendment gave all women the vote in 1920."** It barred denial "on account of sex," but many Black women in the South were still blocked by the same tactics used against Black men, and many Native American and Asian American women faced other barriers.
- **Boundary: "first."** The National Archives calls the Chinese Exclusion Act the first *significant* law restricting immigration; earlier, narrower laws existed. Keep qualifiers like "significant" when you quote.

> **Note on sources and viewpoints:** This lesson uses National Archives milestone documents (with their transcripts), the State Department's Office of the Historian, the National Park Service, the FDA, the Department of Labor, Oyez, and *The American Yawp*. Historians disagree about how to judge Gilded Age industrialists and the Progressive movement, so the lesson presents evidence on both sides rather than a verdict.
