# APP-01.3 — Fluency & Word Recognition

A **fluent** reader reads aloud accurately, at a comfortable speed, and with expression, so it sounds like talking. Fluency matters because a reader who has to stop and work out every word has little attention left over to think about what the words mean. By the end of a sentence, a slow, choppy reader may have forgotten how it began.

Fluency grows from two things: knowing more spelling patterns, so more words can be read quickly, and practicing reading real text aloud with feedback. This lesson does both. It adds new vowel patterns to the code from the last lesson, then shows how to practice and measure smooth reading.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 1-2. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

[APP-01.2 — Phonics — Blending & Decoding](/app-012-phonics-blending-decoding): the learner should read short-vowel words, blends, digraphs (*sh*, *ch*, *th*, *ck*), and silent-*e* words such as *cake* and *hope*. WCPM uses subtraction, as in [APP-02.2 — Addition & Subtraction](/app-022-addition-subtraction). The accuracy percent uses division and percents, as in [13.3 - Fractions, Decimals & Percent](/133-fractions-decimals-percent); an adult can do that part for younger readers.

> **Adult note:** You will need a short passage the child has not practiced (about 100–150 words at the child's level), a copy for you to mark, and a timer. Several passages from the child's school or library books work well.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Read words with the vowel teams *ai*, *ay*, *ee*, *ea*, *oa*, and *ue*, and try a second sound when a team has more than one.
2. Read words with *ar*, *or*, *er*, *ir*, *ur*, and with *oi*/*oy* and *ou*/*ow*.
3. Read a short passage aloud accurately, at a steady pace, with expression that follows the punctuation.
4. Work out words correct per minute and accuracy from a one-minute reading.

## Core ideas

### Vowel teams

A **vowel team** is two letters that work together to spell one vowel sound. Many make a long vowel sound:

| Team | Sound | Examples | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| ai, ay | long *a* | *rain*, *paint*; *day*, *play* | *ay* usually comes at the end of a word |
| ee | long *e* | *feet*, *green* | |
| ea | long *e* | *seat*, *team* | also short *e* in *head*, *bread* |
| oa | long *o* | *boat*, *road* | |
| ue | /oo/ or /yoo/ | *blue*, *glue*; *cue* | |

### R-controlled vowels

When a vowel is followed by *r*, the *r* changes the vowel's sound:

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| ar | *car*, *star*, *farm* |
| or | *for*, *corn*, *horse* |
| er, ir, ur | *her*, *bird*, *turn* (in most U.S. accents, these three spell the same sound) |

Because *er*, *ir*, and *ur* often sound the same, you have to remember which one a word uses when you spell it.

### Vowel sounds that glide: oi/oy and ou/ow

In some vowel sounds your mouth moves while you say them. Say *boy* slowly and feel your mouth change shape.

| Pattern | Examples | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| oi, oy | *coin*, *soil*; *boy*, *toy* | *oy* usually comes at the end of a word |
| ou, ow | *out*, *cloud*; *cow*, *down* | *ow* is long *o* in *snow*, *grow*; *ou* has other sounds in *soup* and *touch* |

### The three parts of fluency

1. **Accuracy:** reading the words correctly.
2. **Rate:** reading at a steady, comfortable speed, not slow and choppy and not racing.
3. **Expression (prosody):** reading in phrases, pausing at commas and periods, raising your voice for a question, and showing feeling for an exclamation.

### Words correct per minute (WCPM)

Teachers often check fluency with a one-minute oral reading. The learner reads aloud from a passage they have **not practiced** for one minute. The listener marks errors: a word read wrong, skipped, read out of order, or supplied by the listener after the reader waits about 3 seconds. Then:

- **WCPM** = words read in one minute − errors
- **Accuracy** = words correct ÷ words read, as a percent

> **Adult note:** Hasbrouck and Tindal's 2017 compiled norms report these 50th-percentile scores. Grade 1: 29 WCPM in winter and 60 in spring (no fall score is reported). Grade 2: 50 in fall, 84 in winter, and 100 in spring. These norms are meant to help educators screen for students who may need more support and to monitor progress. They are not a target to race toward and they cannot diagnose a reading problem. A child well below these numbers should be discussed with their teacher, who can use the school's own assessments.

### How fluency is practiced

The National Reading Panel found that **guided repeated oral reading**, where a child reads a passage aloud several times with feedback from a teacher, parent, or peer, improved word recognition, fluency, and comprehension. It could not find enough strong evidence that independent silent reading **on its own** improves fluency. That does not mean free reading is bad. It means reading aloud with feedback should be part of the practice.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Choosing the sound for *ea*

Sentence: *I ate my bread at the beach.*

1. *bread*: *b-r* is a blend, then *ea*, then *d*. Try long *e* first: /b/ /r/ /ē/ /d/, "breed." That is a real word, but it does not make sense here.
2. Try the other *ea* sound, short *e*: /b/ /r/ /e/ /d/ → *bread*. That makes sense with "I ate my."
3. *beach*: *b*, then *ea*, then the digraph *ch*. Long *e*: /b/ /ē/ /ch/ → *beach*. That makes sense.

Reasoning: when a team has more than one sound, try the most common sound first, then the other one, and keep the one that makes a real word that fits the sentence.

### Example 2: Calculating WCPM

*Illustrative numbers, not a real student.* A second grader reads an unpracticed passage aloud for one minute. When the timer stops, the reader has reached word 68. The listener marked 5 errors.

1. Words read: 68.
2. Errors: 5.
3. WCPM = 68 − 5 = **63**.
4. Accuracy = 63 ÷ 68 = 0.926…, or about **93%**.

Interpretation: about 93 out of every 100 words were correct. The next step is to look at the 5 errors. If most were *ou*/*ow* words, practice that pattern. The WCPM number alone does not tell you *why* the reader made errors.

### Example 3: Reading with expression

Passage: *"Look out!" yelled Max. Was the dog going to jump in the mud? It was. Splash!*

1. *"Look out!"* ends with an exclamation point inside quotation marks. Max is warning someone, so say it loudly and quickly.
2. *yelled Max.* Drop your voice and pause briefly at the period.
3. *Was the dog going to jump in the mud?* A question. Let your voice rise at the end.
4. *It was.* Short and flat, as if the answer was obvious.
5. *Splash!* An exclamation. Say it with feeling.

Reasoning: punctuation tells you how the words should sound. Reading every sentence in the same flat voice is accurate but not fluent.

### Example 4: A repeated-reading routine

*Activity to try* (about 10 minutes):

1. The adult reads a short paragraph aloud as a model, with good phrasing.
2. The learner reads the same paragraph aloud. The adult helps with any hard word and points out one place to improve, such as "pause at the comma."
3. The learner reads it a second and third time.
4. Together, notice what changed: fewer stops, smoother phrases, better expression.

This is a practice routine, not a test. For a WCPM check, always use a passage the learner has **not** practiced.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Fluency means reading as fast as possible."** Racing causes skipped words and flat expression. A fluent reader is accurate, steady, and expressive.
- **"A WCPM score tells you everything about a reader."** It is a quick screening number. It does not measure understanding, and it does not explain the errors. Asking a few questions after reading checks comprehension.
- **"*ow* always says /ow/ as in *cow*."** It also spells long *o* in *snow*, *grow*, and *show*. Try both and keep the one that makes sense.
- **"*er*, *ir*, and *ur* are three different sounds."** In most U.S. accents they sound the same in *her*, *bird*, and *turn*. They are different only in spelling.
- **"Practicing the same passage, then timing it, shows real progress."** Practiced passages always get faster. For an honest check, time a new passage of similar difficulty.
- **"Just letting kids read silently will make them fluent."** The National Reading Panel did not find enough evidence that silent reading alone builds fluency. Reading aloud with feedback has stronger evidence.

## Practice

1. Sort these words by their vowel team: *rain*, *feet*, *boat*, *play*, *seat*, *road*, *tree*, *paint*.
2. Read each word and say whether *ea* is long *e* or short *e*: *team*, *head*, *meat*, *bread*.
3. Read: *star*, *corn*, *bird*, *hurt*, *fern*. Which three have the same vowel sound?
4. Read: *coin*, *toy*, *cloud*, *cow*, *snow*. In which word does *ow* say long *o*?
5. Read this aloud with expression: *"Is it raining?" asked Kim. "Yes! Grab your coat!"* Where should your voice rise?
6. *Illustrative numbers.* A reader reaches word 82 in one minute with 4 errors. What is the WCPM? What is the accuracy, to the nearest whole percent?
7. Two readers both score 60 WCPM. One had 2 errors and the other had 15. Who read more accurately, and how many words did each one read?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. *ai*: *rain*, *paint*. *ay*: *play*. *ee*: *feet*, *tree*. *ea*: *seat*. *oa*: *boat*, *road*.
2. *team*: long. *head*: short. *meat*: long. *bread*: short.
3. *bird*, *hurt*, *fern* (*ir*, *ur*, *er*) have the same vowel sound in most U.S. accents.
4. *snow*.
5. At the end of the question *"Is it raining?"* The second part has exclamation points, so read it with excitement.
6. WCPM = 82 − 4 = 78. Accuracy = 78 ÷ 82 = 0.951…, about 95%.
7. The reader with 2 errors read 62 words (60 + 2). The reader with 15 errors read 75 words (60 + 15). Accuracy: 60 ÷ 62 ≈ 97% versus 60 ÷ 75 = 80%. The first reader was more accurate. The second reader read more words but made many more errors, which shows why WCPM alone is not enough.

**Self-check for learners:** After reading a passage aloud, ask yourself: "Did I read the words right? Did it sound like talking? Can I tell someone what happened?" If the answer to the last question is no, reread it more slowly.

</details>

> **Adult note:** Keep timed readings low-pressure: no more than one timed check per session, and never in front of other children. Celebrate fewer errors and better phrasing, not only a higher number.
