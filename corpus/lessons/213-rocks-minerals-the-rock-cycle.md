# 21.3 - Rocks, Minerals & the Rock Cycle

Every rock tells a story about how it formed. A granite countertop crystallized slowly from molten rock deep underground; a sandstone cliff was once loose sand; a marble statue was once limestone that was heated and squeezed. This lesson explains how geologists identify **minerals**, the building materials, and classify **rocks**, the materials built from them, and then connects all rock types through the **rock cycle**.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. List the characteristics of a mineral and explain how minerals and rocks differ.
2. Identify a mineral from a set of tested properties and explain why color is a weak clue.
3. Classify a rock into one of the three families and a subtype, using evidence.
4. Trace a path through the rock cycle and tell weathering apart from erosion.

## Core ideas

### What is a mineral?

The National Park Service (NPS) lists five characteristics of every mineral. It must:

1. be **naturally occurring** (steel is not a mineral);
2. be **inorganic**;
3. be **solid** at room temperature (usually);
4. have an **ordered internal structure**, with atoms in a repeating pattern that generally forms crystals;
5. have a **definite chemical composition**, which may vary only within set limits.

Quartz, for example, is silicon dioxide (SiO₂); calcite is calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). A **rock** is a natural solid made of one or more minerals (or, for a few rocks such as coal and obsidian, of organic material or natural glass). Granite is a rock made mostly of quartz and feldspar.

### Identifying minerals

| Property | What you test | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Color** | What you see | Often unreliable: trace elements change it. Quartz can be purple, pink, gray, yellow, or milky; hematite can be dull red or shiny black. |
| **Streak** | Color of the powder left on an unglazed porcelain plate | More reliable than color. Hematite leaves a reddish-brown streak whether it looks metallic or earthy. |
| **Luster** | How light reflects from the surface | **Metallic** or non-metallic (glassy, earthy, pearly, silky, resinous). |
| **Hardness** | Resistance to scratching | Compared on the **Mohs scale** from 1 (softest) to 10 (hardest). |
| **Cleavage / fracture** | How it breaks | **Cleavage** is breaking along flat planes of weakness: mica peels into sheets (one direction), halite breaks into cubes (three directions). **Fracture** is an irregular break. |
| **Density** | Mass per unit volume | Most common minerals (quartz, feldspar, calcite, mica) are 2.6–3.0 g/cm³; metallic minerals such as pyrite, hematite, and magnetite are over 5 g/cm³. |
| **Special properties** | Unique tests | Calcite fizzes (gives off carbon dioxide) in dilute acid; magnetite attracts a magnet. |

**The Mohs scale.** Each mineral scratches the ones below it: 1 talc, 2 gypsum, 3 calcite, 4 fluorite, 5 apatite, 6 orthoclase (potassium feldspar), 7 quartz, 8 topaz, 9 corundum, 10 diamond. Everyday reference tools: fingernail about 2.5, copper wire about 3.5, steel knife blade or window glass about 5.5, steel file about 6.5, porcelain streak plate about 7. The scale is a ranking, not a measurement with equal steps: Earle notes that diamond is about three times harder than corundum even though the numbers differ by only 1.

### The three rock families

**Igneous rocks** form when molten rock cools and solidifies. Molten rock underground is **magma**; once erupted, it is **lava**.

- **Intrusive** (plutonic) rocks cool slowly inside Earth, so their crystals grow large enough to see. Example: **granite**.
- **Extrusive** (volcanic) rocks cool quickly at the surface, so crystals are tiny or the rock is glassy. Example: **basalt**, which makes up most of the ocean floor.
- Rule: the slower the cooling, the larger the crystals.

**Sedimentary rocks** form from sediment that accumulates at the surface and is **compacted** and **cemented** into solid rock (**lithification**). They often show layers. NPS groups them as:

- **Clastic**, made of pieces of other rocks, named by grain size: clay is smallest, then silt, then sand; grains larger than 2 mm are pebbles. Examples: shale (fine), sandstone (sand), conglomerate (pebbles).
- **Chemical**, which precipitate from water, for example when water evaporates and leaves minerals behind (rock salt, gypsum).
- **Biologic**, made from the remains of organisms: **coal** from plant material, and **limestone** built mostly from the calcite shells and skeletons of marine organisms.

**Metamorphic rocks** form when heat and pressure change an existing rock (its **parent rock**) without melting it completely. Minerals recrystallize or new minerals grow.

- **Foliated** rocks have aligned, platy minerals that form layers or bands, because they grew under directed pressure. In order of increasing metamorphism: **slate** (from shale) → phyllite → **schist** → **gneiss**.
- **Non-foliated** rocks have no layering: **marble** (from limestone) and **quartzite** (from sandstone).

### The rock cycle

Steven Earle's *Physical Geology* describes the rock cycle as driven by two energy sources: **Earth's internal heat**, which moves material in the mantle and crust, and the **hydrological cycle**, which moves water, ice, and air at the surface and is powered by the Sun.

- **Melting** (internal heat) makes magma, typically about 800–1300 °C.
- **Cooling and crystallization** make igneous rock: slow cooling in the crust takes centuries to millions of years; lava at the surface cools in seconds to years.
- **Uplift** (plate tectonics) exposes buried rock at the surface.
- **Weathering** breaks rock down where it sits.
- **Erosion** moves the pieces by water, ice, wind, waves, and gravity, and **deposition** drops them in rivers, lakes, deserts, and the ocean.
- **Burial, compaction, and cementation** at depths of hundreds of meters or more make sedimentary rock.
- **Heat and pressure** at greater depth make metamorphic rock.

There is no required order. Any rock type can become any other: an igneous rock can be metamorphosed without ever becoming sediment, and a sedimentary rock can be uplifted and weathered again.

### Weathering is not erosion

- **Mechanical weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals. Examples: **frost wedging** (water freezes in a crack and widens it, again and again), **exfoliation** (rock expands and cracks in sheets when overlying rock is removed), and **root wedging** by plants.
- **Chemical weathering** changes the minerals. Rain plus carbon dioxide forms weak **carbonic acid**. Feldspar slowly turns into clay minerals; calcite dissolves in acidic water; quartz is almost unaffected. Chemical weathering is fastest in warm, wet climates.
- **Erosion** is the *transport* of weathered material. A boulder can be weathered in place for thousands of years before anything erodes it.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Identifying a mineral from test results

*Illustrative test results.* A student records: non-metallic glassy luster; scratches glass; is **not** scratched by a steel file; breaks with curved, irregular surfaces (no cleavage); white streak.

1. Scratches glass (about 5.5) → hardness above 5.5.
2. Not scratched by a steel file (about 6.5) → hardness above 6.5.
3. The common mineral on the Mohs list just above 6.5 is **quartz** (7). Topaz (8) and corundum (9) are much less common.
4. No cleavage and a glassy luster fit quartz, which fractures instead of cleaving.
5. Conclusion: most likely quartz. Notice that color was never used.

### Example 2: Narrowing hardness with reference tools

*Illustrative test results.* A colorless mineral is not scratched by a fingernail but is scratched by a copper wire. It breaks into blocky pieces with flat faces. A teacher adds a drop of dilute acid and the surface fizzes.

1. Not scratched by a fingernail → harder than 2.5.
2. Scratched by copper → softer than 3.5.
3. So the hardness is between 2.5 and 3.5; calcite (3) fits.
4. Fizzing in dilute acid is the special property of calcite: the acid releases carbon dioxide gas from calcium carbonate (a chemical change).
5. Conclusion: calcite.

### Example 3: Using density to separate look-alikes

*Illustrative measurements.* Two dark samples are each lowered into a graduated cylinder.

| Sample | Mass | Water level rise |
|---|---|---|
| X | 81 g | 30 mL |
| Y | 104 g | 20 mL |

1. Volume = water displaced, and 1 mL = 1 cm³.
2. Density X $= 81 \div 30 = 2.7\ \text{g/cm}^3$, inside the 2.6–3.0 range of most common minerals.
3. Density Y $= 104 \div 20 = 5.2\ \text{g/cm}^3$, above 5 g/cm³, which points to a metallic mineral such as magnetite or hematite.
4. Next test for Y: if it attracts a magnet, magnetite is likely; if it leaves a reddish-brown streak, hematite is likely.

### Example 4: Classifying three rocks

1. *Light-colored rock with interlocking crystals 3–5 mm across, no layers.* Interlocking crystals → igneous; large, visible crystals → slow cooling → **intrusive**; light color and those crystals fit **granite**.
2. *Rock made of rounded sand grains glued together, in layers, with a ripple pattern on one surface.* Grains cemented together and layered → **clastic sedimentary**; sand-sized grains → **sandstone**.
3. *Dark gray rock that splits into thin, flat sheets; found in a mountain belt where shale layers have been squeezed.* Flat splitting from aligned tiny minerals → **foliated metamorphic**; parent rock shale, low grade → **slate**.

### Example 5: A path around the rock cycle

Trace a grain of quartz from granite to quartzite.

1. Granite (intrusive igneous) is **uplifted** by mountain building and exposed.
2. **Chemical weathering** turns its feldspar to clay, but the quartz survives; **mechanical weathering** frees the quartz grains.
3. A river **erodes** the grains and **deposits** them on a beach as sand.
4. The sand is **buried**, **compacted**, and **cemented** into **sandstone**.
5. During a later plate collision, the sandstone is heated and squeezed into **quartzite** (non-foliated metamorphic).
6. Energy sources: internal heat (uplift, metamorphism) and the Sun-driven water cycle (weathering, erosion). Earle's estimate for a single step like this is very roughly 20 million years, so the full path takes tens of millions of years or more.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Color identifies a mineral."** Many minerals come in several colors. Streak, hardness, cleavage, and density are more reliable.
- **"Hard means it can't break."** Hardness is resistance to *scratching*. Quartz (7) is harder than calcite (3), but a quartz crystal can still shatter if dropped.
- **"Mohs 10 is twice as hard as Mohs 5."** The Mohs scale is an order, not equal steps.
- **"Weathering and erosion are the same thing."** Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion carries the pieces away.
- **"The rock cycle runs in a fixed circle."** Rocks can skip steps or go "backward," for example from igneous straight to metamorphic.
- **"Metamorphic rocks form by melting."** If the rock melts and cools, it becomes igneous. Metamorphism changes rock while it stays solid.
- **Boundary:** classification schemes differ. NPS lists limestone as biologic sedimentary rock; Earle lists it as chemical sedimentary rock while noting it is made almost entirely of shell fragments of marine organisms. Both agree on how it forms; they file it differently.

## Practice

1. Is halite (rock salt dug from a salt mine) a mineral? Is steel? Use the five characteristics to explain.
2. A mineral is scratched by a knife (5.5) but not by copper (3.5). Which Mohs minerals could it be?
3. Why is streak a better clue than color for hematite?
4. *Illustrative data:* A sample has a mass of 39 g and displaces 15 mL of water. Find its density. Is it more likely a common mineral or a metallic one?
5. Classify each rock and name the evidence: (a) black, glassy rock that cooled from lava, (b) layered rock made of shells cemented together, (c) rock with bands of light and dark minerals that formed deep in a mountain belt.
6. Basalt and gabbro have similar minerals, but basalt crystals are tiny and gabbro crystals are large. Explain the difference.
7. Classify each as mechanical weathering, chemical weathering, or erosion: (a) a tree root splits a rock, (b) a river carries sand to the sea, (c) rainwater slowly dissolves a limestone statue, (d) water freezes in a crack and widens it.
8. Describe one path from a sedimentary rock to an igneous rock, naming each process.

<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. Halite is naturally occurring, inorganic, solid, has an ordered crystal structure (it breaks into cubes), and has a definite composition (sodium chloride, NaCl), so it is a mineral. Steel is not, because it is human-made.
2. Hardness between 3.5 and 5.5: fluorite (4) or apatite (5).
3. Hematite can look red or shiny black, but its powder (streak) is reddish-brown either way.
4. $39 \div 15 = 2.6\ \text{g/cm}^3$, within the 2.6–3.0 range of most common minerals.
5. (a) extrusive igneous: glassy texture from very fast cooling. (b) biologic sedimentary (limestone or coquina): cemented shells, layering. (c) foliated metamorphic (gneiss): mineral bands formed under heat and directed pressure.
6. Basalt cooled quickly at or near the surface, so crystals had little time to grow; gabbro cooled slowly underground, so crystals grew large.
7. (a) mechanical weathering (b) erosion (c) chemical weathering (d) mechanical weathering (frost wedging).
8. Example: sandstone is buried deeply and heated (metamorphism to quartzite), continues to heat until it **melts** into magma, and the magma **cools and crystallizes** into igneous rock. Any path that includes melting followed by cooling is correct.

> **Safety note:** Hardness and streak tests are safe with a streak plate, a copper coin or wire, and a steel nail; scratch away from your body and keep sharp edges pointed down. Do **not** do acid tests yourself; the calcite fizz test uses dilute hydrochloric acid and is for a teacher wearing goggles. NPS asks visitors to leave rocks and minerals in national parks where they are found, so collect samples only where it is allowed.
