# 18.4 - Transoceanic Connections

In 1450, no ship had yet crossed the Atlantic and returned with news of the Americas, and no European ship had sailed around Africa into the Indian Ocean. By 1750, ships regularly connected every inhabited continent except Australia. Silver mined in the Americas paid for Chinese silk and tea. American crops such as maize and potatoes fed people in Europe, Africa, and Asia. European diseases killed a large majority of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas in many regions. And European traders forced millions of Africans across the Atlantic to work on plantations. This lesson explains how these connections formed and who paid their costs.

This lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). Its historical-thinking skill is **using quantitative evidence**: reading estimates carefully, calculating with them, and stating what numbers can and cannot tell us.

**Content note:** this lesson discusses conquest, mass death from disease, and the enslavement of Africans. The details are included because they are central to the history. Quoted primary sources come from a survivor of the slave trade.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

4. Define **mercantilism** and the **joint-stock company** and analyze how they linked European states, colonies, and Asian trade.

## Core ideas

### Why Europeans sailed

Historians often summarize the motives as wealth, religion, and rivalry:

- **Wealth:** direct access to African gold and Asian spices and silk, without Venetian, Ottoman, or other middlemen.
- **Religion:** Spain's and Portugal's rulers "were also dedicated to spreading Christianity and combating the spread of Islam."
- **Rivalry:** each monarchy feared the other would win the routes first.

### Key voyages and agreements

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1415 | Portugal captures the Muslim port of **Ceuta** in Morocco, at the end of caravan routes carrying West African gold |
| 1488 | **Bartolomeu Dias** sails around the Cape of Good Hope |
| 1492 | Spain conquers the last Muslim stronghold in Iberia; on August 3, **Christopher Columbus** sails west and in October reaches the Bahamas |
| 1494 | The **Treaty of Tordesillas** divides newly claimed non-Christian lands between Spain and Portugal along a line in the Atlantic, "without reference to the sovereignty" of the people who lived there; the line later gave Portugal Brazil |
| 1498 | **Vasco da Gama** reaches Calicut, India, the first European to sail all the way to India around Africa |
| 1519–1521 | **Hernán Cortés** and his Tlaxcalan allies conquer the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlán, which becomes Mexico City |
| 1532 | **Francisco Pizarro** captures the Inca ruler Atahualpa, during a civil war after the previous ruler died of smallpox |

Columbus made four voyages between 1492 and 1504, all to the Caribbean, and "continued to believe he had found a route to Asia."

### The Columbian Exchange

The **Columbian Exchange** is the transfer of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres that began with Columbus's voyages.

| From Europe, Africa, and Asia to the Americas | From the Americas to Europe, Africa, and Asia |
|---|---|
| Wheat, European grapes, pears, peaches, apples, onions, garlic | Maize (corn), potatoes and sweet potatoes, tomatoes, chili peppers |
| Livestock such as hogs, cattle, and horses | Manioc (cassava), peanuts, pineapples, vanilla |
| Metal tools and weapons | Chocolate and tobacco, which became "especially popular in Europe" |
| Smallpox, measles, typhus, influenza, and other diseases; malaria and yellow fever arrived with the slave trade | |

**Population collapse.** When Columbus arrived, the Indigenous population of the Western Hemisphere "likely numbered around seventy-five million," compared with roughly seventy million in Europe, "although historians' estimates vary greatly." Some scholars estimate death rates from new diseases as high as **95 percent** in affected groups. But OpenStax stresses that disease was not the only cause. Loose European livestock destroyed Indigenous crops; Spanish slave raids drove people into hiding so they could not farm or fish; malnutrition weakened immune systems; and forced labor, violence, and the destruction of communities made recovery harder. On the island of Hispaniola, the Taíno population, "at least several hundred thousand strong" in 1492, had fallen to about **thirty-two thousand** by 1514.

### The Atlantic slave trade

European colonists first tried to force Indigenous people to grow sugar and other cash crops. When those populations collapsed, and European servants were too few and died at high rates from malaria and yellow fever, colonists turned to enslaved Africans. OpenStax summarizes the scale: between the late fifteenth and early nineteenth centuries, Europeans "forcefully transported approximately twelve million Africans across the Atlantic Ocean," and "some two million people died on the voyages."

The **triangular trade** describes the pattern of three legs:

1. **Europe → Africa:** manufactured goods such as cloth, guns, ammunition, and rum were exchanged with African traders for captives.
2. **Africa → Americas (the Middle Passage):** captives were shipped in chains, some ships holding five to six hundred people. Captains expected 10 to 20 percent to die on the voyage.
3. **Americas → Europe:** sugar, tobacco, coffee, indigo, and rice produced by enslaved labor were shipped to Europe and processed.

"Enslaved people were the base on which the triangle rested." In the Caribbean, disease, overwork, and high infant mortality meant the enslaved population "was not self-reproducing," so plantation owners kept buying new captives. In Africa, slave raiding spread fear and violence; guns traded for captives were used to capture more people.

Africans resisted. Queen **Nzinga** of Ndongo and Matamba, in what is now Angola, fought the Portuguese for decades before concluding a peace treaty in 1656.

### Mercantilism and joint-stock companies

**Mercantilism** (a name given by later historians) was the theory that a nation's power depended on its wealth, measured in gold and silver, and that the world's wealth was limited, so "a gain for one nation was a loss for another." To win, a country needed a **favorable balance of trade**: exporting more than it imported. Colonies fit this theory by supplying raw materials and buying the home country's manufactured goods.

A **joint-stock company** raised money by selling shares to many investors, who shared profits and risks. States granted such companies monopolies on trade. The **British East India Company**, founded in 1600, first traded in spices and then in Indian textiles; the **Dutch East India Company** established a settlement at the Cape of Good Hope in 1652.

**Asia and American silver.** China exported tea, silk, and porcelain, rejected most European goods, and "insisted on payment for its exports in silver." Spanish galleons from Manila carried silver from American mines to China and returned with Chinese silks. American silver thus connected the Atlantic and Pacific worlds.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Calculating with estimates

**Question:** Using OpenStax's estimates, what share of Africans forced onto slave ships died during the Atlantic crossing?

1. **Identify the numbers:** about 12 million transported; some 2 million died on the voyages.
2. **Calculate:** 2 ÷ 12 × 100 ≈ 16.7 percent, about one in six.
3. **Check against another figure:** captains expected "10 to 20 percent" to die per voyage. A long-run average of about 17 percent falls inside that range, so the estimates are consistent.
4. **State the limits:** both numbers are rounded estimates built from incomplete ship records. The calculation also leaves out people who died during capture in Africa, while held on the coast, or soon after arrival, so it measures only one part of the trade's death toll.
5. **Write it carefully:** "OpenStax's estimates imply that roughly one in six captives died during the crossing, although the total number killed by the slave trade was higher."

### Example 2: Using a survivor's narrative as evidence

Olaudah Equiano, kidnapped as a child, published his autobiography in London in 1789 and traveled throughout Britain as an abolitionist. He described arriving at the coast:

> "The first object which saluted my eyes when I arrived on the coast was the sea, and a slave ship, which was then riding at anchor, and waiting for its cargo. These filled me with astonishment, which was soon converted into terror when I was carried on board."

1. **Meet the source:** a published first-person narrative by a formerly enslaved man, written decades after the events.
2. **Purpose and audience:** Equiano wrote for British readers during the campaign to abolish the slave trade, so he aimed to make them feel its horror.
3. **What it shows:** how terrifying and unfamiliar capture and the slave ship were to a child taken from inland Africa, a perspective ship records cannot provide.
4. **Corroborate:** OpenStax's broader account of slave raids that reached deep into the African interior and of crowded, deadly Middle Passage ships, drawn from many kinds of evidence, is consistent with his description.
5. **Conclusion:** Equiano's narrative is powerful evidence of the experience of enslavement. Like any memoir, it was written with a purpose and from memory, so historians read it alongside other sources rather than alone.

### Example 3: Explaining a population collapse with multiple causes

**Claim to test:** "Indigenous Americans died because they had no immunity to European diseases."

1. **Supporting evidence:** smallpox, measles, typhus, and other diseases were new to the Americas, and some estimated death rates reached 95 percent.
2. **Additional causes (OpenStax):** livestock destroyed crops; slave raids prevented farming and fishing; malnutrition weakened immune systems; forced labor and violence killed people directly.
3. **Case evidence:** on Hispaniola, some Taíno "had been deliberately killed by the Spanish, and others had died from hard labor and poor living conditions after being enslaved," although "the vast majority" died of disease.
4. **Revised claim:** "Disease was the largest single cause of Indigenous population collapse, but conquest, enslavement, and the destruction of food supplies made the epidemics far deadlier."

### Example 4: Applying mercantilist logic

**Scenario:** England imports 100 units (by value) of goods and exports 80.

1. **Calculate the balance:** exports − imports = 80 − 100 = −20, an **unfavorable** balance of trade.
2. **Mercantilist interpretation:** England must pay the difference in gold or silver, so its wealth flows to rivals.
3. **Mercantilist solution:** acquire colonies that supply raw materials (so England imports less from rivals) and that must buy English manufactured goods (so England exports more).
4. **Connect to evidence:** this logic helps explain why England, France, and the Netherlands "set out to establish colonies in the Americas" after Spain's and Portugal's success.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Columbus proved the Earth was round."** Educated Europeans already knew it was round. Columbus underestimated its size, and he "continued to believe he had found a route to Asia."
- **"Columbus was the first person to reach the Americas."** Millions of Indigenous people already lived there, and OpenStax notes his 1492 voyage "did not represent the first contact between European explorers and Indigenous peoples."
- **"Disease alone explains the population collapse."** Disease was the largest cause, but violence, enslavement, famine, and the destruction of communities made it far worse.
- **"The Columbian Exchange mostly benefited Europe."** American crops such as maize, potatoes, and manioc became staples in Africa and Asia too. The exchange's benefits and costs were distributed very unequally, with the heaviest costs falling on Indigenous Americans and enslaved Africans.
- **"Africans had no role or no resistance in the slave trade."** African traders and rulers sold captives in exchange for European goods, and many Africans resisted, from village watch systems to Queen Nzinga's long war against the Portuguese. Both facts are part of the history.
- **"The triangular trade was a neat triangle."** It is a simplified model. Ships followed many routes, and goods such as molasses and rum moved among colonies too.
- **Boundary: all numbers here are estimates.** The hemisphere's 1492 population is especially uncertain. Report estimates with their source and say "about."
