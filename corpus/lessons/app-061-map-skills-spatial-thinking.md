# APP-06.1 — Map Skills & Spatial Thinking

Every map is a model. It shrinks a piece of the Earth, leaves most details out, and turns the rest into symbols. A good map reader asks the same questions a good reader of any source asks: who made this, for what purpose, and what did they choose to show or hide? This lesson teaches the tools geographers use to read and make maps: map elements, latitude and longitude, scale, contour lines, and map projections. Every later lesson in this course depends on them.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). It also introduces the core habit of **spatial thinking**: describing *where* something is, explaining *why* it is there, and asking how places are connected.

## Prerequisites

- No earlier lesson is required. This is the first lesson in the [Geography course](/subject/geography).
- Comfort with ratios, multiplication, and division (for example, 3.5 × 2,000 = 7,000).
- Access to a globe, a printed atlas, or a school-approved digital map. Before you begin, find the equator and the prime meridian on it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Identify the **elements of a map** (title, orientation, legend, scale, grid, date, author, and sources) and explain what each one tells a reader.
2. Locate places with **latitude and longitude**, convert degrees to approximate ground distances, and calculate time differences from longitude.
3. Measure distance with a **map scale** and elevation change with **contour lines** on a topographic map.
4. Explain why every flat map **distorts** the Earth, compare the Mercator, Robinson, and equal-area projections, and choose a suitable projection or thematic map for a purpose.

## Core ideas

### Kinds of maps

| Kind of map | What it shows | Example question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Political (reference)** | Borders, countries, states, cities | Which countries border Brazil? |
| **Physical (reference)** | Landforms and water, often with shading for elevation | Where are the highest mountains in Asia? |
| **Topographic** | The shape and elevation of the land using contour lines, plus roads, buildings, and water | How steep is this trail? |
| **Thematic** | One topic or variable, such as rainfall, population density, or election results | Where is rainfall highest? |
| **Chart** | Maps made for navigating ships or aircraft | Is this channel deep enough? |

Thematic maps use several designs. A **choropleth map** shades whole areas (such as counties) by a value. A **dot map** places a dot for a set number of things. A **proportional symbol map** draws larger symbols for larger values. An **isoline map** connects points of equal value, such as equal temperature. Contour lines on a topographic map are isolines of elevation.

### The elements of a map

National Geographic's education site suggests the memory aid **DOGSTAILS**: Date, Orientation, Grid, Scale, Title, Author, Index, Legend, and Sources.

- **Date:** when the map was made, or the date its information describes. A map dated 1847 may show 1847 conditions or may have been drawn later about 1847.
- **Orientation:** a north arrow or compass rose. If there is only one arrow, it usually points north.
- **Grid:** lines that help you find places, either an arbitrary grid (A1, B2) or latitude and longitude.
- **Scale:** how map distances compare with real distances.
- **Title:** what the map shows and its purpose, such as "Population Map of Canada."
- **Author:** who made it. Knowing the author helps you judge purpose and possible bias.
- **Index:** an alphabetical list of places with grid references.
- **Legend (key):** what each symbol, line, and color means on this particular map. Symbols differ from map to map, so always read the legend first.
- **Sources:** where the data came from, such as GPS, national surveys, or government records. Sources are vital for judging accuracy.

### Absolute and relative location

**Absolute location** is a precise position, usually given as latitude and longitude. **Relative location** describes a place in relation to other places ("about 150 kilometers upriver from the coast"). Geographers also distinguish **site**, a place's own physical characteristics, from **situation**, its connections to its surroundings. The open textbook *Introduction to Human Geography* (Dorrell and Henderson) uses New Orleans as an example: its site is poor for a city, because it lies below sea level and floods, but its situation is excellent, because it connects the Mississippi River's network of navigable waterways to the Gulf of Mexico.

### Latitude

**Latitude** measures distance north or south of the equator. According to NOAA's National Ocean Service:

- Lines of latitude, called **parallels**, run east–west, parallel to the equator (0°), and reach 90° at each pole.
- The **Tropic of Cancer** and **Tropic of Capricorn** lie at roughly 23° N and 23° S. The **Arctic** and **Antarctic Circles** lie at roughly 66° N and 66° S.
- Each degree of latitude covers about **111 kilometers** (about 69 miles). A degree divides into 60 minutes (′), and a minute into 60 seconds (″). One second of latitude is only about 30.7 meters.

### Longitude

**Longitude** measures distance east or west of the **prime meridian** (0°). Lines of longitude, called **meridians**, run from pole to pole.

- Unlike the equator, the prime meridian is **arbitrary**. At the International Meridian Conference of 1884 in Washington, D.C., representatives chose a line through the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, England, partly because many charts and time zones already used it. Modern satellite measurements place 0° about 102 meters east of the historic markers.
- Meridians are farthest apart at the equator and **meet at the poles**. One degree of longitude covers about 111 km at the equator, about 56 km at 60° N or S, and zero at the poles.
- The **antimeridian** (180°) is the basis of the International Date Line, but actual date and time-zone boundaries depend on local laws, so the date line zigzags. Travelers crossing it westward move the calendar **forward** one day; crossing eastward moves it back.

Coordinates are written latitude first: 38° N, 78° W means 38 degrees north of the equator and 78 degrees west of Greenwich.

### Longitude and time

The Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, so it turns **15° every hour** (360 ÷ 24 = 15). Before satellites, navigators found longitude by comparing local noon (the sun at its highest point) with a clock set to Greenwich time. Each hour of difference equals 15° of longitude. Standard **time zones** are based on this 15° spacing, but their boundaries are set by governments and follow borders rather than straight meridians.

### Scale

Scale compares map distance with ground distance.

- A **representative fraction** such as **1:24,000** means one unit on the map equals 24,000 of the same units on the ground, in any unit. One inch represents 24,000 inches, which is **2,000 feet**.
- A **bar scale** looks like a ruler marked in kilometers or miles. It stays correct even if the map is enlarged or shrunk, which a printed ratio does not.
- A **large-scale** map shows a small area in great detail (a neighborhood). A **small-scale** map shows a large area with little detail (a continent). Zooming in on a phone changes a small-scale map into a large-scale one.

The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) has made topographic maps since 1879. Its best-known series is the **1:24,000-scale 7.5-minute quadrangle**, so called because each map covers 7.5 minutes of latitude by 7.5 minutes of longitude. Alaska is mapped mainly at 1:63,360, where 1 inch represents 1 mile.

### Contour lines

The distinctive feature of a topographic map is the **contour line**: an imaginary line connecting points of the same elevation, usually measured above mean sea level. The **contour interval** is the elevation difference between neighboring lines (for example, 10 or 20 feet). USGS describes the key reading rule: in steep areas contours are closely spaced; in flatter areas they are far apart. Contours also reveal shape. Closed loops mark hills, and contours that bend into a V usually point upstream where they cross a valley or stream.

### Map projections: why every flat map is wrong somewhere

A **projection** is a method of transferring the curved surface of the Earth onto a flat surface. USGS states the central rule: every flat map misrepresents the surface of the Earth in some way. A map can show true directions, true distances, true areas, or true shapes in some places, but **never all four at once**. "There is no 'best' projection." The mapmaker chooses which distortions matter least for the map's purpose.

National Geographic groups projections into three families: **planar** (most accurate at the center; often used for polar maps), **conic** (accurate in the midlatitudes; often used for maps of the United States), and **cylindrical** (most accurate near the equator; often used for world maps).

| Projection | Type | Preserves | Distorts | Typical use (USGS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mercator** (presented 1569) | Cylindrical | Shapes of small areas (**conformal**); any straight line is a line of constant compass direction | Areas and distances, increasingly toward the poles; the poles cannot be shown | Navigation; maps of equatorial regions |
| **Robinson** (presented 1963) | Pseudocylindrical "compromise" | Nothing exactly; aims to make the world "look right" | Some distortion everywhere, greatest near the poles | General world maps; adopted for National Geographic's world maps in 1988 |
| **Sinusoidal** and other **equal-area** projections (for example, Gall–Peters) | Varies | Relative **areas** | Shapes, especially far from the center | Maps of distributions, where comparing area matters |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Finding longitude from time

**Problem:** A ship's navigator sees the sun reach its highest point (local noon). A clock set to Greenwich time reads 5:00 p.m. What is the ship's longitude?

1. **Find the time difference:** 5:00 p.m. − 12:00 noon = 5 hours.
2. **Convert to degrees:** the Earth turns 15° per hour, so 5 × 15° = **75°**.
3. **Decide east or west:** Greenwich has already passed noon, so the sun reaches Greenwich before it reaches the ship. The sun appears to move westward, so the ship is **west** of Greenwich.
4. **Answer:** 75° W.
5. **Check with NOAA's example:** a 2-hour difference with Greenwich at 2 p.m. gives 30° W, the same method.

### Example 2: Converting degrees to distance

**Problem:** USGS notes that the parallel 38° N passes through Stockton, California, and Charlottesville, Virginia. At 38° N, USGS gives 1° of latitude ≈ 69 miles and 1° of longitude ≈ 54.6 miles. How far apart are two points on 38° N that are 10° of longitude apart? How does this compare with two points 10° of latitude apart on the same meridian?

1. **Along the parallel:** 10 × 54.6 = **546 miles**.
2. **Along the meridian:** 10 × 69 = **690 miles**.
3. **Explain the difference:** meridians converge toward the poles, so a degree of longitude shrinks with latitude. A degree of latitude stays nearly constant.
4. **Limit the claim:** distance along a parallel is not the shortest route between two points; the shortest route on a sphere follows a great circle. For a rough estimate, these numbers work well.

### Example 3: Scale and contours on a topographic map

**Problem:** On a 1:24,000 USGS map, a trail measures 3.5 inches. It starts on the 400-foot contour and ends on the 520-foot contour. The contour interval is 20 feet. Find the trail's length and its average grade.

1. **Ground distance:** 1 inch = 2,000 feet, so 3.5 × 2,000 = **7,000 feet**, or 7,000 ÷ 5,280 ≈ **1.3 miles**.
2. **Elevation gain:** 520 − 400 = **120 feet**. Check by counting intervals: 120 ÷ 20 = 6 contour intervals, so the trail should cross six spaces between lines.
3. **Average grade:** rise ÷ run = 120 ÷ 7,000 ≈ 0.017, or about **1.7 percent**.
4. **Interpret:** that is a gentle average climb. If the contours are bunched together on one part of the trail, that part is steeper than the average, so the average hides variation.

### Example 4: Measuring Mercator's distortion

**Problem:** Why do Greenland, northern Canada, and Russia look so large on a Mercator map, and by how much is area exaggerated at 60° N?

1. **Use a USGS fact about the globe:** on a globe, meridians at 60° are **half as far apart** as at the equator.
2. **Compare with the Mercator map:** on Mercator, all meridians are equally spaced straight lines. So at 60°, east–west distances are stretched by a factor of **2**.
3. **Use the conformal property:** Mercator preserves the shapes of small areas, so north–south distances at 60° must be stretched by the same factor, **2**.
4. **Combine:** area is stretched by 2 × 2 = **4**. A region at 60° appears about four times as large as a region of equal real area at the equator.
5. **Generalize:** the stretch factor at latitude φ is 1 ÷ cos φ, and the area factor is its square. At 30° the area factor is about 1.33, so a region at 60° looks about 3 times larger than an equal-area region at 30° (4 ÷ 1.33 ≈ 3).
6. **Conclusion:** Mercator is not "wrong." It is designed so that a straight line is a constant compass bearing, which helps navigators. It is a poor choice for comparing the sizes of countries.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Mercator map is a mistake."** It is a tool with a purpose. USGS lists navigation as its use; the problem is using it to compare areas.
- **"A large-scale map shows a large area."** The reverse is true. Large scale means objects look large, so the map covers a small area in detail.
- **"Longitude lines are evenly spaced like latitude lines."** Meridians converge. A degree of longitude covers about 111 km at the equator, 56 km at 60°, and zero at the poles.
- **"Every time zone is exactly 15° wide."** The 15° spacing is the basis, but governments set the boundaries, and some places use offsets of half an hour.
- **"The prime meridian is a natural line, like the equator."** The equator is defined by the Earth's rotation; the prime meridian was chosen by international agreement in 1884.
- **"Contour lines are trails or roads."** They are lines of equal elevation. Two contour lines of different elevation do not cross, although they can merge at a vertical cliff.
- **"A choropleth map of totals shows where something is concentrated."** Big areas dominate a choropleth visually. Rates (per person or per square kilometer) are usually fairer than raw totals when areas differ in size.
- **Boundary: GPS and old markers can disagree.** At Greenwich, GPS reads 0° about 102 meters east of the historic line because modern measurement uses a different reference.

## Practice

1. A map has a title, a north arrow, and a legend, but no date, author, or sources. Name two questions you cannot answer about the map and explain why they matter.
2. Name the line of latitude or longitude closest to each location: (a) 23° S; (b) 66° N; (c) 0° latitude, 0° longitude; (d) 180° longitude.
3. **Time and longitude:** (a) At local noon, a Greenwich clock reads 3:00 p.m. Find the longitude. (b) When it is noon in Greenwich, what is the local solar time at 120° E?
4. **Scale:** (a) On a 1:24,000 map, two buildings are 2.25 inches apart. How far apart are they on the ground, in feet? (b) On a 1:63,360 map, a road segment measures 3 inches. How long is it in miles? (c) Which of these two maps is larger scale, and which shows more detail?
5. **Contours:** A map's contour interval is 40 feet. A hiker starts on the 1,200-foot contour and climbs to the 1,480-foot contour. How many contour intervals does she cross, and what is her elevation gain? On which side of the hill are the contours closest together if the east slope is the steepest?
6. **Choose a projection** and give a reason: (a) a sailing route that keeps one compass bearing; (b) a map comparing the forest area of countries; (c) a classroom world wall map; (d) a map of Antarctica.
7. Using Example 4's method, find Mercator's area exaggeration at 45° (hint: cos 45° ≈ 0.707) and at 75° (cos 75° ≈ 0.259).
8. **Suggested activity: map critique (individual or pairs, 20–30 minutes).** Choose one map you can see in print or on a school-approved website, such as a weather map, a transit map, or an atlas page. List which DOGSTAILS elements it has and which are missing. Then write three sentences: what the map's purpose seems to be, one thing it distorts or leaves out, and whether the projection or scale suits that purpose. No account or personal information is needed. If you choose a map of your own neighborhood, do not include your home address.

> **Note on sources and viewpoints:** Facts in this lesson come from U.S. government science agencies (NOAA's National Ocean Service and the U.S. Geological Survey), National Geographic's education encyclopedia, and an openly licensed university textbook. Map conventions are human choices: the prime meridian at Greenwich, "north up" orientation, and the use of Mercator for world maps all reflect particular histories. Other conventions are possible, and a critical map reader asks why a mapmaker chose them. The Mercator calculation in Example 4 is a geometric model; distortion on real maps also depends on the version of the projection used.
