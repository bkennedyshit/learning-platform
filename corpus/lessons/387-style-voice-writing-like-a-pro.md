# 38.7 — Style & Voice: Writing Like a Pro

> *"Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."* — William Strunk Jr.

Grammar gets your writing to "correct." This chapter gets it to "compelling." The difference is style — the choices you make at the sentence level that give your prose its character, rhythm, and authority. Style can be learned. Voice develops through practice and attention. This chapter gives you the principles and the drills.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Distinguish active from passive voice and apply each correctly.
2. Identify and cut deadwood from prose: nominalizations, throat-clearing, hedging, and padding.
3. Vary sentence structure and length to create rhythm and emphasis.
4. Achieve parallel structure in lists, comparisons, and series.
5. Apply Zinsser's four principles of nonfiction: clarity, brevity, simplicity, and humanity.
6. Identify your own default style patterns — and deliberately expand them.

## 📚 1. Active vs. Passive Voice

### The distinction

**Active:** Subject performs the action.
> *The engineer deployed the fix.*

**Passive:** Subject receives the action; the agent may be omitted.
> *The fix was deployed (by the engineer).*

### Why active is usually better

Active voice is:
- **Clearer** — the doer is immediately visible.
- **More direct** — fewer words for the same information.
- **More energetic** — the action lands harder.

### When passive is correct (and even preferred)

| When | Example |
|------|---------|
| The agent is unknown | *The server was restarted at 3 AM.* (who did it is unknown or irrelevant) |
| The agent is unimportant or obvious | *The package was installed.* (context: it's clear who installed it) |
| The receiver of the action is the focus | *Three engineers were promoted this quarter.* (the engineers are the story, not who promoted them) |
| Scientific writing convention | *The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm.* (passive is standard in methods sections) |
| Deliberate evasion of accountability (recognize this!) | *Mistakes were made.* (famously passive to avoid assigning blame) |

### Identifying passive voice

Passive = **form of *to be*** + **past participle**

*was deployed, is being reviewed, has been committed, were deleted, will be approved*

**Quick test:** Ask "by whom?" after the verb. If you can answer and the answer isn't in the sentence, it's likely passive.

## 📚 2. Cutting Deadwood

Deadwood is writing that adds length but not meaning. It is the enemy of clarity.

### 2.1 Nominalizations (Zombie Nouns)

A **nominalization** is a verb or adjective converted into a noun. They make prose abstract, heavy, and slow.

| Nominalization | Revived verb/adjective version |
|---------------|-------------------------------|
| *make a decision* | *decide* |
| *give consideration to* | *consider* |
| *conduct an investigation* | *investigate* |
| *reach an agreement* | *agree* |
| *provide assistance* | *help* / *assist* |
| *demonstrate an ability* | *can* |
| *perform an analysis* | *analyze* |
| *have a tendency to* | *tend to* |
| *is in agreement with* | *agrees* |
| *exhibits a preference for* | *prefers* |

> **Rule of thumb:** If you have a weak verb (*make, give, have, do, perform, conduct, provide, reach*) + an abstract noun, look for a strong verb that carries the meaning directly.

**Before:** *The committee reached a decision to conduct an investigation of the system's failure modes.*
**After:** *The committee decided to investigate how the system fails.*

### 2.2 Throat-Clearing Openers

Phrases that delay the actual sentence:

| Throat-clearer | Cut to: |
|---------------|---------|
| *It is important to note that…* | (just state the important thing) |
| *It should be pointed out that…* | (point it out) |
| *The fact that…* | (rewrite without "the fact that") |
| *Due to the fact that…* | *Because…* |
| *In order to…* | *To…* |
| *At this point in time…* | *Now* |
| *In the event that…* | *If* |
| *For all intents and purposes…* | (cut entirely) |
| *It goes without saying that…* | (then don't say it) |
| *As previously mentioned…* | (cut — if it was important, the reader remembers) |

### 2.3 Redundant Pairs and Triplets

The English legal tradition gave us redundant pairs (*null and void, cease and desist*). Only one word is needed:

| Redundant pair | Use instead |
|---------------|------------|
| *each and every* | *every* |
| *first and foremost* | *first* |
| *true and accurate* | *accurate* |
| *basic and fundamental* | *fundamental* |
| *various different* | *various* or *different* |
| *completely finished* | *finished* |
| *end result* | *result* |
| *past history* | *history* |
| *future plans* | *plans* |
| *advance warning* | *warning* |
| *free gift* | *gift* |
| *final outcome* | *outcome* |

### 2.4 Hedging Overload

Hedging is appropriate when you're genuinely uncertain. But over-hedging drains authority from every sentence:

| Over-hedged | Direct |
|------------|--------|
| *It seems like it might possibly be the case that…* | *It appears that…* or just assert the claim |
| *In my personal opinion, I think that…* | *I believe…* or just state the claim |
| *could potentially* | *could* (potential is already embedded) |
| *seems to suggest* | *suggests* |

> **The rule:** Hedge when you're uncertain. Be direct when you're not. Never hedge as a style default.

## 📚 3. Sentence Variety and Rhythm

Prose that's all one length is monotonous. Professional writers vary sentence length, structure, and rhythm to control pacing and emphasis.

### 3.1 Sentence Length as a Tool

| Short sentence | Effect |
|---------------|--------|
| Use after a long complex sentence | Emphasis. Landing the point. Breathing room. |
| Use for climax | *Everything else was negotiable. This wasn't.* |
| Use for urgency | *Deploy it now.* |

| Long sentence | Effect |
|--------------|--------|
| Building up context | Unfolding a complex idea with qualifications, examples, and dependent clauses that each add a layer of meaning. |
| Immersion | Narrative prose that draws the reader into a rhythm. |

**The Zinsser principle:** The sentence that earns the most weight should often be the shortest.

### 3.2 Sentence-Opening Variety

Don't start every sentence with the subject. Vary with:
- **Adverb:** *Slowly, the team accepted the diagnosis.*
- **Prepositional phrase:** *In the third week of the sprint, the architecture began to show cracks.*
- **Participial phrase:** *Reviewing the logs, she found the pattern.*
- **Adverb clause:** *When the deadline moved up, the quality went down.*
- **Inverted sentence:** *Gone were the days of simple deployments.*

### 3.3 Parallel Structure

When listing, comparing, or connecting ideas, make all elements grammatically parallel. Broken parallelism is jarring.

| Broken | Parallel |
|--------|---------|
| *She likes coding, to read, and long walks.* | *She likes coding, reading, and walking.* |
| *The system should be fast, scalable, and easy to maintain.* | *The system should be fast, scalable, and maintainable.* |
| *He was not only brilliant but also he worked hard.* | *He was not only brilliant but also hardworking.* |
| *To deploy or not deploying?* | *To deploy or not to deploy?* |

**Correlative conjunctions** (*either/or, neither/nor, not only/but also, both/and*) must connect grammatically parallel elements:
- ❌ *Either the tests pass or we need to delay.*
- ✅ *Either the tests pass or we delay.*

## 📚 4. Zinsser's Four Principles

From *On Writing Well* (Zinsser) — the four principles of excellent nonfiction prose:

### Clarity
The reader should never have to re-read a sentence to understand it. If they do, rewrite it.

Test: Read every sentence aloud. Where you stumble, the reader will too.

### Brevity
Every word must earn its place. Cut without mercy, then cut again.

Zinsser: "Look for the clutter in your writing and prune it ruthlessly. Be grateful for everything you can throw away."

**The revision habit:** After drafting, cut 10% of every document. Not 10% of sentences — 10% of words, spread across everything. The document will almost always be better.

### Simplicity
Prefer the simple word to the complex word, the concrete to the abstract, the specific to the general. Don't use *utilize* when *use* works. Don't use *commence* when you mean *start*.

**The simple word list:**

| Complex | Simple |
|---------|--------|
| *utilize* | *use* |
| *commence* | *start, begin* |
| *terminate* | *end, stop* |
| *facilitate* | *help, ease* |
| *prioritize* | *rank, order, focus on* |
| *leverage* (as verb) | *use* |
| *operationalize* | *apply, implement* |
| *conceptualize* | *imagine, design, think about* |
| *endeavor* | *try* |
| *demonstrate* | *show* |
| *ascertain* | *find out, determine* |
| *subsequent to* | *after* |

### Humanity
Writing is a human act, from a person to a person. Don't hide behind institutional language or impersonal passive constructions. Let the voice of a thinking person come through.

> "I" is not banned in professional writing. False objectivity — using passive voice and impersonal constructions to seem more authoritative — usually achieves the opposite, producing prose that sounds evasive and bureaucratic.

## 📚 5. Voice

**Voice** is the distinctive personality that comes through in prose. It is built from:

- **Sentence rhythm** — long and complex, or short and punchy?
- **Vocabulary level** — latinate and formal, or Anglo-Saxon and direct?
- **Tone** — ironic, earnest, analytical, warm, precise?
- **Persona** — what version of yourself do you project on the page?

Voice is not invented — it is uncovered through writing a lot, reading a lot, and paying attention to what you've produced.

### Finding your voice

1. Read writers you admire and articulate *why* you admire their prose. Is it the sentences? The vocabulary? The rhythm?
2. Imitate deliberately — pick a paragraph from a writer you love and reconstruct it from scratch with different content but the same syntactic structure.
3. Write without editing in your daily log. The constraints that disappear when no one is watching reveal your natural rhythms.
4. Read your drafts aloud. Where you sound like yourself, keep it. Where you sound like a robot, rewrite.

## ✍️ 6. Worked Examples — Revision Drills

### Revision 19.7.1 — Deadwood extraction

**Before:**
> *At this point in time, it is of considerable importance to give consideration to the fact that the implementation of the new deployment pipeline has demonstrated an ability to achieve a significant reduction in the amount of time required for builds.*

**After (29 words cut to 14):**
> *The new deployment pipeline cuts build time significantly.*

**What was removed:**
- "At this point in time" → (cut)
- "it is of considerable importance to" → (cut)
- "give consideration to" → "consider" → (point was embedded — just state the claim)
- "the fact that" → (cut)
- "the implementation of" → (nominalization → converted to subject)
- "has demonstrated an ability to achieve" → (zombie noun stack → "cuts")
- "a significant reduction in the amount of time required for" → "cuts time"

### Revision 19.7.2 — Passive to active

**Before:**
> *The decision was made by the team to migrate the database. The old schema was deprecated, and a new indexing strategy was implemented. Performance improvements were observed.*

**After:**
> *The team decided to migrate the database. They deprecated the old schema, implemented a new indexing strategy, and observed performance improvements.*

### Revision 19.7.3 — Parallel structure

**Before:**
> *The ideal engineer is someone who writes clean code, has the ability to communicate clearly, and showing up on time is important to them.*

**After:**
> *The ideal engineer writes clean code, communicates clearly, and shows up on time.*

## 🧮 7. Hands-On Lab

**10% cut:** Take any 500+ word document you've written (a project README, a daily log entry, an essay draft). Cut 10% of the words without losing any meaning. Track every word you cut and categorize it: deadwood, throat-clearing, redundancy, hedging, nominalization.

**Imitation exercise:** Pick one paragraph from *On Writing Well* or *The Elements of Style*. Reconstruct its syntactic structure using your own content. Compare rhythms.

**Before/after log:** For one week, whenever you revise a sentence, record the before and after. At the end of the week, look for patterns in what you're cutting.

**Tool:** Run every document through [Hemingway Editor](https://hemingwayapp.com/) and address every Grade 12+ sentence, every use of passive voice, and every adverb flag.
