# APP-01.2 — Phonics — Blending & Decoding

In the last lesson you heard the sounds inside spoken words. Now you will see those sounds on paper. **Phonics** is knowing which letters stand for which sounds, and using that to read and spell words. **Decoding** means turning letters into sounds and blending them into a word. When you see *s-a-t*, you say /s/ /a/ /t/ and blend: *sat*.

Letters are a code for sounds. Once you know the code, you can read words you have never seen before.

The course plan places this lesson in kindergarten and grade 1. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

## Prerequisites

[APP-01.1 — Phonemic Awareness & the Alphabet](/app-011-phonemic-awareness-the-alphabet): the learner should be able to blend three spoken sounds into a word and say the most common sound for most single letters, including the five short vowels.

> **Adult note:** Write each example word on a card or a whiteboard, or use letter tiles. Introduce one new pattern at a time and review the old ones first. Read the "Practice" words aloud only after the child has tried them. A U.S. Department of Education practice guide recommends this kind of order: common consonants and short vowels first, then blends and digraphs, then long vowels with silent *e*, and later vowel teams.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Read short-vowel words such as *sat*, *bed*, *sit*, *hop*, and *sun* by blending sounds from left to right.
2. Read words with blends (*stop*, *black*, *crab*) and digraphs (*ship*, *chin*, *thin*, *when*, *duck*), and say how a blend is different from a digraph.
3. Read and spell silent-*e* words such as *cake*, *bike*, and *hope*, and compare them with *cap*, *kit*, and *hop*.
4. Read a short sentence made of words like these and check that each word is a real word that makes sense.

## Core ideas

### Short vowels and the CVC pattern

The vowels are *a*, *e*, *i*, *o*, and *u*. Each has a **short** sound:

| Vowel | Short sound | Key word |
|---|---|---|
| a | /a/ | *cat* |
| e | /e/ | *bed* |
| i | /i/ | *sit* |
| o | /o/ | *hop* |
| u | /u/ | *sun* |

Many first words follow the pattern **consonant-vowel-consonant (CVC)**: *c-a-t*, *b-e-d*, *h-o-p*. In a CVC word, the vowel usually makes its short sound.

### Two ways to blend

- **Sounding out:** say each sound from left to right, stretching and connecting them: /sssaaat/, then say the word faster: *sat*.
- **Chunking:** add one sound at a time: /h/ + /a/ = /ha/; /ha/ + /t/ = *hat*.

Both work. Always move left to right.

### Blends: two letters, two sounds

A **consonant blend** is two or three consonants side by side where you still hear each sound: *st* in *stop* (/s/ /t/), *bl* in *black*, *cr* in *crab*, *fl* in *flag*. To read a blend, say each sound and push them together.

### Digraphs: two letters, one sound

A **digraph** is two letters that make one new sound:

| Digraph | Sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| sh | /sh/ | *ship*, *fish* |
| ch | /ch/ | *chin*, *lunch* |
| th | /th/ | *thin*, *this* (two slightly different sounds) |
| wh | /w/ in many accents | *when*, *whip* |
| ck | /k/ | *duck*, *back* |

The digraph *ck* usually comes right after a short vowel at the end of a word: *back*, *duck*, *sick*.

### Silent *e*: the CVCe pattern

Add an *e* to the end of some CVC words and the vowel changes from short to **long**. A long vowel says its own name. The *e* itself is silent.

| CVC (short) | CVCe (long) |
|---|---|
| *cap* | *cape* |
| *kit* | *kite* |
| *hop* | *hope* |
| *cut* | *cute* |

Other CVCe words: *cake*, *bike*, *home*, *made*.

### Words to know by sight

Some very common words follow the code: *and*, *in*, *it*, *did*. Others have a part that does not follow the usual sounds, like *of*, *was*, *said*, and *the*. These are practiced until you know them instantly. Even in these words, most letters still give a clue: in *said*, the *s* and *d* make their usual sounds.

> **Adult note:** The National Reading Panel found that systematic phonics instruction, meaning a planned sequence of letter-sound patterns taught explicitly, helps children in kindergarten and first grade learn to read and spell better than instruction with little or no phonics. The Panel also warned that phonics is "a means to an end": children need to use it while reading real sentences and stories, not only in drills.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Sounding out a CVC word

Word: **mat**

1. Look at the first letter, *m*. Say its sound and hold it: /mmm/.
2. Slide to the next letter, *a*, without stopping: /mmmaaa/.
3. Add the last letter, *t*: /mmmaaat/.
4. Say it fast: *mat*.
5. Check: "Is *mat* a real word? Does it make sense?" Yes, like a doormat.

Reasoning: /m/ and /a/ can be stretched, so the sounds stay connected. That is why words starting with *m*, *s*, *f*, *n*, and *l* are good first words to blend.

### Example 2: A blend versus a digraph

Words: **stop** and **shop**

1. *stop* starts with *s-t*. Say each one: /s/ /t/. You hear two sounds. That is a **blend**.
2. Blend the whole word: /s/ /t/ /o/ /p/ → *stop*. Four letters, four sounds.
3. *shop* starts with *s-h*. If you say /s/ /h/ /o/ /p/, you get a word that is not real.
4. Together, *sh* makes one sound: /sh/. That is a **digraph**.
5. Blend: /sh/ /o/ /p/ → *shop*. Four letters, three sounds.

Check: when two consonants are side by side, ask, "Do I hear both sounds, or one new sound?"

### Example 3: Short vowel or silent *e*?

Words: **kit** and **kite**

1. *k-i-t* is CVC. The *i* makes the short sound /i/: *kit*, like a first-aid kit.
2. *k-i-t-e* ends with *e* after one consonant. That is the CVCe pattern.
3. The *e* is silent, and the *i* says its name, /ī/.
4. Blend: /k/ /ī/ /t/ → *kite*.

Reasoning: you have to look at the **whole** word before you choose the vowel sound. If you stop after *k-i-t*, you will read *kit* even when the word is *kite*.

### Example 4: Reading a decodable sentence

Sentence: **The fish is in a big ship.**

1. *The* is a sight word. Read it instantly.
2. *fish*: /f/ /i/ /sh/ → *fish*. The *sh* is a digraph.
3. *is*: /i/ + /z/. The *s* in *is* sounds like /z/. Read it as a sight word for now.
4. *in*: /i/ /n/ → *in*. *a* is a sight word.
5. *big*: /b/ /i/ /g/ → *big*.
6. *ship*: /sh/ /i/ /p/ → *ship*.
7. Reread the whole sentence smoothly, then ask: "Does it make sense?" A fish in a ship is silly, but every word is real and the sentence makes sense as a sentence.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"If I don't know a word, I can guess from the picture or the first letter."** Guessing from *h* that *h-o-m-e* is *house* gives the wrong word. Look at every letter from left to right. The practice guide cited below discourages guessing from first letters or pictures because it stops working as books get harder.
- **"*sh*, *ch*, and *th* are two sounds."** They are digraphs: each pair makes one sound. Reading *ship* as /s/ /h/ /i/ /p/ does not make a real word.
- **"A blend is one sound."** In a blend like *st* or *bl*, you still hear both letters. Saying each sound and then joining them is easier than memorizing *bl* as a new sound.
- **"Silent *e* always makes the vowel long."** It usually does in CVCe words, but some very common words break the pattern: *have*, *give*, *come*, *some*, *love*. Learn these as sight words.
- **"Saying sounds slowly with an *uh* is fine."** Saying /buh/ /a/ /tuh/ makes *buhatuh*, not *bat*. Keep stop sounds short.
- **"*Th* always sounds the same."** The *th* in *thin* and the *th* in *this* are slightly different. Put your hand on your throat: it buzzes for *this*.

> **Adult note:** Letter reversals (*b*/*d*) and blending mistakes are common in kindergarten and first grade. If a child is not making progress after steady practice, talk with their teacher. This lesson is not a test for dyslexia or any reading disability.

## Practice

1. Read these CVC words: *sat*, *pen*, *dig*, *mop*, *bus*.
2. Sort into **blend** or **digraph**: *flag*, *chip*, *stem*, *thin*, *crab*, *duck*.
3. How many sounds are in *fish*? How many letters?
4. Read each pair. Which word has a long vowel? *cap / cape*, *pin / pine*, *not / note*.
5. Add a silent *e* to make a new word: *tub*, *rob*, *tap*.
6. Change one letter to make a new word: *sit* → (change *s* to *h*) → ? ; *ship* → (change *p* to *n*) → ?
7. Read this sentence: *The duck can swim in the pond.* Which words have a blend or a digraph?
8. Spelling: the adult says *chop*, *step*, and *bike*. Write each word.
