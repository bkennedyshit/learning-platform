# 59.6 - Copywriting & Product Writing

**Copywriting** is writing meant to lead a reader to act: sign up, buy, donate, register, read further, or click a button. **Product writing** (often called UX writing or content design) is the text inside a product that helps people use it: button labels, form instructions, empty screens, confirmations, and errors. Both are short, both are judged by what readers do next, and both are arguments compressed into a few words. A landing page is a single argument for a single action; a button label is a promise about what happens when you press it.

This lesson is for college students and professionals who write for products, nonprofits, campaigns, or small businesses. It teaches persuasion that survives scrutiny: specific, honest, and useful. It is educational, not legal advice; advertising law varies by country and by product, so check with your organization's legal or compliance staff before publishing claims.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Translate **features into benefits** that stay accurate.
2. Outline a page or email with **AIDA** or **before-after-bridge** and audit it for overclaiming.
3. Write **microcopy** that says what will happen and works with assistive technology.
4. Apply **truth-in-advertising** principles at an educational level.

## Core ideas

### Features and benefits

A **feature** is what a product has or does (*offline sync*). A **benefit** is what that does for the reader (*your notes are there when the Wi-Fi isn't*). Readers decide on benefits, but a benefit is still a claim. It must follow from the feature and be true for the reader it addresses. A useful test is the **"so what?" ladder**: state the feature, ask "so what?", and stop at the first answer the reader cares about and you can support.

### One page, one argument

The **AIDA** model (**Attention, Interest, Desire, Action**) describes the path a persuasive message takes: earn attention with a headline that names the reader's situation, build interest with specifics, create desire by showing the outcome, and make the action obvious. **Before-after-bridge** is a compact alternative: describe the reader's situation now, describe it after, and present the product or action as the bridge. Both are versions of the argument structure from Lesson 59.3: the claim is "this action is worth taking," the grounds are the specifics, and the warrant is "this fits your situation." A page that asks for three different actions makes three weak arguments instead of one strong one.

### Specific beats boastful

In a 1997 study of web writing, Nielsen Norman Group found that users "detested 'marketese'," the "promotional writing style with boastful subjective claims ('hottest ever')." On one sample site, John Morkes and Jakob Nielsen measured usability 27% higher when the text was written in an objective style instead of a promotional one (and 58% higher for concise text, 47% for scannable text, 124% for all three combined). It is one older study of one site, so treat the numbers as a strong illustration, not a law; but the principle matches the persuasion lesson from argument: credibility suffers "when users clearly see that the site exaggerates."

Specificity is persuasive because it is checkable. That is also why it carries responsibility: a specific claim must be true.

### Truth in advertising (U.S., educational overview)

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's guide for small businesses summarizes three rules under the FTC Act: advertising "must be truthful and non-deceptive"; advertisers "must have evidence to back up their claims"; and ads "cannot be unfair." Before an ad runs, the company must have a "reasonable basis" for its claims, meaning "objective evidence that supports the claim," and "at a minimum, an advertiser must have the level of evidence that it says it has." The FTC's own example: "Two out of three doctors recommend ABC Pain Reliever" must be supported by a reliable survey to that effect. A money-back guarantee "is not a substitute for substantiation."

For reviews and testimonials, the FTC's guidance on its Endorsement Guides states that "endorsements must reflect the honest opinions or experiences of the endorser," and a business cannot condition an incentive (such as a discount) on a review being positive. In August 2024 the FTC announced a final rule that prohibits, among other things, fake or false consumer reviews and testimonials, including reviews by people who do not exist (it names AI-generated fake reviews) or who had no actual experience with the product, and the sale or purchase of fake social-media indicators such as bot-generated followers or views.

For commercial email, the FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide says the subject line "must accurately reflect the content of the message," the message must be identified as an ad, it must include a valid physical postal address, and opt-out requests must be honored within 10 business days.

### Microcopy

Nielsen Norman Group defines **microcontent** as "a type of UX copywriting in the form of short text fragments or phrases, often presented with no additional contextual support," such as page titles, headlines, and email subject lines. Interface microcopy follows the same logic: each fragment must make sense on its own.

- **Buttons.** The Mailchimp Content Style Guide says button copy "should always include verbs," should be clear and concise, and uses sentence case. A label should say what happens: *Create account*, *Send invoice*, *Delete 3 files*, not *Submit* or *OK*.
- **Links.** The W3C's accessibility writing tips say to write link text "so that it describes the content of the link target" and to avoid ambiguous text "such as 'click here' or 'read more'." WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 2.4.4 (Level A) requires that a link's purpose can be determined from its text alone or from the text with its programmatically determined context. Screen-reader users often navigate by a list of links, where *click here* repeated ten times tells them nothing.
- **Empty states.** NN/g's guidance on empty states recommends using them to communicate system status (is it loading, filtered, or truly empty?), to provide learning cues, and to offer a direct path to the key task.
- **Errors.** See Lesson 59.5: say what went wrong and how to fix it, without blaming the user.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Feature-to-benefit ladder

**Illustrative product:** a note-taking app with offline sync.

| Rung | Text | Keep? |
|---|---|---|
| Feature | *Offline sync with conflict resolution.* | Accurate but means little to most readers. |
| So what? | *You can edit notes without an internet connection; changes merge when you reconnect.* | Yes: true and concrete. |
| So what? | *Your field notes are there in the lab basement or on the train.* | Yes, for a researcher or commuter audience: still follows from the feature. |
| So what? | *You'll never lose an idea again.* | No: an absolute claim the feature cannot guarantee (devices fail; people forget to open the app). |

Reasoning: climb the ladder until the benefit matters to the reader, and stop before the claim goes beyond what the feature does. The last rung is where copy turns into a promise you cannot keep.

### Example 2: Auditing a landing page

**Illustrative draft for a tutoring service:**

> **The #1 tutoring service for college students!**
> Thousands of students trust us. Our revolutionary method guarantees better grades. Sign up, follow us on social media, and download our free guide!

| Line | Problem | Revision |
|---|---|---|
| *The #1 tutoring service* | superlative needing substantiation ("number one" by what measure, according to whom?) | *One-on-one tutoring for intro chemistry, calculus, and statistics* |
| *Thousands of students trust us* | vague social proof; must be true and documented if used | If records support it: *Since 2021, 1,240 students have booked at least one session.* (illustrative figure; publish only a number you can document) |
| *revolutionary method guarantees better grades* | outcome claim no service can promise; would need evidence of the level implied | *Sessions focus on working problems from your own course, with a written summary afterward.* |
| three calls to action | competing actions weaken the argument | one button: **Book a free 20-minute session** |

**AIDA outline of the revision:** *Attention:* "Stuck on problem sets in intro chemistry, calculus, or statistics?" *Interest:* what a session covers and who the tutors are. *Desire:* a description of the summary students receive. *Action:* **Book a free 20-minute session**.

Reasoning: each revision replaces a claim that would need proof the writer probably lacks with a specific, verifiable description. The page now makes one argument and asks for one action.

### Example 3: Rewriting microcopy

| Context | Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sign-up form button | *Submit* | *Create account* | names the result; verb-first |
| Delete dialog | *Are you sure? OK / Cancel* | *Delete 3 files? These files will be removed for everyone in the project.* **Delete files** / **Keep files** | states the consequence; buttons say what each does |
| Link in an article | *To read our accessibility policy, click here.* | *Read our accessibility policy.* (with *accessibility policy* as the link text) | link text describes its target (W3C; WCAG 2.4.4) |
| Empty search results | *No results.* | *No projects match "budgt." Check the spelling or clear your filters.* **Clear filters** | communicates status and offers a path |

### Example 4: Writing an email subject line

**Illustrative situation:** a campus bookstore emails students about a 20% discount on used textbooks, valid through Friday.

| Subject line | Assessment |
|---|---|
| *RE: Your order* | Deceptive: there was no order, and the *RE:* implies a reply. CAN-SPAM requires subject lines to reflect the content. |
| *URGENT: Action required on your account* | Deceptive and alarming; no account action is required. |
| *Huge savings!!!* | Not deceptive, but vague and easy to ignore. |
| *20% off used textbooks through Friday* | Accurate, specific, and includes the deadline (kairos) without inventing urgency. |

Reasoning: the best subject line is also the honest one, because it tells the reader exactly what the message offers. Accurate urgency (a real deadline) is legitimate; manufactured urgency is not.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Copywriting is hype."** NN/g's research found readers distrust promotional exaggeration. Strong copy is usually plain, specific, and about the reader.
- **"Specific numbers always help."** Only if they are true and current. The FTC says an advertiser needs "at a minimum ... the level of evidence that it says it has"; a precise number implies precise evidence.
- **"Benefits mean promising outcomes."** A benefit must follow from the feature. Promises of guaranteed results usually go beyond what a product can control.
- **"Testimonials are free marketing."** They must reflect honest experiences; incentives cannot be conditioned on positive reviews, and fake reviews (including AI-generated ones) are prohibited under the FTC's 2024 rule.
- **"*Click here* is clearer for users."** Link text that describes its destination serves everyone, including screen-reader users navigating by links. W3C advises avoiding *click here* and *read more*.
- **"Microcopy is too small to matter."** Each label is a decision about what the user expects to happen. A vague button causes errors and support requests.
- **Boundary: jurisdiction and product type.** This lesson summarizes U.S. FTC guidance for educational purposes. Other countries have their own rules, and some products (health, finance, alcohol, products for children) have additional requirements. It is not legal advice.
- **Boundary: persuasion and manipulation.** Emotional appeals are legitimate when they fit the facts (Lesson 59.3). Fake scarcity, fake deadlines, and interfaces that hide the cancel option risk crossing from persuasion into deception.

## Practice

1. Write a feature-to-benefit ladder (feature + two "so what?" rungs) for each, and mark where you would stop: (a) a water bottle with a double-walled vacuum lining; (b) a calendar app that detects time zones automatically.
2. Identify the problem in each claim and rewrite it so it could be supported: (a) *Loved by everyone who tries it.* (b) *The fastest laptop on the market.* (c) *Doctors recommend our vitamin.* (d) *Join 10,000+ happy members* (the organization has 3,200 members).
3. Rewrite each piece of microcopy: (a) a button labeled *Go* that saves a draft; (b) a link reading *Learn more* that opens a pricing page; (c) an empty inbox that shows only *Nothing here.*
4. Write an accurate subject line for each email: (a) a nonprofit's monthly newsletter featuring a volunteer drive on May 10; (b) a reminder that a free webinar starts in one hour; (c) a store's announcement that it is closing one location.
5. Outline a landing page using before-after-bridge for a campus service you know (for example, a writing center or a bike-repair co-op). Write the headline, one sentence for each part, and a single call-to-action button.
6. **Extended task.** Write a one-screen landing page (150-250 words) for a real or plausible product, service, or campus program. Include a headline, a subhead, three benefit statements tied to features, one call-to-action button, and a footnote listing the evidence you would need for each claim before publishing. Score it with the rubric.

> **Integrity note:** Never invent reviews, testimonials, user counts, ratings, or endorsements, and never write reviews of products you have a connection to without disclosing it. When this lesson uses numbers (such as a student count), they are illustrative. In your own work, publish only claims you can document, and keep the documentation.
