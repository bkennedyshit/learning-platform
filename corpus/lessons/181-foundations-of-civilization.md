# 18.1 - Foundations of Civilization

For most of human history, people lived by hunting and gathering. Then, beginning about twelve thousand years ago, some communities began to farm. Farming made it possible for thousands of people to live in one place, and within a few thousand years the first cities, kings, written records, and law codes appeared along great rivers. This lesson follows that long story from the first farming villages (around 10,000 BCE) to the classical empires of Persia, Greece, Rome, China, and India, which flourished until roughly 600 CE.

The lesson is written for high school students (grades 9–12). It also teaches a historian's skill that matters especially for this period: **separating evidence from inference**. Many early societies left no writing, or writing we cannot read, so much of what we "know" comes from interpreting objects. You will practice saying what an artifact shows, what scholars infer from it, and how confident anyone can be.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Explain what changed in the **Neolithic Revolution**, name at least three regions where farming began independently, and describe the crops domesticated in each.
2. Identify the **shared features of early civilizations** (food surplus, specialization, social stratification, writing, and organized government) with evidence from Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus valley, and Shang China.
3. Compare how **classical states** such as Persia, Athens, Rome, Qin-Han China, and Maurya India organized and justified power.
4. Evaluate archaeological and written evidence by separating **what a source shows** from **what scholars infer** about it.

## Core ideas

### The Neolithic Revolution

The **Neolithic Revolution** is the shift from hunting and gathering to a way of life based mainly on farming and herding. The free textbook *World History* from OpenStax (Rice University) calls it "possibly the most important transformation in the history of modern humans." Two points about it are often misunderstood:

- **It happened more than once.** Farming "occurred independently at different times and in several different areas." Each region domesticated plants that already grew wild there.
- **It was slow and uneven.** Many groups kept hunting and gathering, "either because the new strategy wasn't practicable in their environment or because for them the costs outweighed the benefits."

| Region | Early domesticated plants (OpenStax) |
|---|---|
| Near East (Fertile Crescent) | Wheat and barley; wheat was first domesticated around 10,000 BCE |
| Mesoamerica | Squash, and later maize (corn) |
| China | Millet and rice |
| Sub-Saharan Africa and South America | Other independent centers of domestication |

Farming changed far more than diet. It allowed much larger populations, encouraged the domestication of animals, and, in OpenStax's words, "necessitated the abandonment of hunter-gatherer-style egalitarianism." In other words, once people stored food and owned fields, some families could become richer than others.

The first large permanent settlements appeared in the Fertile Crescent. **Jericho** (8300–6500 BCE), along the Jordan River, and **Çatalhöyük** (7200–6000 BCE), in what is now Turkey, had populations as high as about six thousand.

### What made a "civilization"

Historians use the word **civilization** for a particular kind of society, not as praise. OpenStax describes early civilizations as places where "large numbers of people lived in a relatively small, concentrated area and worked to produce a surplus of food." From that surplus came a set of linked features:

1. **Surplus:** farmers grew more food than their own families needed.
2. **Specialization:** surplus freed some people to work full time as artisans, priests, scribes, soldiers, or rulers.
3. **Exchange:** specialists traded goods and services; a weaver needed a shepherd's wool, and a farmer needed an artisan's tools.
4. **Social stratification:** families who accumulated goods passed wealth and power to their children, producing ranked classes.
5. **Government:** priests and kings gained "authority to command the obedience of subjects," who provided food, goods, and eventually taxes in return for protection.
6. **Writing and records** (in most cases): used first for accounts, contracts, and taxes, and later for laws and literature.

Nomadic and seminomadic peoples did not disappear. They remained "an integral part of the ancient world," moving goods and ideas between cities.

### Four river valley civilizations

| Civilization | River(s) | Key evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sumer (Mesopotamia)** | Tigris and Euphrates | **Cuneiform**, wedge-shaped signs pressed into clay, a full writing system by about 3000 BCE; Uruk may have had as many as fifty thousand people | Writing first served record-keeping: "contracts and receipts as well as taxes and payrolls" |
| **Egypt** | Nile | Upper and Lower Egypt unified about 3150 BCE, possibly by a king named Narmer; the Narmer Palette appears to depict the unification | A single kingdom ruled by a pharaoh along one river corridor |
| **Indus valley (Harappan)** | Indus | Planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro, each likely about thirty thousand people at their height; citywide covered drains; seals with a script "that remains to be deciphered" | Advanced urban planning, but little evidence of how the cities were governed |
| **Shang China** | Yellow River (Huang He) | **Oracle bones**: inscribed shells and bones used in divination, the earliest Chinese written records | Kings served as high priests; religion and ritual were "the backbone of Shang society" |

Notice that the evidence differs. We can read Sumerian and Egyptian writing, so we know rulers' names and laws. We cannot read the Indus script, so the political organization of Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro "remains imperfectly understood." Comparing civilizations always means comparing the evidence, too.

### Law and hierarchy: the Code of Hammurabi

Around the first half of the eighteenth century BCE, **Hammurabi**, king of Babylon, conquered nearly all of Mesopotamia and had his laws inscribed on stone pillars set up across his realm. The most complete surviving copy, a stele found at Susa in modern Iran, shows Hammurabi before the sun-god Shamash. You will read part of the code in Example 2.

### Classical states, about 600 BCE–600 CE

Later states governed far larger territories and developed new ideas about who should rule and why.

| State | How it organized power | How it justified power |
|---|---|---|
| **Achaemenid Persia** (from 550 BCE under Cyrus II) | Darius I divided the empire, which reached from the edge of India to Libya, into twenty provinces (**satrapies**) run by governors (**satraps**); royal inspectors called the "eyes and ears of the great king" audited them; protected roads carried trade | Rule by a royal dynasty over many peoples, with local rulers allowed to manage their own ethnic or religious affairs |
| **Athens** | **Democracy**, "rule by the people": citizens voted in the assembly and served on juries | Participation by citizens; but "women, enslaved people, and foreign residents could not participate" |
| **Rome** | A **republic** from 509 BCE, after Romans expelled their last king; by 27 BCE Octavian (Augustus) held power alone while keeping "a façade" of the Republic | Shared offices and the Senate; later, one ruler who claimed to restore the Republic |
| **Zhou, Qin, and Han China** | The Qin dynasty (221–206 BCE) unified China; the Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) governed it for four centuries | The **Mandate of Heaven**: heaven grants the right to rule to a just dynasty and can withdraw it; disasters and rebellions were read as signs a dynasty was losing it |
| **Maurya India** (322–about 185 BCE) | A large empire with officials and spies; under Ashoka (from about 268 BCE) ministers built hospitals, wells, and rest-houses | After the bloody conquest of Kalinga, which caused an estimated 100,000 deaths, Ashoka promoted Buddhism and "compassionate rule," carved on stone pillars |

### How classical empires ended

Classical states did not all "fall" at once, and historians debate the causes in each case. The Han dynasty ended in 220 CE and China divided for several centuries. In the west, the Roman Empire split into eastern and western halves; the last western emperor was removed in 476 CE, while the eastern (Byzantine) empire continued for almost a thousand more years. Common explanations include costly wars on long frontiers, disputes over succession, economic strain, and pressure from neighboring peoples, but the weight given to each varies by case and by historian.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Evidence and inference at Göbekli Tepe

**The evidence.** Göbekli Tepe, in southeast Turkey near the Syrian border, has large circular and rectangular structures, T-shaped stone pillars, and carvings of boars, snakes, birds, and foxes. According to OpenStax, its earliest structures were built "about 11,600 years ago," while the earliest evidence of agriculture at the site dates to "about one thousand years later."

1. **What the evidence shows:** people built monumental stone structures *before* they farmed at this site.
2. **What it challenges:** scholars had assumed that farming was "a necessary prerequisite" for monumental building. The dates contradict that assumption.
3. **Inference A:** excavator Klaus Schmidt concluded the site was a kind of temple and that "religion emerged first," with agriculture later.
4. **Inference B:** after finding evidence that Göbekli Tepe was a year-round settlement, archaeologist Lee Clare suggested its builders "may have been resisting" agriculture, with carvings that celebrated hunter-gatherer life.
5. **Conclusion:** both scholars accept the same dates; they disagree about meaning. A good answer states the evidence first, then labels each interpretation as an interpretation.

### Example 2: Reading a law code as evidence of social structure

These lines come from the L. W. King translation of the Code of Hammurabi:

> "196. If a man put out the eye of another man, his eye shall be put out. ... 198. If he put out the eye of a freed man, or break the bone of a freed man, he shall pay one gold mina. 199. If he put out the eye of a man's slave, or break the bone of a man's slave, he shall pay one-half of its value."

1. **Observe:** the same injury (a destroyed eye) receives three different penalties.
2. **Compare:** injuring an equal brings the same injury in return; injuring a "freed man" brings a fine; injuring an enslaved person brings payment of half "its value," to the owner.
3. **Infer:** Babylonian law ranked people into classes, and enslaved people were treated partly as property. This matches the feature of **social stratification**.
4. **Limit the claim:** a law code tells us what the king **ordered**, not how often these penalties were applied in real cases. Also, "freed man" is one translator's term for a social category; modern translations use different English words, so check the translation before building an argument on one word.

### Example 3: Building a comparison of two civilizations

**Question:** How were Sumer and the Indus valley civilization similar and different?

1. **Similarities (with evidence):** both arose along rivers; both had large cities (Uruk possibly fifty thousand people; Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro about thirty thousand each); both produced writing (cuneiform; the Indus seals).
2. **Differences (with evidence):** Sumerian records name kings, gods, laws, and taxes. The Indus script cannot be read, and the cities show impressive planning (citywide covered drains) but "little evidence" of how they were ruled.
3. **Claim:** Both civilizations built large planned urban societies on river agriculture, but we know far more about Sumerian government because its writing can be read.
4. **Why this is a strong claim:** it names a similarity and a difference, and it explains the difference partly by the **nature of the evidence**, not only by the societies themselves.

### Example 4: Sourcing a classical speech

Pericles' Funeral Oration is often quoted as a description of Athenian democracy:

> "Its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy."

1. **Who and when:** the Athenian leader Pericles gave the speech after the first battles of the Peloponnesian War (which began in 431 BCE) to honor Athenians who had been killed. It survives because the historian **Thucydides** included it in his history of that war.
2. **Purpose:** a funeral speech in wartime praises the city and encourages citizens. It is persuasive, not neutral.
3. **Corroborate:** OpenStax confirms that Athenian citizens voted and served on juries, so "the many" did take part. It also notes that women, enslaved people, and foreign residents were excluded.
4. **Conclusion:** the speech is good evidence of how Athenian leaders **wanted** their democracy to be seen. It is weaker evidence of how equal Athens actually was.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Farming was invented once and spread from one place."** It began independently in several regions, including the Near East, China, Mesoamerica, South America, and sub-Saharan Africa, each with local crops.
- **"Farming made everyone's life better."** It supported larger populations, but it also produced inequality and harder labor for many people. Some groups chose not to farm because the costs outweighed the benefits.
- **"Civilization means 'more advanced' or 'better.'"** Historians use the term to describe a set of features (cities, surplus, specialization, hierarchy, government). Hunter-gatherer and nomadic peoples built monuments such as Göbekli Tepe and linked cities through trade.
- **"Writing was invented for literature or religion."** In Sumer, the earliest writing mainly recorded contracts, receipts, taxes, and payrolls.
- **"Athens was a democracy like modern ones."** Only adult male citizens participated. Women, enslaved people, and foreign residents were excluded.
- **"The Code of Hammurabi shows how justice actually worked."** It shows the law the king proclaimed. Evidence of actual cases is needed to know how laws were applied.
- **Boundary: dates and numbers are approximate.** Dates such as "about 3150 BCE" and populations such as "about thirty thousand" come from archaeology and are revised as new evidence appears. Say "about" and name your source.
- **Boundary: Rome did not fall in a single day.** The western empire's last emperor was removed in 476 CE, but the eastern empire continued for almost a thousand years.

## Practice

2. Put the six features of early civilizations in a cause-and-effect chain, starting with "surplus." Explain one link in the chain in your own words.
3. Classify each piece of evidence by civilization (Sumer, Egypt, Indus valley, or Shang China): (a) oracle bones; (b) the Narmer Palette; (c) clay tablets recording payrolls; (d) seals with an undeciphered script; (e) citywide covered drains.
