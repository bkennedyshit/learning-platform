# 38.6 — Research Writing & Academic Style

> *"Research is formalized curiosity. It is poking and prying with a purpose."* — Zora Neale Hurston

Research writing is argument with receipts. The skills from chapters 38.4 and 38.5 — thesis, paragraphs, rhetoric — still apply, but now every claim must be anchored to evidence you can point to, and that evidence must be evaluated, cited, and synthesized, not just collected. This chapter covers the mechanics and the ethics.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Find, evaluate, and select credible sources using the CRAAP and SIFT frameworks.
2. Distinguish between primary, secondary, and tertiary sources.
3. Paraphrase, summarize, and quote sources correctly.
4. Format in-text citations and works-cited/reference entries in MLA and APA.
5. Build an annotated bibliography.
6. Structure a full research essay: literature synthesis, argument, evidence integration.
7. Define plagiarism precisely and understand how to avoid all five types.

## 📚 1. Finding and Evaluating Sources

### 1.1 Source Types

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Primary** | Original, firsthand material | Original research studies, raw data, interviews, original literary works, court documents, letters |
| **Secondary** | Analysis or interpretation of primary sources | Academic journal articles (reviews, meta-analyses), textbook chapters, biographies |
| **Tertiary** | Compiled or synthesized from secondary sources | Encyclopedias (including Wikipedia), databases, bibliographies |

> **Wikipedia rule:** Wikipedia is a tertiary source — excellent for orientation and finding primary/secondary sources, but not citable itself in academic work. Use it to find the sources it cites.

### 1.2 CRAAP Test (Source Evaluation)

| Letter | Criterion | Questions to ask |
|--------|----------|-----------------|
| **C** | Currency | When was it published? Has it been updated? Is currency important for this topic? |
| **R** | Relevance | Does it directly address your research question? Is the scope and depth appropriate? |
| **A** | Authority | Who is the author? What are their credentials? What institution/publication? Is it peer-reviewed? |
| **A** | Accuracy | Is it based on evidence? Are sources cited? Can the data be verified? Are there obvious errors? |
| **P** | Purpose | Why was this written? To inform, persuade, sell, entertain? Is there bias? Who funded the research? |

### 1.3 SIFT Method (for online sources)

| Letter | Action | Meaning |
|--------|--------|---------|
| **S** | Stop | Pause before sharing or citing. Check your reaction — are you being manipulated? |
| **I** | Investigate the source | Who produced this? What is their reputation and mission? |
| **F** | Find better coverage | Look for corroborating sources or expert analysis of the claim |
| **T** | Trace claims | Go upstream — find the original study, data, or document behind the claim |

### 1.4 Where to Find Credible Sources

| Source | Useful for |
|--------|-----------|
| **[Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/)** | Peer-reviewed academic articles; see citations and find full text |
| **[JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/)** | Humanities and social science journals (many free) |
| **[PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)** | Biomedical and life science literature |
| **[ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/)** | Education research |
| **[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/)** | CS, physics, math preprints (not peer-reviewed) |
| **Library databases** | Your local or university library grants access to ProQuest, EBSCOhost, etc. |
| **[WorldCat](https://www.worldcat.org/)** | Find books at your local library |
| **[Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/)** | Free full text of public domain literature |

## 📚 2. Working with Sources

### 2.1 Direct Quotation

Copy the exact words from the source, enclosed in quotation marks.

**When to quote directly:**
- The exact wording is important (legal documents, literary analysis)
- The source's language is particularly striking or authoritative
- The precise phrasing matters for the argument

**When NOT to quote:**
- The idea is what matters, not the phrasing
- The quotation would be too long to serve the essay efficiently
- You can express it more clearly in your own words

### 2.2 Paraphrase

Restate the source's idea in your own words and sentence structure, similar in length to the original.

**Key rule:** A paraphrase is not a word-swap. Change the syntax and vocabulary substantially. If you retain three or more consecutive words from the original, use quotation marks.

**Original:** *"The greatest challenge in distributed systems is not writing code that works under normal conditions, but writing code that works correctly when things go wrong — when networks fail, nodes crash, and messages arrive out of order."*

**Bad paraphrase (too close):** *The greatest challenge in distributed systems is not code that functions normally but code that works correctly when things fail — when networks break down, nodes crash, and messages arrive out of order.*

**Good paraphrase:** *In distributed systems, the real engineering difficulty is not building functionality for ideal conditions, but ensuring correctness under the range of realistic failures: dropped connections, crashed processes, and non-deterministic message delivery.*

### 2.3 Summary

A condensed version of the source's main point(s), much shorter than the original. Useful for giving background or surveying a field.

## 📚 3. Citation Formats

### 3.1 MLA (Modern Language Association)

Used in: humanities (literature, arts, philosophy, history)

**In-text citation format:** (Author Last Name Page#)
- *The use of darkness as a symbol intensifies in the play's third act (Bradley 247).*
- No "p." before page number. No comma between name and page number.

**Works Cited entry formats:**

**Book:**
> Last, First. *Title of Book*. Publisher, Year.
> Kennedy, Bill. *Systems Thinking for Engineers*. O'Reilly, 2026.

**Journal article (print):**
> Last, First. "Title of Article." *Journal Title*, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. #–#.

**Website:**
> Last, First (if available). "Title of Page." *Website Name*, Day Month Year, URL.

**Key MLA mechanics:**
- Title of longer work: *italicized*
- Title of shorter work (article, poem, chapter): "in quotes"
- Works Cited page: alphabetical by author last name; hanging indent.

### 3.2 APA (American Psychological Association)

Used in: social sciences, education, psychology, nursing, business

**In-text citation format:** (Author, Year) or (Author, Year, p. #) for direct quotes
- *Remote work increases productivity under controlled conditions (Smith & Jones, 2024).*
- *Smith and Jones (2024) found that remote work increases productivity.*
- Direct quote: *(Smith & Jones, 2024, p. 47)*

**References entry formats:**

**Book:**
> Last, F. M. (Year). *Title of book: Subtitle*. Publisher.
> Kennedy, B. (2026). *Systems thinking for engineers*. O'Reilly.

**Journal article:**
> Last, F. M., & Last, F. M. (Year). Title of article. *Journal Title*, *Volume*(Issue), page–page. https://doi.org/xxxxx

**Website:**
> Last, F. M. (Year, Month Day). Title of page. *Website Name*. URL

**Key APA mechanics:**
- Book and journal titles: *italicized*; only first word of title capitalized (and after colon, and proper nouns)
- Journal names: fully capitalized, italicized
- References page: alphabetical; hanging indent.

### 3.3 Chicago (Brief Overview)

Used in: history, some humanities, publishing. Two systems:
- **Notes-Bibliography** (humanities): superscript footnotes with full citations; bibliography at end.
- **Author-Date** (sciences/social sciences): similar to APA in-text.

For full Chicago: consult the [Purdue OWL Chicago guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/chicago_manual_17th_edition/index.html).

## 📚 4. Plagiarism — Five Types

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's work or ideas as your own. It has five forms:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Direct (copy-paste)** | Reproducing exact text without quotation marks and citation | Pasting a paragraph from a source with no attribution |
| **Mosaic (patchwriting)** | Replacing some words from the original but keeping the structure and most phrasing | Word-swapping without genuine paraphrase |
| **Paraphrase without attribution** | Restating an idea in your own words but not citing the source | Good paraphrase, no citation |
| **Idea theft** | Using someone's original argument, analysis, or interpretation without credit | Claiming an analytical insight as your own |
| **Self-plagiarism** | Submitting your own previous work as new without disclosure | Reusing an essay for a different course without permission |

**The citation rule:** If the idea, data, argument, or specific information came from a source — cite it. The only exception is **common knowledge** (facts that appear in 5+ sources without attribution and are not contested, e.g., "Shakespeare was born in 1564").

## 📚 5. The Research Essay — Structure

A full research essay adds **source integration and synthesis** to the basic essay structure from chapter 19.4.

### Structure

1. **Introduction**
   - Context/background (drawing on sources if needed)
   - The research question or issue
   - Thesis: your answer to the question

2. **Literature synthesis** (for longer essays)
   - Survey of existing knowledge and debate on the topic
   - "They say" — what scholars/experts have argued
   - Identifies the gap your argument fills

3. **Body paragraphs** (argument + evidence)
   - Topic sentence (your point)
   - Evidence from sources (quoted, paraphrased, or summarized — cited)
   - Analysis: how the evidence supports your point
   - Transition

4. **Counterargument paragraph**
   - Acknowledge the strongest objection
   - Provide evidence for it if relevant
   - Rebut

5. **Conclusion**
   - Synthesize; restate thesis in light of evidence assembled
   - Broader implications or further questions

## 📚 6. The Annotated Bibliography

An annotated bibliography is a list of sources, each followed by a brief note (annotation) that describes and evaluates the source.

**Annotation components:**
1. **Summary** — what the source argues or covers (2–3 sentences)
2. **Evaluation** — credibility, methodology, relevance (1–2 sentences)
3. **Reflection** — how you plan to use it in your essay (1–2 sentences)

**Example (APA format):**

> Smith, J., & Jones, A. (2024). Remote work and knowledge worker productivity. *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, *45*(3), 112–130. https://doi.org/10.xxxx

> *This peer-reviewed study analyzes productivity data from 500 knowledge workers across 12 companies before and during remote work transitions. The authors use objective output metrics (code commits, task completion rates) rather than self-report surveys, which strengthens the reliability of findings. I will use this as primary evidence for my thesis that remote work increases measurable engineering output.*

## ✍️ 7. Worked Example — Source Integration

**Three ways to use the same source:**

Source: *"Code review, when practiced consistently, reduces defect density by an average of 60% compared to testing alone" (McConnell, 2004, p. 480).*

**1. Direct quotation:**
> McConnell (2004) found that "code review, when practiced consistently, reduces defect density by an average of 60% compared to testing alone" (p. 480).

**2. Paraphrase:**
> Consistent code review practices, according to McConnell (2004), cut defect rates by approximately 60% beyond what testing alone achieves.

**3. Summary (in context of broader argument):**
> Multiple studies confirm that peer review processes substantially reduce software defects beyond what automated testing alone catches, with estimates ranging from 60% to 80% reductions in defect density (McConnell, 2004; Fagan, 1986).

## 🧮 8. Hands-On Lab

**Research essay:** On any topic from your vault (game dev, AI, neuroscience, language learning — your choice), write a 750–1000 word research essay. Requirements:
- Minimum 3 credible sources, at least 2 peer-reviewed or expert-authored
- Full citation in either MLA or APA format
- In-text citations for every quotation, paraphrase, and summary
- Works Cited or References page

**CRAAP audit:** For each source you find, run it through the full CRAAP test and score it. Drop any source that fails on Authority or Accuracy.
