# APP-04.7 — Chemical Reactions

When wood burns, iron rusts, or bread bakes, new substances form that were not there before. That is a **chemical reaction**. In every reaction the atoms that were present at the start are still present at the end. They have only been rearranged into new combinations. This lesson shows how to tell a chemical change from a physical one, how to read and balance a chemical equation, and how to recognize five common types of reactions.

The course plan places this lesson in grade 8.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Sort changes into physical and chemical and name the evidence you used.
2. Identify reactants and products and explain conservation of mass with atoms.
3. Balance equations by changing coefficients and check them with an atom table.
4. Classify a reaction into one of five types.

## Core ideas

### Physical change versus chemical change

- A **physical change** alters the form or state of a substance but not its identity. Melting ice, dissolving sugar, and cutting paper are physical changes.
- A **chemical change** (chemical reaction) produces one or more **new substances** with new properties. Burning, rusting, and cooking an egg are chemical changes.

Common **evidence** that a reaction is happening:

1. A color change that is not just mixing two colors.
2. A gas forms (bubbles or a smell) when nothing is being boiled.
3. A **precipitate**, a solid, forms when two liquids are mixed.
4. Energy is released or taken in: the mixture gets warmer or colder, or gives off light.

These are clues, not proof. Boiling water makes bubbles, but it is a physical change. The real test is whether a new substance formed.

### Reading a chemical equation

$$\text{reactants} \rightarrow \text{products}$$

The **reactants** are the starting substances (left side); the **products** are the new substances (right side). The arrow means "react to form." A plus sign separates substances on the same side.

- A **subscript** is part of a formula: $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ always means 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
- A **coefficient** is a number written in front of a formula: $2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ means two water molecules, which together contain 4 H and 2 O.

### Conservation of mass

Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction; they are rearranged. So the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. This is the **law of conservation of mass**. It only appears to fail when something escapes or enters, such as a gas bubbling out of an open cup.

### Balancing equations by inspection

A **balanced** equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. To balance one:

1. Write the correct formulas for every reactant and product.
2. Count atoms of each element on each side, in a table.
3. Add **coefficients** to make the counts equal. **Never change subscripts**: that would change which substance it is.
4. It often helps to balance elements that appear in only one formula on each side first, and to leave single elements (like $\text{O}_2$) for last.
5. Recount every element, and use the smallest whole-number coefficients.

### Five common types of reactions

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Synthesis (combination) | $\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{AB}$ | $4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ |
| Decomposition | $\text{AB} \rightarrow \text{A} + \text{B}$ | $2\text{HgO} \rightarrow 2\text{Hg} + \text{O}_2$ |
| Single replacement | $\text{A} + \text{BC} \rightarrow \text{AC} + \text{B}$ | $\text{Zn} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$ |
| Double replacement | $\text{AB} + \text{CD} \rightarrow \text{AD} + \text{CB}$ | $\text{AgNO}_3 + \text{NaCl} \rightarrow \text{AgCl} + \text{NaNO}_3$ |
| Combustion | fuel $+ \text{O}_2 \rightarrow$ products, releasing heat and light | $\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |

In the double-replacement example, silver chloride (AgCl) does not dissolve, so it forms as a solid precipitate. These examples are for reading and classifying, not for doing; several of these substances are hazardous.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Balancing the formation of water

Unbalanced: $\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$

1. Count: left has 2 H and 2 O; right has 2 H and 1 O. Oxygen is unbalanced.
2. Put a 2 in front of water: $\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Now the right has 4 H and 2 O.
3. Hydrogen is now unbalanced (2 on the left, 4 on the right). Put a 2 in front of $\text{H}_2$.
4. Result: $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
5. Check: H: 2 × 2 = 4 left, 2 × 2 = 4 right. O: 2 left, 2 × 1 = 2 right. ✓
6. Wrong way: changing $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ to $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ also "balances" oxygen, but $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance.

### Example 2: Balancing methane combustion

Unbalanced: $\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

1. Carbon: 1 left, 1 right. Balanced already.
2. Hydrogen: 4 left, 2 right. Put a 2 in front of water: $\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
3. Oxygen (last, since $\text{O}_2$ is a single element): right side has 2 (in $\text{CO}_2$) + 2 (in $2\text{H}_2\text{O}$) = 4. Left needs 4, so use $2\text{O}_2$.
4. Result: $\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

| Element | Reactants | Products | Balanced? |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 1 | 1 | yes |
| H | 4 | 2 × 2 = 4 | yes |
| O | 2 × 2 = 4 | 2 + 2 = 4 | yes |

### Example 3: Conservation of mass in open and closed containers

*Prediction, not a recorded result.* Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) reacts with vinegar (acetic acid) to make sodium acetate, water, and carbon dioxide gas:

$$\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$$

1. Atom check: Na 1 → 1; H 1 + 4 = 5 → 3 + 2 = 5; C 1 + 2 = 3 → 2 + 1 = 3; O 3 + 2 = 5 → 2 + 1 + 2 = 5. Balanced as written.
2. **Open cup on a scale:** the $\text{CO}_2$ bubbles out into the air, so conservation of mass predicts the scale reading will **drop** by the mass of the escaped gas.
3. **Sealed zip bag on a scale:** the gas is trapped, so the prediction is that the reading stays the same, within the scale's precision.
4. Conclusion: the "missing" mass in the open cup did not disappear. It left as a gas. Mass is conserved when you count everything.

### Example 4: Mass bookkeeping

*Illustrative example using rounded atomic masses.* When magnesium burns ($2\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{MgO}$), 2.4 g of magnesium combines with 1.6 g of oxygen from the air.

1. Total mass of reactants = 2.4 g + 1.6 g = 4.0 g.
2. Conservation of mass: products = 4.0 g of magnesium oxide.
3. Why the solid seems to "gain weight": the ash (4.0 g) is heavier than the metal (2.4 g) because it now includes oxygen atoms taken from the air.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Burning destroys matter."** The atoms leave as gases (such as carbon dioxide and water vapor) or stay as ash. If you could trap all the products, their mass would equal the mass of the fuel plus the oxygen used.
- **"Balance by changing subscripts."** Subscripts define the substance. Only coefficients may change.
- **"Every color change or bubble means a reaction."** Mixing paints, boiling water, and dissolving a drink powder are physical changes. Look for a new substance.
- **"Dissolving is a chemical reaction."** Dissolving sugar is a physical change; evaporating the water gets the sugar back unchanged.
- **"The coefficients tell the mass of each substance."** Coefficients count particles (molecules or atoms), not grams. In $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$, the oxygen has more mass than the hydrogen even though its coefficient is smaller.
- **"Mass is conserved, so the scale never changes."** It can change in an open container when a gas escapes or when a gas from the air (like oxygen in rusting) is added.

## Practice

1. Physical or chemical change? (a) toasting bread (b) freezing juice (c) a nail rusting (d) dissolving salt in water (e) a candle's wick burning
2. Balance: (a) $\text{N}_2 + \text{H}_2 \rightarrow \text{NH}_3$ (b) $\text{Fe} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ (c) $\text{KClO}_3 \rightarrow \text{KCl} + \text{O}_2$
3. Balance the combustion of propane: $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$
4. Classify each reaction: (a) $2\text{Na} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl}$ (b) $2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$ (c) $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$ (d) $\text{Pb(NO}_3\text{)}_2 + 2\text{KI} \rightarrow \text{PbI}_2 + 2\text{KNO}_3$
5. *Illustrative example:* In a sealed flask, 12 g of reactants react completely. What is the total mass of the products?
6. A student balances $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{HCl}$ by writing $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{Cl}_2$. What is wrong, and what is the correct balanced equation?
7. When baking soda and vinegar are mixed, what evidence suggests a chemical reaction? Why is that evidence alone not proof?
