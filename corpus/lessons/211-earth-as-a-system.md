# 21.1 - Earth as a System

A **system** is a set of parts that interact so that a change in one part affects the others. Earth scientists study the planet this way: rock, water, air, and living things are connected by flows of **energy** and **matter**. A volcanic eruption is not only a "rock event"; it also changes the air, can change the weather, and affects living things. This lesson builds the vocabulary used throughout the course: the four spheres, Earth's layers, the planet's energy sources, matter cycles, and feedback loops.

The course plan describes this track as middle-school Earth science that also lays a foundation for high-school environmental science.

## Core ideas

### Systems vocabulary

- **Reservoir** (or store): a place where matter is held, such as the ocean, a glacier, or a layer of limestone.
- **Flux** (or flow): the movement of matter or energy between reservoirs, such as rain falling from the atmosphere to the land.
- **Input** and **output**: what enters and leaves a system. For energy, Earth is an **open** system: sunlight comes in and heat radiates out to space. For matter, Earth is very nearly **closed**: almost all of its atoms stay on the planet and are recycled from one reservoir to another.

### The four spheres

| Sphere | What it includes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Geosphere** | Solid Earth: rock, sediment, soil minerals, and the interior | Granite, sand, magma, the core |
| **Hydrosphere** | All water: oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, ice, water vapor | A glacier, an aquifer, a cloud droplet |
| **Atmosphere** | The layer of gases around Earth | Nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, wind |
| **Biosphere** | All living things and recently dead organic matter | Trees, plankton, bacteria, people |

Some books split frozen water into a fifth sphere, the **cryosphere**. The spheres overlap: soil contains minerals (geosphere), water (hydrosphere), air (atmosphere), and organisms (biosphere) at the same time.

### Earth's layers

The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) describes three main layers: **crust**, **mantle**, and **core**. Earth is about 12,750 km in diameter, so its radius is about 6,375 km.

| Layer | Approximate thickness | State and composition |
|---|---|---|
| Oceanic crust | about 5 km | Rigid, brittle rock |
| Continental crust | about 30 km on average, up to about 100 km under large mountain ranges | Rigid, brittle rock |
| Mantle | about 2,900 km | Hot, dense, **semi-solid** rock |
| Outer core | about 2,200 km | **Liquid** iron-nickel alloy; its motion creates Earth's magnetic field |
| Inner core | about 1,250 km | **Solid** iron-nickel alloy |

Two more terms describe how the outer layers behave. The **lithosphere** is the crust plus the cool, rigid top of the mantle; it averages at least 80 km thick and is broken into tectonic plates. Below it, the **asthenosphere** is a zone of hot, semi-solid mantle rock that can slowly soften and flow over geologic time. Lesson 21.2 shows how the plates move on it.

### Energy inputs

Earth has two main energy sources:

1. **Sunlight.** NASA describes Earth's climate as "a solar powered system." Of the roughly 340 watts per square meter (W/m²) of sunlight arriving at the top of the atmosphere (averaged over the globe and the year), about **29%** is reflected back to space by clouds, particles, and bright surfaces such as snow and ice, about **23%** is absorbed by the atmosphere, and about **48%** is absorbed by the surface. Absorbed sunlight drives evaporation, winds, ocean currents, and photosynthesis.
2. **Internal heat.** Heat from Earth's interior keeps the mantle moving, which drives plate motion, volcanoes, mountain building, and the deep parts of the rock cycle.

### Matter cycles

- **Water cycle.** Water moves between the atmosphere and the surface by evaporation, evapotranspiration (evaporation plus water released by plants), and precipitation. On land it moves as snowmelt, runoff, and streamflow, and it moves into the ground by infiltration and groundwater recharge. Lesson 21.4 covers this cycle in detail.
- **Carbon cycle.** NASA separates a **fast** carbon cycle, which moves carbon through living things on timescales of a lifespan (photosynthesis takes carbon dioxide out of the air; respiration, decay, and fire return it), from a **slow** carbon cycle, in which carbon takes 100–200 million years to move between rocks, soil, ocean, and atmosphere. Most of Earth's carbon, about 65,500 billion metric tons, is stored in rocks. Volcanoes return rock carbon to the air. Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from the slow cycle to the fast cycle quickly (lesson 21.8).
- **Rock cycle.** Rock is slowly changed from one type to another. The rock cycle is driven by Earth's internal heat and by the water cycle, which is powered by the Sun (lesson 21.3).
- The **nitrogen** and **phosphorus** cycles move nutrients through soil, water, and organisms; they are treated in [02.5 — Ecology & Ecosystems](/025-ecology-ecosystems).

### Feedback loops

A **feedback** happens when the result of a change loops back and affects the original change.

- A **positive feedback** *amplifies* the change. "Positive" does not mean "good."
- A **negative feedback** *reduces* the change and tends to stabilize the system.

**Sea ice and sunlight (positive).** The National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC) reports that sea ice reflects 50–70% of incoming solar energy, while the darker open ocean reflects only about 6%. When warming melts sea ice, more dark ocean is exposed, more sunlight is absorbed, and the water warms further, which melts more ice.

**Rock weathering and carbon dioxide (negative).** NASA describes how, if carbon dioxide rises (for example, from more volcanic activity), temperatures rise, leading to more rain, which dissolves more rock. The dissolved ions eventually deposit more carbon on the ocean floor, lowering carbon dioxide again. This stabilizing loop is very slow: it takes a few hundred thousand years.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Splitting the incoming sunlight

Using NASA's averages, how many W/m² of the 340 W/m² of incoming sunlight are reflected, absorbed by the atmosphere, and absorbed by the surface?

1. Reflected: $0.29 \times 340 = 98.6\ \text{W/m}^2$.
2. Absorbed by the atmosphere: $0.23 \times 340 = 78.2\ \text{W/m}^2$.
3. Absorbed by the surface: $0.48 \times 340 = 163.2\ \text{W/m}^2$.
4. Check the parts: $98.6 + 78.2 + 163.2 = 340$. ✓
5. Total absorbed by the Earth system: $78.2 + 163.2 = 241.4\ \text{W/m}^2$, about 71% of the incoming sunlight. NASA states that the Earth system absorbs an average of about 240 W/m², so the calculation agrees.

### Example 2: How thin is the crust?

Use the USGS layer thicknesses to check that the layers add up to Earth's radius, and find what fraction of the radius is continental crust.

1. Radius: $12{,}750 \div 2 = 6{,}375\ \text{km}$.
2. Add the layers using average continental crust: $30 + 2{,}900 + 2{,}200 + 1{,}250 = 6{,}380\ \text{km}$. This is within rounding of 6,375 km, so the numbers are consistent.
3. Fraction that is crust: $\dfrac{30}{6{,}375} \approx 0.0047$, or about **0.5%** of the radius.
4. Interpretation: if Earth were shrunk to a ball with a 1-meter radius, the average continental crust would be about 5 mm thick (0.47% of 1,000 mm). USGS describes the crust as "only skin deep."

### Example 3: One eruption, four spheres

*Illustrative example.* A large volcano erupts. Trace the interactions.

1. **Geosphere → atmosphere:** magma reaches the surface and releases gases and ash into the air. NASA lists large eruptions that send light-reflecting particles as high as the stratosphere as a natural climate influence.
2. **Atmosphere → hydrosphere and geosphere:** ash falls on land and water; rain can wash it into rivers.
3. **Geosphere → biosphere:** ash can bury plants nearby; over time, weathered volcanic rock forms new soil that plants colonize.
4. **Geosphere → atmosphere (carbon):** volcanic carbon dioxide is one way the slow carbon cycle returns carbon from rock to the air.
5. Conclusion: one event connects all four spheres, which is why a systems view is useful.

### Example 4: Classifying a feedback

Is this loop positive or negative? *Warmer air → more water evaporates → more water vapor (a heat-trapping gas) → warmer air.*

1. Start with the change: the air warms.
2. Follow the loop: warming causes more evaporation; more water vapor traps more heat.
3. The final effect is in the **same** direction as the starting change (more warming).
4. Therefore it is a **positive** (amplifying) feedback.
5. Check with the weathering loop above: more CO₂ → warmer → more rain and weathering → **less** CO₂. The ending change is opposite to the starting change, so that loop is **negative**.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The mantle is an ocean of liquid magma."** USGS describes the mantle as hot, semi-solid rock. The large liquid layer is the **outer core**. Magma forms only in limited places where rock partly melts.
- **"Each sphere is separate."** Most important processes cross spheres. A cloud is hydrosphere water floating in the atmosphere; soil belongs to all four spheres.
- **"Positive feedback means a good outcome."** In science, positive means *amplifying* and negative means *stabilizing*. The sea-ice loop is positive and contributes to warming.
- **"Most carbon is in the air and in living things."** NASA reports that most of Earth's carbon is stored in rocks; the atmosphere, ocean, plants, soil, and fossil fuels hold the rest.
- **"Earth's internal heat warms our weather."** Internal heat drives plate tectonics and volcanoes, but the energy that powers weather and climate is overwhelmingly sunlight.
- **Boundary:** Earth is "closed" for matter only approximately. A small amount of material arrives as meteorites and some light gases escape to space, but these flows are tiny compared with the flows inside the cycles.
