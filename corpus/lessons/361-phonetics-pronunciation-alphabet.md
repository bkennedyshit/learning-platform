# 36.1 — Phonetics, Pronunciation & Alphabet

> *"Spanish is a language that rolls off the tongue — once you stop trying to roll it the way English does."*

Pronunciation is the first fork in the road. Learn it right early and it compounds; learn it wrong and you're unlearning bad habits for years. Spanish phonetics are dramatically more regular than English — spelling is nearly perfectly phonetic. Once you know the rules, you can pronounce any word you've never seen.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Produce all five Spanish vowels with pure, consistent quality.
2. Pronounce the letters unique to Spanish: *ñ*, *rr*, *j*, *ll*, *h*, *v/b*, *c/z*, *g/j*.
3. Apply the three Spanish stress rules and use written accent marks correctly.
4. Distinguish the major pronunciation differences between Castilian and Latin American Spanish.
5. Use the IPA symbols for Spanish phonemes as a reference tool.

---

## 📚 1. The Spanish Alphabet

Spanish uses the Latin alphabet (27 letters — the standard 26 plus **ñ**). The *ch* and *ll* were once treated as single letters but are now alphabetized separately.

| Letter | Name | Sound notes |
|--------|------|------------|
| A a | *a* | Always like "ah" — never like English "ay" or "uh" |
| B b | *be* | Same as V — bilabial fricative between vowels; plosive at start |
| C c | *ce* | Before *e/i*: "s" (Latin America) or "th" (Castilian); before *a/o/u*: "k" |
| D d | *de* | Plosive at start; dental fricative (like "th" in *the*) between vowels |
| E e | *e* | Always like "eh" — never like English "ee" or silent |
| F f | *efe* | Same as English |
| G g | *ge* | Before *e/i*: like English "h" (guttural); before *a/o/u*: like English "g" |
| H h | *hache* | **Always silent** — *hora* = "ora", *hacer* = "acer" |
| I i | *i* | Always like "ee" — short and pure |
| J j | *jota* | Guttural "h" — like clearing throat (not English "j") |
| K k | *ka* | Same as English; only in foreign words |
| L l | *ele* | Similar to English |
| M m | *eme* | Same as English |
| N n | *ene* | Same as English |
| Ñ ñ | *eñe* | "ny" sound — like "canyon" → *cañón*, *mañana*, *año* |
| O o | *o* | Always like "oh" — pure, never diphthong-like English "oh-oo" |
| P p | *pe* | Like English but **no aspiration** — no puff of air (feel the difference: English "pin" vs. Spanish *pin*) |
| Q q | *cu* | Always followed by *ue* or *ui*; the *u* is silent; "k" sound |
| R r | *erre* | Tap (single flap of tongue) between vowels; trilled at start of word/after n,l,s |
| RR rr | *erre doble* | Trill (multiple rapid flaps) — *carro*, *perro*, *arroz* |
| S s | *ese* | Like English "s" — never "z" sound in standard Spanish |
| T t | *te* | Like English but **no aspiration** and dental (tongue on teeth, not ridge) |
| U u | *u* | Always like "oo" — pure. Silent after *q* and after *g* before *e/i* |
| V v | *uve* | **Identical to B** in pronunciation — bilabial |
| W w | *doble uve* | Only in foreign words |
| X x | *equis* | Usually "ks" (*éxito*); in some place names "h" (*México* = "Méjico") |
| Y y | *ye* (or *i griega*) | Consonant: like English "y"; as vowel (meaning "and"): like "ee" |
| Z z | *zeta* | Latin America: "s"; Castilian: "th" (as in *think*) |

## 📚 2. The Five Pure Spanish Vowels

This is the most important thing in this chapter. **Spanish has exactly 5 vowel sounds. English has ~15.** Every Spanish vowel is:

- **Pure** — it does not glide into another sound (no diphthong in the vowel itself)
- **Short** — roughly the same length regardless of position
- **Consistent** — spelled the same way always

| Vowel | IPA | Description | English approximation | Bad English habit |
|-------|-----|-------------|----------------------|------------------|
| A | /a/ | Low open, central | "ah" in "father" | Saying "ay" (*cama* ≠ "kay-muh") |
| E | /e/ | Mid-front | "e" in "bet" (but more pure) | Saying "ay" or "ee" (*mesa* ≠ "MAY-suh") |
| I | /i/ | High front | "ee" in "see" (short) | Reducing to "ih" (*sí* ≠ "sih") |
| O | /o/ | Mid back | "o" in "go" (but no glide to "oo") | Diphthonging (*foto* ≠ "FOH-toh-oo") |
| U | /u/ | High back | "oo" in "moon" (short) | Reducing to "uh" (*mucho* ≠ "MOO-chuh") |

> **Drill:** Say "a e i o u" 20 times per day for the first week. Record yourself. Compare to a native speaker recording of the same. The vowels must be identical each time — no variation.

### Diphthongs (two vowels together)

When two vowels combine in the same syllable, one or both may be "weak" (i or u):

| Combination | Example | Pronunciation |
|------------|---------|--------------|
| ai/ay | *aire*, *hay* | "eye" |
| ei/ey | *reina*, *ley* | "ay" |
| oi/oy | *oigo*, *hoy* | "oy" |
| au | *auto* | "ow" |
| eu | *Europa* | "eh-oo" |
| ia | *hacia* | "yah" |
| ie | *bien*, *tierra* | "yeh" |
| io | *violín* | "yo" |
| ua | *agua*, *cuatro* | "wah" |
| ue | *bueno*, *puedo* | "weh" |
| ui | *cuidado* | "wee" |

> When a written accent mark appears on *í* or *ú* in a potential diphthong, it breaks the diphthong into two separate syllables: *día* (DI-a, 2 syllables) vs. *dia-* as part of *diario* (diphthong, 1 syllable).

## 📚 3. The Tricky Letters — R, RR, J, LL, Ñ

### The R — Tap and Trill

- **Single R between vowels:** a single tap of the tongue tip against the alveolar ridge (same position as English "d" or "t") — *caro* (expensive), *pero* (but)
- **Single R at start of word, after L/N/S:** full trill — *rojo*, *enredo*, *Israel*
- **RR always:** full trill — *carro*, *perro*, *correo*

> **The trill:** relax the tongue. Practice by saying "better butter" very fast — the flap in American English "t" between vowels is close to the Spanish single *r*. The trill is rapid repeated flaps. Practice with *brrr* (cold sound) until the air gets under the tongue.

### The J and G (before e/i) — The Jota

The *j* (and *g* before *e/i*) is a guttural fricative — produced in the back of the throat, like an exaggerated English "h" or the Scottish "loch." It is **not** the English "j" sound.

*jefe* (KHEH-feh), *gente* (KHEHN-teh), *joven* (KHO-ben), *hijo* (EE-khoh)

### The LL — Yeísmo

Historically *ll* was a palatal lateral (like "lli" in "million"). In most of Latin America and much of Spain today, *ll* and *y* are identical: both pronounced like English "y". This is called **yeísmo**.
- *ella* = "EH-ya", *calle* = "KAH-yeh", *llegar* = "yeh-GAR"
- Argentina/Uruguay exception: *ll* and *y* are pronounced "zh" (like French *j*) — *calle* = "KAH-zheh"

### The H — Always Silent
*hacer, hora, hambre, hotel, hijo* — the H is invisible. Treat it as if it's not there.

### B and V — Identical
In standard Spanish, B and V are pronounced identically. Both are:
- A full **bilabial stop** [b] after a pause or after *m/n*: *banco, también, venir*
- A **bilabial fricative** [β] between vowels: *haber, lavar, saber*

## 📚 4. Stress Rules

Spanish stress is predictable from spelling. There are three rules and they cover every word.

### Rule 1 — Words ending in a vowel, N, or S
Stress falls on the **second-to-last syllable** (penultimate).
*ca-SA, ha-BLO, jo-VEN, li-BROS, co-MEN*

### Rule 2 — Words ending in any consonant except N or S
Stress falls on the **last syllable**.
*ha-BLAR, ciu-DAD, pa-RED, es-PA-ñol*

### Rule 3 — Written accent marks (´)
If a written accent appears on a syllable, stress that syllable — regardless of Rules 1 and 2.
*ca-FÉ, mé-di-co, a-QUÉL, tam-BIÉN*

> **The accent also distinguishes meaning:**
> *sí* (yes) vs. *si* (if) | *él* (he) vs. *el* (the) | *tú* (you) vs. *tu* (your) | *más* (more) vs. *mas* (but)

### Esdrújulas (stress on the third-to-last)
These always have a written accent: *mú-si-ca, rá-pi-do, es-drú-ju-la, sim-pá-ti-co*

## 📚 5. Castilian vs. Latin American Pronunciation

| Feature | Latin American | Castilian (Spain) |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| C before e/i | /s/ — *cerveza* = "ser-BEH-sa" | /θ/ (th) — *cerveza* = "ther-BEH-tha" |
| Z | /s/ | /θ/ (th) |
| LL | /y/ (yeísmo dominant) | /y/ (yeísmo dominant in most regions; /ʎ/ in some) |
| S at end of syllable | Pronounced /s/ | Often aspirated or dropped (*estos* → "ehtoh") |
| Vosotros | Not used | Used for 2nd person plural informal |

> **This curriculum defaults to neutral Latin American Spanish** (most useful for US/Mexico contexts). Neither is "more correct" — they are regional varieties.

## 🛠️ 6. Practice Methods

### Shadowing (most important)
1. Find a 30-second clip of native Spanish at your level (Dreaming Spanish beginner, NHK equivalent).
2. Listen 5 times. Notice the rhythm, not just the words.
3. Listen and repeat simultaneously — match the speaker's exact rhythm, speed, and vowel quality.
4. Record yourself. Compare. Repeat.

### Minimal pairs drill
Practice pairs that differ by one sound — forces fine-grained listening:
- *pero* (but) vs. *perro* (dog) — single r vs. trill
- *caro* (expensive) vs. *carro* (car) — same
- *peso* (weight/currency) vs. *preso* (prisoner) — s vs. r
- *casa* (house) vs. *caza* (hunt) — s vs. z (Latin Am: same sound; Castilian: different)

## ⚠️ 7. Common Errors (English Speakers)

| Error | What happens | Fix |
|-------|-------------|-----|
| English vowels in Spanish words | *mesa* → "MAY-suh" | Pure Spanish /e/ — "MEH-sah" |
| Aspirating P, T, K | English "pin" has a puff; Spanish *pin* doesn't | Put hand in front of mouth — no puff allowed |
| English R for Spanish R | "arena" with English r sounds foreign | Practice the tap — it's close to the "d/t" in American "butter" |
| Pronouncing H | *hacer* → "HA-ser" | H is always silent — "ah-SER" |
| Treating B and V differently | Overcorrecting to English v-sound | Both are the same in Spanish; don't overthink it |
| Flat stress | English reduces unstressed vowels to "uh"; Spanish doesn't | Every vowel must be its full sound |
