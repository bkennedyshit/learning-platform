# 21.7 - Earths History

Earth is about **4.54 billion years** old. No one was there to record its history, so geologists read it from rocks: the order of layers, the fossils inside them, and the steady decay of radioactive atoms. **Relative dating** tells you which event came first. **Absolute** (or numerical) **dating** tells you how many years ago something happened. Combining the two produces the **geologic time scale**, the calendar of Earth's past.

## Core ideas

### Relative dating principles

- **Superposition:** in a sequence of sedimentary layers that has not been overturned or disrupted by faulting, the layers at the **bottom are older** than those at the top. USGS credits James Hutton with formally proposing this way of classifying rocks by relative age in the late 1700s.
- **Original horizontality:** sediments and lava flows are laid down in nearly **horizontal** layers. If layers are tilted or folded, something happened to them **after** they formed.
- **Cross-cutting relationships:** a feature that **cuts across** another (a fault, or a dike of igneous rock) is **younger** than the rock it cuts.
- **Inclusions:** a rock fragment enclosed in another rock is **older** than the rock around it.
- **Unconformities:** surfaces where layers were eroded or not deposited, representing a **gap** in the record. In the Grand Canyon, tilted Proterozoic rocks (825–1,250 million years old) are overlain by flat Paleozoic rocks, a gap of nearly 300 million years.

### Fossils and correlation

William Smith found that particular layers contain particular fossils, and that fossils appear in a consistent order (**faunal succession**). An **index fossil** is a good time marker when the organism:

- lived for a **short** time span,
- was **widespread** geographically, and
- is easy to recognize.

Earle notes that sharks are poor index fossils because they have existed for over 400 million years, while ammonites are useful because each species lasted a short time and has distinctive features. Rocks containing the same index fossil are about the same age even if they are on different continents.

### Radioactive decay and half-life

Some isotopes are **radioactive**: a **parent** isotope spontaneously changes into a **daughter** isotope of a different element at a constant rate. The **half-life** is the time for half of the parent atoms in a sample to decay.

| Half-lives passed | Fraction of parent remaining |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1 (100%) |
| 1 | 1/2 (50%) |
| 2 | 1/4 (25%) |
| 3 | 1/8 (12.5%) |
| 4 | 1/16 (6.25%) |

In general, after $n$ half-lives the fraction remaining is $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n$.

- **Carbon-14** has a half-life of **5,730 years**. Living things keep a nearly constant proportion of carbon-14 while alive; after death it decays. USGS notes it can date events only within about the past **50,000 years**, so it is used for wood, bone, charcoal, and cloth, not for ancient rocks.
- **Potassium-40** decays to argon-40 with a half-life of about **1.3 billion years** (Earle). Long-lived isotopes like this, and uranium isotopes that decay to lead, date rocks millions to billions of years old.
- USGS points out that radiometric methods mostly date **igneous** rocks, which usually lack fossils. Sedimentary layers are dated by **bracketing** them between igneous layers whose ages are known.

### How old is Earth?

USGS gives the best age for Earth and the solar system as **4.54 billion years**, with an uncertainty of less than 1 percent, based on lead isotopes in Earth's oldest lead ores compared with meteorites. Meteorites date from 4.53 to 4.58 billion years. The oldest known Earth rocks, the Acasta Gneisses of northwestern Canada, are about 4.03 billion years old, and single zircon crystals from Western Australia are as old as about 4.3–4.4 billion years. Earth's original crust has been recycled by plate tectonics, which is why no rocks survive from the very beginning.

### The geologic time scale

Time is divided into nested units: **eons → eras → periods → epochs**. Boundaries are set where the rock record shows major changes, often in fossils. The International Commission on Stratigraphy (ICS) chart (version 2024/12) gives these boundary ages, in millions of years ago (Ma):

| Eon | Era | Span (Ma) | Highlights (NPS chart) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hadean | — | 4,567 – 4,031 | Earth forms; crust forms |
| Archean | — | 4,031 – 2,500 | Oldest known rocks; early bacteria and algae (stromatolites) |
| Proterozoic | — | 2,500 – 538.8 | Simple, then complex, multicellular organisms |
| Phanerozoic | Paleozoic | 538.8 – 251.9 | Early shelled organisms (Cambrian); fish; first land plants and forests; amphibians; reptiles; coal swamps; supercontinent Pangaea |
| Phanerozoic | Mesozoic | 251.9 – 66.0 | First dinosaurs and first mammals (Triassic); Pangaea begins to break up; dinosaurs abundant (Jurassic); first flowering plants (Cretaceous) |
| Phanerozoic | Cenozoic | 66.0 – present | Early primates; grassy ecosystems spread; ice-age glaciations (Pleistocene); modern humans |

Boundary ages are revised as dating improves. For example, the NPS chart (2018) dates the start of the Cambrian at 541 Ma, while the newer ICS chart gives 538.8 Ma.

### The five largest mass extinctions

The NPS geologic time scale marks five mass extinctions, often called the "Big Five." Approximate ICS boundary ages are given:

1. **End-Ordovician** (Ordovician-Silurian), about 443 Ma.
2. **Late Devonian**, around 372 Ma.
3. **End-Permian**, about 252 Ma, the boundary between the Paleozoic and Mesozoic eras.
4. **End-Triassic**, about 201 Ma.
5. **End-Cretaceous** (K-Pg), 66 Ma, which ended the age of non-avian dinosaurs and the Mesozoic Era.

Causes differ. NPS links the end-Ordovician and Devonian extinctions to climate change, glaciation, and sea-level change. For the end-Cretaceous event, NPS describes a worldwide clay layer rich in iridium (discovered by Luis and Walter Alvarez) and an asteroid roughly 10–20 km across that struck near the Yucatán Peninsula and left a crater about 190 km wide.

### Humans on the time scale

The Smithsonian's Human Origins Program states that *Homo sapiens* evolved in Africa about **300,000 years ago**, which is about 66 million years after the end-Cretaceous extinction.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Ordering a cross-section

*Illustrative cross-section.* From bottom to top: sandstone A, shale B, limestone C, all horizontal. A dike of basalt D cuts through A and B but stops at the base of C. A fault F breaks A, B, C, and the dike.

1. Superposition: A formed first, then B (A and B are older than C).
2. Cross-cutting: D cuts A and B, so D is younger than B. D stops at the base of C, so C was deposited after D formed (the top of the dike was probably eroded first).
3. C is deposited.
4. F cuts everything, including C and D, so F is the youngest event.
5. Order: **A → B → D → C → F**.

### Example 2: Carbon-14 age

A piece of charcoal has 25% of the carbon-14 found in living wood. How old is it?

1. 25% = 1/4 = $(1/2)^2$, so **2 half-lives** have passed.
2. Age $= 2 \times 5{,}730 = 11{,}460$ years.
3. Check with USGS's own example: half the carbon-14 means one half-life, 5,730 years; a quarter should be twice that. ✓
4. Limit: this is well within the roughly 50,000-year range of the method.

### Example 3: Potassium-argon age

A feldspar crystal in granite has 12.5% of its original potassium-40 left. Estimate its age.

1. 12.5% = 1/8 = $(1/2)^3$, so **3 half-lives**.
2. Age $\approx 3 \times 1.3\ \text{billion years} = 3.9$ billion years.
3. Earle uses the same case: after 3.9 billion years, 12.5% of the original potassium-40 remains. ✓

### Example 4: Bracketing a sedimentary layer

*Illustrative ages.* A shale layer containing fossils lies on top of a lava flow dated at 150 million years and beneath a volcanic ash layer dated at 140 million years.

1. Superposition: the lava flow is older than the shale, and the ash is younger.
2. So the shale (and its fossils) formed **between 150 and 140 million years ago**.
3. If the same index fossil is found in an undated shale on another continent, that shale can also be assigned to roughly 140–150 million years.

### Example 5: Earth history as one calendar year

Compress 4.54 billion years into one 365-day year, with Earth forming at midnight on January 1. When do these events occur?

1. Scale: one day represents $4{,}540 \div 365 \approx 12.4$ million years.
2. Start of the Cambrian (538.8 Ma): $538.8 \div 4{,}540 \times 365 \approx 43.3$ days before the year ends → about **November 18**.
3. End-Cretaceous extinction (66 Ma): $66 \div 4{,}540 \times 365 \approx 5.3$ days before the end → about **December 26**.
4. *Homo sapiens* (300,000 years): $0.3 \div 4{,}540 \times 365 \times 24 \times 60 \approx 35$ minutes before midnight on **December 31**.
5. Interpretation: almost all of Earth's history happened before animals with shells became common, and humans appear in the final minutes.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The bottom layer is always the oldest."** Only if the layers have not been overturned by folding or moved by faults. Look for evidence of disturbance first.
- **"Carbon-14 is used to date dinosaurs."** Dinosaur fossils are tens of millions of years old, far beyond the roughly 50,000-year range of carbon-14. Long-lived isotopes in nearby igneous rocks are used instead.
- **"After two half-lives, all the parent is gone."** Each half-life removes half of what is **left**: after two half-lives, 25% remains.
- **"Humans lived alongside dinosaurs."** The last non-avian dinosaurs died 66 million years ago; *Homo sapiens* appeared about 300,000 years ago.
- **"Radiometric dating works on any rock."** It mainly dates igneous (and some metamorphic) minerals. Sedimentary rocks are usually dated by bracketing and by fossils.
- **"The dates on the time scale are fixed."** The order of units is well established, but boundary numbers are refined as dating improves (541 versus 538.8 Ma for the Cambrian base, for example).
- **"Unconformities are missing rock, so nothing happened."** An unconformity records time when erosion removed rock or no sediment was deposited; the events still happened, but their record is missing at that spot.

## Practice

1. A fault cuts layers 1–4 but not layer 5 above it. Is the fault older or younger than layer 5? Explain.
2. A pebble of granite is found inside a sandstone. Which is older? Which principle applies?
3. What three characteristics make a fossil a good index fossil?
4. A bone has 1/8 of its original carbon-14. How old is it?
5. An isotope has a half-life of 700 million years. What fraction remains after 2.1 billion years?
6. Why can't geologists date a sandstone layer directly with potassium-argon dating in most cases?
7. Put in order from oldest to youngest: first flowering plants, first land plants, end-Permian extinction, *Homo sapiens*, oldest known Earth rocks.
8. In the one-year calendar model, about what date would the end-Permian extinction (about 252 Ma) fall on?

<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. Older than layer 5. The fault cuts layers 1–4, so it formed after them; it does not cut layer 5, so layer 5 was deposited after the faulting.
2. The granite is older (principle of inclusions): the pebble existed before the sandstone formed around it.
3. Short time span, wide geographic distribution, and easy recognition.
4. 1/8 = 3 half-lives: $3 \times 5{,}730 = 17{,}190$ years.
5. $2{,}100 \div 700 = 3$ half-lives; $(1/2)^3 = 1/8$ (12.5%) remains.
6. The sand grains came from older rocks, so their isotopes record when those source rocks formed, not when the sandstone was deposited. Sedimentary layers are usually bracketed by dated igneous layers instead.
7. Oldest known Earth rocks (Archean) → first land plants (Silurian) → end-Permian extinction (about 252 Ma) → first flowering plants (Cretaceous) → *Homo sapiens*.
8. $252 \div 4{,}540 \times 365 \approx 20.3$ days before the end of the year → about **December 11**.
