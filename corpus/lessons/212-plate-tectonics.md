# 21.2 - Plate Tectonics

**Plate tectonics** is the theory that Earth's rigid outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into a dozen or more large and small **plates** that move relative to one another on hotter, more mobile rock beneath them. It explains why earthquakes and volcanoes occur in narrow belts, how mountain ranges and ocean basins form, and why fossils of the same land plants are found on continents now separated by oceans. This lesson follows the evidence from Wegener's continental drift hypothesis to the modern theory, then applies it to plate boundaries and plate-motion rates.

## Core ideas

### Continental drift: a good idea without a mechanism

In 1912 the German meteorologist **Alfred Wegener** proposed that the continents had once been joined in a supercontinent, **Pangaea**, which began to split apart about 200 million years ago. His evidence, as summarized by USGS:

- **Fit of the coastlines**, especially South America and Africa.
- **Matching fossils** on coasts now separated by the Atlantic, of organisms that could not have crossed an ocean, including the fossil fern *Glossopteris* in regions that are now polar.
- **Climate clues out of place**: coal formed from tropical plants found in Antarctica, and glacial deposits in present-day dry parts of Africa.
- **Matching rock structures** on opposite coasts.

His idea was rejected for decades mainly because he could not explain **what force could move continents**. He suggested that continents plowed through the ocean floor, and the geophysicist Harold Jeffreys argued, correctly, that solid rock could not plow through the ocean floor without breaking up.

### New evidence from the ocean floor (1950s–1960s)

USGS lists four developments that led to plate tectonics:

1. **Ocean-floor mapping** with echo sounding revealed the **mid-ocean ridge**, a mountain chain more than 50,000 km long that winds around the globe "like the seam on a baseball." In 1947, researchers also found that sediment on the Atlantic floor was much thinner than expected for an ancient ocean.
2. **Magnetic striping.** Basalt on the sea floor contains the magnetic mineral magnetite. When lava cools, its magnetite grains lock in the direction of Earth's magnetic field at that time. Earth's field has reversed many times, so the sea floor shows **alternating stripes** of normal and reversed polarity laid out **symmetrically** on both sides of the ridge.
3. **Seafloor spreading.** Harry Hess and Robert Dietz proposed that new oceanic crust forms at the ridge crest and spreads away on both sides, and that old oceanic crust sinks back into the mantle at deep **trenches**. Drill cores collected by the research ship *Glomar Challenger* in 1968 showed rock ages increasing with distance from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
4. **Earthquake zones** were found to be concentrated along trenches and ridges, exactly where crust was predicted to be created and destroyed.

Because crust is destroyed at about the same rate it is created, Earth does not grow larger.

### The theory and an open question

Plates are pieces of **lithosphere**, carrying both continents and ocean floor. They move over the slowly flowing **asthenosphere**. The theory is widely accepted, but USGS notes that scientists still debate the exact nature of the forces that propel the plates, which is the same question Wegener could not answer.

### Types of plate boundaries

| Boundary | Motion | Typical features | Example (USGS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Divergent** | Plates move apart; new crust forms | Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, shallow earthquakes, volcanoes | Mid-Atlantic Ridge and Iceland; East African Rift |
| **Convergent: oceanic-continental** | Dense oceanic plate subducts under continent | Deep trench, coastal volcanic mountains, strong and deep earthquakes | Nazca Plate under South America (Andes); Cascades |
| **Convergent: oceanic-oceanic** | One oceanic plate subducts under the other | Trench and a curved **island arc** of volcanoes | Mariana Trench and Mariana Islands; Aleutians |
| **Convergent: continental-continental** | Neither plate easily subducts | Crust crumples into high mountains and plateaus | India colliding with Asia (Himalayas, Tibetan Plateau) |
| **Transform** | Plates slide past each other horizontally | Shallow earthquakes; crust neither created nor destroyed | San Andreas fault zone, California |

USGS also describes **plate-boundary zones**, broad belts such as the Mediterranean-Alpine region where several small microplates make the boundary hard to define.

The Challenger Deep, at the southern end of the Mariana Trench, plunges nearly 11,000 m below sea level, farther than Mount Everest rises above it.

### Hotspots

Most volcanoes are near plate boundaries, but the Hawaiian Islands formed more than 3,200 km from the nearest boundary. In 1963 J. Tuzo Wilson proposed that a long-lasting hot region in the mantle, a **hotspot**, feeds a volcano while the Pacific Plate moves over it. As the plate carries a volcano away, it goes extinct and a new one forms over the hotspot. The result is a chain of volcanoes that gets **older in one direction**. On Kauai, the oldest volcanic rocks are about 5.5 million years old and deeply eroded; on the Island of Hawaii, the oldest exposed rocks are less than 0.7 million years old and new rock is still forming. USGS adds that scientists now debate whether hotspots are deep and fixed, as the original model assumed.

### Measuring plate motion

- **Long-term averages** come from magnetic stripes of known age and from matching offset rock formations across a boundary.
- **Present-day motion** is measured with satellite methods, especially GPS. USGS reports that rates averaged over several years compare well with rates averaged over millions of years.
- Rates: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at about **2.5 cm/yr** (25 km per million years); the Arctic Ridge at less than 2.5 cm/yr; the East Pacific Rise near Easter Island at more than **15 cm/yr**. Along the San Andreas fault, the Pacific Plate has moved past the North American Plate at an average of about **5 cm/yr** for 10 million years.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: How wide can an ocean grow?

At 2.5 cm/yr, how far apart would two points on opposite sides of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge move in 100 million years?

1. Distance = rate × time = $2.5\ \tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{yr}} \times 100{,}000{,}000\ \text{yr} = 250{,}000{,}000\ \text{cm}$.
2. Convert: $250{,}000{,}000\ \text{cm} \div 100{,}000\ \tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{km}} = 2{,}500\ \text{km}$.
3. Check with the USGS shortcut: 25 km per million years × 100 million years = 2,500 km. ✓
4. Interpretation: USGS describes spreading over the past 100–200 million years turning the Atlantic from a narrow inlet into a full ocean; a slow rate becomes thousands of kilometers over geologic time.

### Example 2: Offset along the San Andreas

If the Pacific Plate has moved past the North American Plate at an average of 5 cm/yr for 10 million years, how much total offset does that rate imply?

1. $5\ \tfrac{\text{cm}}{\text{yr}} \times 10{,}000{,}000\ \text{yr} = 50{,}000{,}000\ \text{cm}$.
2. $50{,}000{,}000 \div 100{,}000 = 500\ \text{km}$.
3. Boundary: this assumes the average rate held the whole time. USGS describes plate edges that stay locked for long periods and then move suddenly in large earthquakes, so motion along a boundary is not necessarily smooth from year to year.

### Example 3: A hotspot chain rate

*Illustrative example with round numbers (not measured values).* A volcanic island's oldest rocks are 4 million years old, and the island is now 400 km from the active hotspot. Estimate the plate's average speed.

1. Rate = distance ÷ time = $400\ \text{km} \div 4\ \text{million yr} = 100\ \text{km per million years}$.
2. Convert to cm/yr: $100\ \text{km} = 10{,}000{,}000\ \text{cm}$, and $10{,}000{,}000\ \text{cm} \div 1{,}000{,}000\ \text{yr} = 10\ \text{cm/yr}$.
3. Direction: the plate moves **away from** the hotspot toward the older islands. Older islands should also be more eroded, as USGS describes for Kauai.

### Example 4: Reading the sea floor

A ship crosses a ridge and records magnetic stripes: normal, reversed, normal, **ridge crest (normal)**, normal, reversed, normal. Drill samples get older away from the crest.

1. The pattern on the left mirrors the pattern on the right, so the crust was formed at the crest and carried away symmetrically.
2. The crest stripe has normal polarity because it formed in Earth's present field.
3. Rock ages increasing away from the crest match seafloor spreading, not a sea floor that formed all at once.
4. Conclusion: this is a divergent boundary.

### Example 5: Classifying a boundary from clues

1. *A deep trench, a curved chain of volcanic islands, and frequent earthquakes* → oceanic-oceanic convergence (like the Marianas).
2. *The highest mountains on a continent with a high plateau, formed where two continents met* → continental-continental convergence (like the Himalayas).
3. *Shallow earthquakes and streams offset sideways along a long fault on land* → transform boundary (like the San Andreas).
4. *A widening rift valley with volcanoes on a continent* → divergent boundary beginning on land (like the East African Rift).

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Continents plow through the ocean floor."** That was Wegener's mechanism and it was physically impossible. Whole lithospheric plates move, carrying continents and ocean floor together.
- **"Plates float on an ocean of liquid magma."** The asthenosphere is hot, semi-solid rock that flows very slowly over geologic time; it is not a liquid layer.
- **"New crust at ridges means Earth is getting bigger."** Subduction destroys old oceanic lithosphere at about the same rate that ridges create new crust.
- **"All volcanoes are at plate boundaries."** Hotspot volcanoes such as Hawaii form far from boundaries.
- **"When continents collide, one subducts like ocean floor."** USGS explains that continental rock is relatively light and resists sinking, so the crust buckles upward and sideways instead.
- **"Plates move fast enough to notice."** Typical rates are a few centimeters per year; they are measured with GPS and long-term geologic records, not felt day to day. Sudden slip during an earthquake is a different, brief event.
- **"Everything about plate tectonics is settled."** The overall theory is strongly supported, but the driving forces and the nature of hotspots are still debated.

7. Why does a trench form at an oceanic-continental boundary but not at a continental-continental boundary?
