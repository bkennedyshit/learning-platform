# 21.6 - The Solar System & Universe

Earth is one planet orbiting one ordinary star, the Sun, in one galaxy among a vast number of others. The motions of Earth, the Moon, and the Sun explain everyday patterns: day and night, the seasons, the Moon's changing shape, eclipses, and tides. Farther out, the same laws of gravity and light let astronomers describe how stars live and die and how the universe has changed over about 13.8 billion years. This lesson moves outward from Earth to the edge of the observable universe.

## Core ideas

### The Earth-Moon-Sun system

- **Day and night.** Earth spins on its axis once a day, so every place turns into and out of sunlight.
- **Seasons.** NASA states that Earth's axis is tilted **23.4°** relative to the plane of its orbit, and that this tilt causes the seasons. The axis keeps pointing the same direction in space as Earth orbits, so around June the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun and receives more direct rays and longer days (summer), and around December it leans away (winter). The hemispheres have opposite seasons.
- **Distance is not the cause.** Earth is closest to the Sun (**perihelion**, about 91.4 million miles) in **January** and farthest (**aphelion**, about 94.5 million miles) in **July**, which is northern winter and summer, the opposite of what a distance explanation predicts.
- **Moon phases.** The Moon makes no light of its own; it reflects sunlight. Half of the Moon is always lit. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. The eight phases are new, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, third (last) quarter, and waning crescent; the cycle repeats about every **29.5 days**. A full Moon rises around sunset; a first-quarter Moon rises around midday.
- **Eclipses.** A **solar eclipse** happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and its shadow falls on Earth; it can occur only at **new Moon**. A **lunar eclipse** happens when Earth's shadow falls on the Moon; it can occur only at **full Moon**. Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's, so the three bodies usually do not line up exactly.
- **Tides.** Ocean tides come mainly from the Moon's gravity, with help from the Sun. NOAA explains that tidal force depends on the **cube** of distance, so although the Sun is about 27 million times more massive than the Moon, it is about 390 times farther away, and its tide-generating force is only about **half** the Moon's. **Spring tides** (largest range) occur near new and full Moon, when Sun and Moon line up; **neap tides** (smallest range) occur near first and third quarter, when they are at right angles. Many coasts have two high and two low tides each day.

### The solar system

NASA lists **eight planets**:

| Group | Planets | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner (terrestrial)** | Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars | Small, rocky, solid surfaces, close to the Sun |
| **Outer (giant)** | Jupiter, Saturn (gas giants); Uranus, Neptune (ice giants) | Large, no solid surface, deep atmospheres around a core |

- **Dwarf planets:** NASA recognizes five: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union's definition of "planet" moved Pluto into this category.
- **Asteroids** are rocky bodies; most orbit in the **main asteroid belt** between Mars and Jupiter. The largest, Vesta, is about 530 km across, and all asteroids together have less mass than the Moon.
- **Comets** are "cosmic snowballs" of frozen gases, rock, and dust. Near the Sun they heat up and release gas and dust that form a glowing head and a tail pointing away from the Sun.
- The **Kuiper Belt** is a donut-shaped region of icy bodies beginning at Neptune's orbit, about 30 AU from the Sun. Pluto is its largest known member.
- The **Oort Cloud** is a *hypothesized* spherical shell of icy comet nuclei, much of it roughly 50,000 AU (nearly a light-year) from the Sun. It has not been directly observed; astronomers infer it as the source of long-period comets.

### Stars

Stars form in cold clouds of gas and dust. A collapsing clump becomes a **protostar**; when the core gets hot and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei **fuse** into helium, releasing energy. A star that is steadily fusing hydrogen into helium is a **main-sequence** star. This is the longest stage of a star's life; NASA says the Sun is roughly midway through it. A star's **mass** controls its life: low-mass stars burn longer, dimmer, and cooler, while massive stars burn their fuel much faster.

- **Low-mass stars like the Sun:** main sequence → **red giant** → outer layers drift off as a **planetary nebula** → the core remains as a **white dwarf**.
- **High-mass stars:** main sequence → red giant or supergiant → **supernova** explosion → the remaining core becomes a **neutron star** or, if massive enough, a **black hole**. Supernovae also spread newly made heavy elements into space.

The **Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram** plots stars' **luminosity** (true brightness) against **surface temperature**, with hot stars on the left by convention. Most stars fall on a diagonal band, the **main sequence**, running from hot, bright stars (upper left) to cool, dim stars (lower right). Red giants sit above the main sequence on the right (cool but very luminous because they are huge); white dwarfs sit below it on the left (hot but dim because they are small). Astronomers also sort stars into spectral classes by temperature, from hottest to coolest O, B, A, F, G, K, M; the Sun is a G-type "yellow dwarf."

### Galaxies and cosmic distances

The Sun is one of more than 100 billion stars in the **Milky Way**, a spiral galaxy with a central bar and two major arms. The solar system takes about 230 million years to orbit the galaxy's center.

| Unit | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---|
| **Astronomical unit (AU)** | Earth-Sun mean distance, defined exactly by the IAU | 149,597,870,700 m (about 150 million km) |
| **Light-year (ly)** | Distance light travels in one year | about 9.46 trillion km |
| **Parsec (pc)** | Distance at which 1 AU appears to span 1 arcsecond | about 206,265 AU, or about 3.26 ly |

The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.2 light-years away.

### The Big Bang

NASA describes the universe as about **13.8 billion years** old and expanding. Two key lines of evidence:

1. **Redshift and expansion.** Light from a source moving away from us is stretched to longer wavelengths, toward the red end of the spectrum. In 1929 Edwin Hubble, using redshifts measured by Milton Humason, showed that galaxies are moving away from us and that more distant galaxies are receding faster: the universe is expanding. Running the expansion backward points to a hot, dense early universe.
2. **The cosmic microwave background (CMB).** About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough for atoms to form, and light could travel freely. That glow is still detectable everywhere in the sky as microwaves; NASA calls it the oldest light we can observe.

In 1998 astronomers found that the expansion is **speeding up**, which scientists attribute to an unexplained "dark energy." What came before cosmic inflation, and what dark energy is, remain open questions.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Is distance the reason for summer?

Use NASA's perihelion and aphelion distances to test the "closer means summer" idea.

1. Difference: $94.5 - 91.4 = 3.1$ million miles.
2. Average distance: $(94.5 + 91.4) \div 2 = 92.95$ million miles.
3. Relative change: $3.1 \div 92.95 \approx 0.033$, or about **3%**.
4. Timing: Earth is closest in January, during Northern Hemisphere winter.
5. Conclusion: the distance change is small and has the wrong timing for the Northern Hemisphere, and it cannot explain why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same moment. Axial tilt explains all three observations.

### Example 2: Why the Moon controls the tides

NOAA says the Sun is about 27 million times more massive than the Moon but about 390 times farther away, and tidal force varies with mass divided by distance cubed. Compare the Sun's tidal effect with the Moon's.

1. Distance factor: $390^3 = 390 \times 390 \times 390 = 59{,}319{,}000$, about 59 million.
2. Ratio (Sun ÷ Moon): $\dfrac{27{,}000{,}000}{59{,}319{,}000} \approx 0.46$.
3. Interpretation: the Sun's tide-generating force is a little less than half the Moon's, so the Moon dominates. When they line up (new and full Moon), their effects add to produce spring tides.

### Example 3: How old is sunlight?

How long does light take to travel 1 AU?

1. Distance: $1\ \text{AU} = 149{,}597{,}870.7\ \text{km}$. Speed of light: $299{,}792.458\ \text{km/s}$.
2. Time $= \dfrac{149{,}597{,}870.7}{299{,}792.458} \approx 499\ \text{s}$.
3. Convert: $499 \div 60 \approx 8.3$ minutes.
4. Interpretation: you always see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago. Looking at distant objects is looking back in time.

### Example 4: Converting cosmic distances

How far is Proxima Centauri (about 4.2 ly) in kilometers and in AU?

1. Kilometers: $4.2 \times 9.46 \times 10^{12} \approx 3.97 \times 10^{13}$ km, about 40 trillion km.
2. 1 AU is about $1.496 \times 10^{8}$ km, so $\dfrac{3.97 \times 10^{13}}{1.496 \times 10^{8}} \approx 2.65 \times 10^{5}$ AU, about 265,000 AU.
3. Check the parsec: 4.2 ly ÷ 3.26 ly/pc ≈ 1.3 pc.
4. Interpretation: the nearest star is about 265,000 times farther than the Sun. Large distances need large units.

### Example 5: Reading an H-R diagram

*Illustrative descriptions.* Place each star on an H-R diagram: (A) very hot, very luminous; (B) cool, very luminous; (C) hot, very dim; (D) temperature and luminosity similar to the Sun.

1. A: upper left of the main sequence; a massive, blue main-sequence star with a short life.
2. B: upper right; a red giant (or supergiant), large and cool.
3. C: lower left; a white dwarf, small and hot.
4. D: middle of the main sequence, like the Sun.
5. Check: luminosity depends on both temperature and size, which is why a cool star can be very bright if it is enormous.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Seasons happen because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer."** Earth is closest in January, and the hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time. The 23.4° tilt causes the seasons.
- **"Moon phases are caused by Earth's shadow."** Phases come from seeing different portions of the Moon's sunlit half. Earth's shadow on the Moon is a lunar eclipse, which happens only occasionally.
- **"There is an eclipse every month."** The Moon's orbit is tilted, so the new or full Moon usually passes above or below the Sun-Earth line.
- **"The dark side of the Moon never gets sunlight."** The Moon has a day side and a night side that change as it rotates; the far side is lit at new Moon.
- **"A light-year is a unit of time."** It is a distance: how far light travels in a year.
- **"The Big Bang was an explosion at one point in space."** The evidence describes the expansion of space itself from a hot, dense state everywhere, not debris flying outward from a center.
- **"The Oort Cloud has been photographed."** It is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets and has not been directly observed.

> **Safety note:** Never look directly at the Sun, including during a partial or annular eclipse. NASA advises using certified solar viewing ("eclipse") glasses or a handheld solar viewer at all times, or an indirect method such as a pinhole projector; regular sunglasses are not safe, and you must never look through binoculars, a telescope, or a camera with only eclipse glasses. NASA notes that it is always safe to watch a lunar eclipse with the naked eye.
