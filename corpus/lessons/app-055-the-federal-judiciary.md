# APP-05.5 — The Federal Judiciary

The **federal judiciary** is the system of national courts that decides cases under the Constitution, federal laws, and treaties. Article III, Section 1 of the Constitution provides that "the judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish." Federal judges "hold their Offices during good Behaviour," and their pay "shall not be diminished during their Continuance in Office." Those protections are designed to make judges independent of the elected branches.

This lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). It explains how federal courts are organized, how the Supreme Court decides which cases to hear, where the power of **judicial review** came from, and how a few landmark decisions shaped American law. Several of those decisions remain politically controversial; the lesson states what the Court held and identifies where people disagree, without asking you to take a side.

## Core ideas

### How the federal courts are organized

| Level | Number | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. district courts (trial courts) | 94 | "Resolve disputes by determining the facts and applying the law to those facts," in the words of the federal judiciary's website. Trials, witnesses, juries. |
| U.S. courts of appeals | 13: twelve regional circuits plus the Federal Circuit | Decide "whether the law was applied correctly in the trial court or federal administrative agency." No new trial; judges review the record. |
| Supreme Court of the United States | 1, with a Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices | Final word on federal law and the U.S. Constitution |

Congress created the lower courts and set the Supreme Court's size. The Judiciary Act of 1789 "created a Supreme Court with six justices" and "established the lower federal court system." Later laws set the Court's size anywhere "from a low of five to a high of 10," and "shortly after the Civil War, the number of seats on the Court was fixed at nine."

**State courts handle most cases.** According to the federal judiciary, state courts hear most criminal cases, probate, most contract and personal-injury cases, and family law. "State courts are the final arbiters of state laws and constitutions," but their interpretation of federal law or the U.S. Constitution may be appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, which "may choose to hear or not to hear such cases."

### Jurisdiction

**Jurisdiction** is a court's legal authority to hear a case. Under Article III, the Supreme Court has **original jurisdiction** (the case starts there) in a small set of cases, such as "suits between two or more states" and cases involving ambassadors. In almost every other case it has **appellate jurisdiction**: it reviews decisions of lower courts.

### How the Supreme Court chooses its cases

- A party asks for review by filing a **petition for a writ of certiorari**. Since the Certiorari Act of 1925, the Court has discretion over most of its appellate docket.
- Under the Court's rules, "four of the nine Justices must vote to accept a case" (often called the rule of four).
- The Court accepts only a small fraction of requests. The federal judiciary's educational page says the Court "agrees to hear about 100-150 of the more than 7,000 cases that it is asked to review each year"; the Supreme Court's own page says "approximately 5,000-7,000" cases are filed each year. The figures differ by source and year, but both show that most petitions are denied.
- A denial of certiorari leaves the lower court's decision in place. It is not a ruling that the lower court was right.

### Kinds of opinions

| Opinion | Who writes it | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Majority opinion | A justice writing for a majority of the justices who took part in the case | States the Court's holding and reasoning; binding precedent |
| Concurring opinion | A justice who agrees with the outcome | May agree with different reasoning or add a point |
| Dissenting opinion | A justice who disagrees with the outcome | Not binding, but can influence later courts and public debate |
| Per curiam opinion | "By the court," unsigned | Used, for example, in *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) and *New York Times Co. v. United States* (1971) |

### Judicial review and precedent

**Judicial review** is "the ability of the Court to declare a Legislative or Executive act in violation of the Constitution." The federal judiciary's site notes that "it is not found within the text of the Constitution itself; the Court established this doctrine in the case of *Marbury v. Madison* (1803)." Alexander Hamilton had argued for a similar idea in Federalist No. 78, where he called the judiciary the "least dangerous" branch because it has "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment."

**Stare decisis** ("to stand by things decided") is "the doctrine that courts will adhere to precedent in making their decisions." Lower courts must follow the Supreme Court's precedents. The Supreme Court usually follows its own precedents but can **overrule** them.

### Judicial philosophies

- **Originalism** holds that a legal text "should be interpreted as it was understood at the time of its adoption."
- **Living constitutionalism** holds that "the Constitution is living and can evolve with society, and that the meaning of constitutional texts changes over time." (Both definitions are from Cornell's Legal Information Institute.)
- **Judicial restraint** means deferring to the elected branches and precedent unless a violation is clear. **Judicial activism** is usually a criticism, used for decisions the speaker thinks go beyond the judge's proper role. People across the political spectrum use the label, often for opposite decisions, so treat it as an argument rather than a neutral description.

### Checks on the courts

- The president nominates and the Senate confirms federal judges; the Senate has confirmed 126 Supreme Court nominations.
- Judges can be impeached by the House and removed by the Senate.
- Congress sets the number of justices, creates lower courts, and controls much of their jurisdiction.
- A constitutional amendment can reverse a constitutional decision. For example, the Fourteenth Amendment's first sentence, granting citizenship to "all persons born or naturalized in the United States," rejected the *Dred Scott* holding on citizenship.
- Courts depend on the executive branch to enforce their judgments, as Hamilton noted.

### Landmark cases

| Case | Year | Holding (source summaries) |
|---|---|---|
| *Marbury v. Madison* | 1803 | Established judicial review; for the first time the Court declared an act of Congress unconstitutional |
| *McCulloch v. Maryland* | 1819 | Congress may charter a bank as an implied power; states cannot tax the national bank |
| *Gibbons v. Ogden* | 1824 | Congress has the power to "regulate commerce," and federal law takes precedence over a conflicting state monopoly |
| *Dred Scott v. Sandford* | 1857 | Held that people of African descent whose ancestors had been enslaved, "whether they had become free or not," were not U.S. citizens, and that Congress could not ban slavery in federal territories; later overturned by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments |
| *Plessy v. Ferguson* | 1896 | Upheld a Louisiana law providing "equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races" on railways |
| *Brown v. Board of Education* | 1954 | Unanimously held that segregated public schools violate the Fourteenth Amendment: "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal" |
| *Gideon v. Wainwright* | 1963 | Unanimously held that states must provide lawyers to criminal defendants who cannot afford them |
| *Miranda v. Arizona* | 1966 | 5–4: suspects in police custody must be warned of the right to remain silent and the right to an attorney before questioning |
| *Roe v. Wade* | 1973 | 7–2: recognized a constitutional right to choose abortion, balanced against state interests |
| *Obergefell v. Hodges* | 2015 | 5–4: the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognize marriages between two people of the same sex |
| *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* | 2022 | Held that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and overruled *Roe* and *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Tracing two cases to the Supreme Court

**Illustrative federal case:** a person is charged with a federal crime.

1. Trial in a U.S. district court (facts found, verdict reached).
2. Appeal to the U.S. court of appeals for that circuit, which reviews legal errors.
3. Petition for certiorari to the Supreme Court. If fewer than four justices vote to hear it, the court of appeals' decision stands.

**Illustrative state case:** a person is charged under state law in a state trial court and argues that a police search violated the Fourth Amendment.

1. Trial and appeals in the state system, ending at the state's highest court.
2. Because the case raises a question of federal constitutional law, the person may petition the U.S. Supreme Court. If the question were only about state law, the state's highest court would have the final word.

**Reasoning check:** cases move up only on appeal, and the Supreme Court usually decides only the federal legal question, not the facts.

### Example 2: Reconstructing *Marbury v. Madison* (1803)

**Facts (National Archives):** outgoing President John Adams had issued William Marbury a commission as a justice of the peace, but the new secretary of state, James Madison, refused to deliver it. Marbury asked the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus ordering delivery.

1. **Does Marbury have a right to the commission?** Marshall concluded yes.
2. **Can the Supreme Court issue the writ in this case?** The Judiciary Act of 1789 gave the Court original jurisdiction to issue such writs. But Article III lists the Court's original jurisdiction, and this kind of case is not on the list.
3. **What happens when a law conflicts with the Constitution?** Marshall wrote, "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is," and "a Law repugnant to the Constitution is void."
4. **Holding:** the part of the Judiciary Act giving the Court that original jurisdiction was unconstitutional, so the Court could not issue the writ.

**Why it is a famous strategic decision:** Marbury lost his commission, and the Court ordered no one to do anything. But the Court claimed the power to strike down an act of Congress, a much larger power than the one it gave up.

### Example 3: Precedent, overruling, and how to describe it neutrally

| | Earlier precedent | Later decision | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Segregation | *Plessy* (1896) upheld "equal but separate" | *Brown* (1954): "in the field of public education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place" | Brown rejected "any language in *Plessy* ... contrary to this finding" |
| Abortion | *Roe* (1973) recognized a constitutional right | *Dobbs* (2022): "*Roe* ... and ... *Casey* ... are overruled" | Oyez summarizes five factors the majority weighed, including whether the earlier cases were grounded in text and history, whether their tests were "workable," and reliance interests |

**How to write about it:** state who decided what, when, and on what reasoning, and attribute evaluations to the people who made them. For example: "The *Dobbs* majority concluded the right was not 'deeply rooted in the nation's history'; the dissenting justices disagreed." Avoid writing that a case was "obviously right" or "obviously wrong" in an analytical answer; give the reasons each side offered.

### Example 4: Why a dissent can matter

In *Plessy*, Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented: "Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens." The dissent was not law in 1896. Later advocates and judges quoted it, and *Brown* reached a result consistent with it 58 years later.

**Read the whole source:** the same dissent also says, "The white race deems itself to be the dominant race in this country," and includes a derogatory passage about Chinese immigrants. A careful reader can recognize the dissent's influence on equal-protection arguments while noting that it also reflected prejudices of its time.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The Supreme Court must hear every appeal."** With few exceptions, the Court chooses its cases, and most petitions are denied.
- **"Denial of certiorari means the Court agreed with the lower court."** A denial leaves the lower decision in place without deciding whether it was correct.
- **"Judicial review is written in the Constitution."** The federal judiciary's own site says it is not in the text; *Marbury* established it.
- **"Most cases go to federal court."** State courts handle most criminal, contract, injury, and family cases.
- **"A dissent is part of the law."** Only the majority's holding binds later courts, although dissents can be influential.
- **"Federal judges serve for life no matter what."** They serve "during good Behaviour" and can be impeached and removed.
- **"Supreme Court decisions can never be changed."** They can be overruled by the Court or reversed by constitutional amendment, and Congress can change a statute the Court has interpreted.
- **Boundary: the number nine is set by law.** The Constitution does not fix the number of justices; Congress has changed it before. Proposals to change it again are a political debate, not a settled question.

2. Original or appellate jurisdiction for the Supreme Court? (a) a dispute between two states over a river boundary; (b) an appeal from a federal court of appeals about a federal statute; (c) a state supreme court decision on a First Amendment question.
3. Explain in three or four sentences why *Marbury v. Madison* is described as both a loss and a gain for the Court.
4. A student writes, "The Court denied cert, so the Supreme Court ruled the lower court was correct." Correct the statement.
5. Choose two landmark cases from the table. For each, write one sentence stating the question, one stating the holding, and one stating its significance.

Des Moines* (1969) from Lesson 5.6, assign roles (two attorneys per side and a panel of justices), give each attorney three minutes, and have the justices write short majority and dissenting opinions. Compare the student opinions to the real 7–2 decision afterward.
