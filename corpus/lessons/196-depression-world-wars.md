# 19.6 - Depression & World Wars

The years 1920 to 1945 swung between extremes. The 1920s brought new consumer goods, jazz, and the vote for women, along with immigration restrictions and a revived Ku Klux Klan. The Great Depression that began in 1929 was the worst economic collapse in American history, and the New Deal greatly expanded the federal government's role in daily life. Then the United States entered the Second World War, the deadliest war in human history, and emerged as a global superpower.

The lesson is for high school students (grades 9–12). It discusses the Holocaust, the incarceration of Japanese Americans, and the atomic bombings, which are difficult subjects; take breaks if you need them and talk with a teacher or trusted adult about questions. Its main skill is **using quantitative evidence**: reading numbers as historical evidence and stating their limits.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. Analyze the **1920s** as a contest between change and reaction.
2. Explain the **causes and depth of the Great Depression** with evidence.
3. Evaluate **New Deal** programs by their goals, results, and limits.
4. Explain the move from **neutrality to war** and evaluate wartime decisions.

## Core ideas

### The 1920s: change and reaction

| Change | Reaction |
|---|---|
| The **Great Migration** of Black Southerners to Northern cities: New York's Black population grew from 91,709 in 1910 to 327,706 by 1930 (*American Yawp*) | The **Ku Klux Klan** revived "with millions of new members," targeting Black Americans, immigrants, Catholics, and Jews |
| The **Harlem Renaissance**: writers such as Langston Hughes and Claude McKay; Alain Locke's anthology *The New Negro* (1925) | Race riots and lynchings continued |
| The **Nineteenth Amendment** (ratified August 18, 1920) barred denying the vote "on account of sex" | Many Black women in the South were still blocked from voting |
| Cities, cars, radio, and consumer credit | **Prohibition** (18th Amendment, 1919) tried to enforce older moral standards and was repealed by the 21st Amendment on December 5, 1933 |
| Immigration from southern and eastern Europe before the war | The **Immigration Act of 1924** set quotas at 2 percent of each nationality counted in the 1890 census and "completely excluded immigrants from Asia" (Office of the Historian) |

### The Great Depression

Federal Reserve History describes the Depression as "the longest and deepest downturn in the history of the United States and the modern industrial economy," lasting from 1929 to 1941.

- **The crash.** On "Black Monday," October 28, 1929, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell nearly 13 percent.
- **Banking panics.** Regional banking panics in 1930 and 1931 and national and international crises from 1931 to 1933 followed. The downturn hit bottom in March 1933, when "the commercial banking system collapsed and President Roosevelt declared a national banking holiday."
- **Money and prices.** "From the fall of 1930 through the winter of 1933, the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent," and prices fell by about the same amount. Falling prices made debts harder to repay.
- **Unemployment.** National unemployment reached about 25 percent in 1932, and Black unemployment reached as high as 50 percent (*American Yawp*).
- **The Federal Reserve's role.** In 2002, Fed governor Ben Bernanke said of the Depression: "we did it. We're very sorry." Economists widely agree that the Fed's failure to stop the fall in the money supply made the Depression worse. Historians and economists still weigh other causes, such as weak farm prices, unequal income, and international debts and tariffs.

### The New Deal

President Franklin D. Roosevelt took office in March 1933. Historians often sort New Deal programs into **relief** (immediate help), **recovery** (restarting the economy), and **reform** (preventing future crises).

| Program | Date | Purpose | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) | May 18, 1933 | Recovery and reform: a federal corporation to build dams for flood control, navigation, and cheap electricity | First agency "directed to address the total resource development needs of a major region" |
| Banking Act of 1933 (Glass-Steagall) | June 16, 1933 | Reform: separated commercial and investment banking and created the **FDIC** to insure deposits | Deposit insurance still exists |
| National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA) | June 1933 | Recovery: industry "codes of fair competition" that fixed prices and wages; guaranteed collective bargaining | The National Archives says the codes "did little to help recovery"; the Supreme Court struck down the code system in *Schechter Poultry* (May 1935) |
| National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act | July 5, 1935 | Reform: protected workers' right to organize; created the National Labor Relations Board | Union membership grew |
| **Social Security Act** | Signed August 14, 1935 | Reform and relief: old-age benefits, unemployment insurance, aid to dependent children and to people who are blind or disabled | Still the core of the federal safety net |
| Fair Labor Standards Act | June 25, 1938 | Reform: minimum wage of 25 cents an hour, 44-hour maximum workweek, ban on "oppressive child labor" | Covered industries employing only about one-fifth of the labor force at first |

The economy improved after 1933 but fell back in the "double-dip recession" of 1937, and full employment returned only with wartime production. Many programs also left people out: the first FLSA covered only some industries, and some New Deal programs were run locally in ways that discriminated against Black workers.

### From neutrality to war

- **Aid short of war.** In 1940, after Britain lost many destroyers to the German Navy, Roosevelt traded 50 American destroyers for leases on British bases. In his January 1941 "Four Freedoms" message, he named freedom of speech, freedom of worship, freedom from want, and freedom from fear. The **Lend-Lease Act** (March 11, 1941) let the United States lend or lease war supplies to any nation "vital to the defense of the United States."
- **Pearl Harbor.** The United States had responded to Japanese expansion in China and Indochina with a full embargo and a freeze on Japanese assets. On December 7, 1941, Japan attacked the U.S. naval base at Pearl Harbor, killing or wounding more than 3,500 Americans. The next day Roosevelt called it "a date which will live in infamy," and Congress declared war.
- **The home front.** Executive Order 8802 (June 1941), issued after A. Philip Randolph and other Black leaders pressed the president, banned discrimination in defense industries and federal agencies. Women took millions of industrial jobs. Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942) led to the incarceration of Japanese Americans (Example 2).
- **The Holocaust.** The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum defines it as "the systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by the Nazi German regime and its allies and collaborators," between 1933 and 1945. In 1939 the United States and Canada refused to admit the 937 passengers of the *St. Louis*, almost all Jewish refugees; 254 of them were later killed in the Holocaust.
- **The end of the war.** Germany signed its surrender on May 7, 1945. In July 1945, after the first atomic test, the final decision to use the bomb was made; the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima (August 6, 1945) and Nagasaki (August 9). Japan signed the Instrument of Surrender aboard the USS *Missouri* on September 2, 1945.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Reading Depression numbers carefully

Data from this lesson: the Dow fell nearly 13 percent on October 28, 1929; the money supply fell nearly 30 percent from fall 1930 to winter 1933; unemployment reached about 25 percent in 1932.

1. **Interpret 25 percent.** One in four workers in the labor force was unemployed. If 40 workers lived on a street, about 10 would have had no job.
2. **Compare groups.** Black unemployment "reached as high as 50 percent," about twice the national rate. A national average can hide very different experiences.
3. **Compare dates.** The crash came in 1929, but unemployment peaked in 1932 and the banking system hit bottom in 1933. The crash began the crisis; bank failures and the falling money supply deepened it.
4. **State the limits.** Figures for the 1930s are estimates, and even one source words them differently (*The American Yawp* says unemployment was "hovering around 25 percent" in 1932 and that it "peaked at 25 percent"). Say "about 25 percent," and name your source.

### Example 2: Executive Order 9066

The order (February 19, 1942) authorized the secretary of war and military commanders "to prescribe military areas ... from which any or all persons may be excluded."

1. **Observe:** the text names no ethnic group. The National Archives notes, "Although the language of the order did not specify any ethnic group," the Western Defense Command applied its curfews and removals to Japanese Americans.
2. **Effects:** about 112,000 people were sent to "assembly centers" and then to "relocation centers"; "nearly 70,000 of the evacuees were American citizens," and "there were no charges of disloyalty against any of these citizens" (National Archives).
3. **Court ruling:** in ***Korematsu v. United States*** (1944), the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion order 6–3. The majority said it responded to military needs, not racial prejudice (Oyez).
4. **Later judgment:** in 1988, Public Law 100-383 (the Civil Liberties Act) "acknowledged the injustice of the incarceration, apologized for it," and paid 20,000 dollars to each person who had been incarcerated.

**Takeaway:** a document's neutral wording does not prove neutral enforcement. Compare the text, how it was applied, and how the government later judged it.

### Example 3: Evaluating the New Deal

**Question:** Did the New Deal end the Great Depression?

1. **Evidence it helped:** banking stabilized after the 1933 bank holiday and the creation of deposit insurance; Social Security, the FDIC, and labor laws became lasting parts of American life.
2. **Evidence of limits:** the NIRA codes "did little to help recovery" and were struck down; the economy fell into a second recession in 1937; full employment returned only during the war.
3. **Distinguish goals:** as **relief and reform**, much of the New Deal succeeded and lasted; as **recovery**, it was incomplete.
4. **Claim:** The New Deal did not by itself end the Depression, but it changed the federal government's role permanently by creating programs that still protect bank deposits, retirees, and workers.

### Example 4: The decision to use atomic bombs

1. **The decision:** President Harry Truman approved the bombings in summer 1945, while the U.S. military was planning an invasion of Japan.
2. **The human cost (Department of Energy history):** at Hiroshima, "some 70,000 people probably died as a result of initial blast, heat, and radiation effects," and the toll was "probably over 100,000" by the end of 1945. At Nagasaki, "the best estimate is 40,000 people died initially," approaching 70,000 by January 1946.
3. **Arguments historians examine:** supporters argue the bombs ended the war quickly and avoided an invasion that would have killed many more Americans and Japanese. Critics argue Japan was close to surrender, that alternatives (such as a demonstration or changed surrender terms) were not fully tried, and that the bombs killed mostly civilians. Some historians also point to the Soviet Union's declaration of war on Japan in August 1945.
4. **How to answer a "should" question:** state the decision-makers' goals, the evidence available to them at the time, the consequences, and your own reasoned judgment, while acknowledging the strongest opposing argument. The Truman Library's student inquiry (linked in Sources) is designed for this.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The stock market crash caused the Great Depression by itself."** The crash began the downturn, but banking panics, the fall in the money supply, and other weaknesses turned it into a decade-long depression.
- **"The New Deal ended the Depression."** Recovery was incomplete; full employment returned with war production.
- **"Everyone benefited equally from the New Deal."** Several programs excluded or discriminated against groups of workers, including many Black workers.
- **"The United States entered the war to stop the Holocaust."** The United States entered after Pearl Harbor. U.S. immigration policy limited Jewish refugees, as the *St. Louis* shows.
- **"Japanese Americans were incarcerated because they were spies."** No charges of disloyalty were brought against the citizens who were incarcerated, and Congress later apologized.
- **"The 1920s were good times for everyone."** Farm prices were weak through much of the decade, and the period saw the Klan's revival and harsh immigration restrictions.
- **Boundary: casualty figures are estimates.** Even official sources say "no one will ever know for certain" how many died at Hiroshima. Use ranges and name sources.

## Practice

1. Give one example of change and one example of reaction in the 1920s, each with a specific fact.
2. Explain how bank failures and a falling money supply made the Depression worse. Use the 30 percent figure.
3. Classify each as relief, recovery, or reform (some fit more than one): (a) FDIC; (b) NIRA codes; (c) Social Security old-age benefits; (d) TVA dams.
4. What does Executive Order 9066 say, and how was it applied? Name the Supreme Court case and the 1988 law that judged it.
5. Using a number from the lesson, explain why a national unemployment average can hide differences between groups.
6. List two events that moved the United States from neutrality toward war before December 1941.
7. **Suggested activity: structured inquiry on the atomic bombs (individual or small group, 45 minutes).** Use the Truman Library's "Decision to Drop the Atomic Bomb" inquiry. Write a one-paragraph answer to its key question that includes at least two primary sources and one counterargument. Some materials describe injuries and deaths; your teacher can suggest which documents to use.
