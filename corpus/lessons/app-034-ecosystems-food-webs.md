# APP-03.4 — Ecosystems & Food Webs

In a pond, algae soak up sunlight, tadpoles eat algae, fish eat tadpoles, herons eat fish, and when any of them die, bacteria and fungi break their bodies down into nutrients the algae can use again. Each living thing depends on others and on the sunlight, water, and temperature of the pond. A place like this, with its living things and its non-living surroundings working together, is an **ecosystem**. This lesson is about how ecosystems are put together and how energy and matter move through them.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 4-5. The main text talks to the learner. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding them.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

2. Label organisms as **producers**, **herbivores**, **carnivores**, **omnivores**, or **decomposers**, and draw energy-flow arrows correctly.
3. Use a **food web** to predict what happens when a population is removed or added.
4. Estimate how much energy reaches each level of a food chain and explain why energy flows one way while matter cycles.

## Core ideas

### Biotic and abiotic parts

- **Biotic factors** are the living parts of an ecosystem: plants, animals, fungi, and bacteria.
- **Abiotic factors** are the non-living parts: sunlight, water, temperature, soil, air, and, in water, how much salt or light there is.

Abiotic factors decide what can live in a place. Cacti can survive with very little rain, but most rainforest plants cannot. Fish that need cold water cannot live in a warm, shallow pond.

### Roles in an ecosystem

| Role | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Producer** | Makes its own food from sunlight (photosynthesis) | grass, trees, algae, phytoplankton |
| **Primary consumer (herbivore)** | Eats producers | rabbit, deer, grasshopper, krill |
| **Secondary consumer** | Eats primary consumers; often a **carnivore** | frog that eats grasshoppers, snake |
| **Tertiary consumer** | Eats other consumers | hawk, shark |
| **Omnivore** | Eats both plants and animals | bear, raccoon, people |
| **Decomposer** | Breaks down dead plants, animals, and wastes into nutrients | bacteria, fungi (mushrooms and molds) |

Each feeding step is called a **trophic level**. Producers are the first level.

Some animals, such as earthworms and many beetles, eat bits of dead material. They are often grouped with decomposers because they help break it down. Whatever is left is broken down by bacteria and fungi into nutrients that producers take up again.

### Food chains and food webs

A **food chain** shows one path that food takes:

> grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk

The arrows point **from the food to the eater**, in the direction the energy moves. A good way to read the arrow is "is eaten by."

Real ecosystems have many connected chains. A **food web** shows all of them together. In a food web, a hawk might eat snakes, mice, and rabbits; a mouse might eat seeds and insects. Because most animals have more than one food, a food web is a more accurate model than a single chain. It also helps scientists predict what happens if one population changes.

### Energy flows, matter cycles

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight. Producers store some of it in sugar. When a rabbit eats grass, it uses most of the energy from that grass to move, breathe, keep warm, and stay alive; much of the energy leaves as heat. Only a small part is stored in the rabbit's body, where the next eater can get it.

A classroom **rule of thumb**, often called the **10 percent rule**, says that about one-tenth of the energy at one level ends up in the next level. It is an estimate, not a law. Measured values vary: in one well-studied ecosystem, Silver Springs, about 14.5 percent passed from producers to the next level. Because so much is lost at each step, most food chains have only a few levels. After four to six transfers, there is not enough energy left to support another level.

**Matter** is different. The carbon, water, and nutrients in living things are used again and again. Decomposers return nutrients to the soil and water, and producers take them up again. So we say **energy flows** through an ecosystem in one direction, while **matter cycles**.

### Ecosystem services

People depend on ecosystems for **ecosystem services**: bees and other animals pollinate crops, soil organisms keep soil fertile, and healthy ecosystems help provide clean drinking water and breathable air.

### Biomes

A **biome** is a large area with a similar climate and similar kinds of plants and animals.

| Biome | Climate clues | Living things |
|---|---|---|
| **Tropical rainforest** | Warm all year; very wet months | broad-leaved trees, huge variety of species |
| **Temperate forest** | Cold winters, warm summers; about 75–150 cm of rain and snow a year | trees that drop their leaves in fall (deciduous) |
| **Temperate grassland** (prairie) | Hot summers, cold winters; about 25–75 cm a year | grasses, few trees except along rivers, grazing animals |
| **Desert** | Hot subtropical deserts get less than 30 cm a year | cacti and other plants that save water |
| **Arctic tundra** | Very cold; average winter about −34 °C; a growing season of only about 10–12 weeks; frozen ground called **permafrost** | low plants, lichens |
| **Aquatic** | Water: freshwater (lakes, rivers) or marine (ocean) | algae, fish, plankton |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Building and labeling a food chain

*Illustrative scenario.* In a meadow, grasshoppers eat grass, shrews eat grasshoppers, and owls eat shrews.

1. Find the producer: **grass** (it makes food from sunlight).
2. Who eats the producer? **Grasshopper:** primary consumer, herbivore.
3. Who eats the grasshopper? **Shrew:** secondary consumer, carnivore.
4. Who eats the shrew? **Owl:** tertiary consumer, carnivore.
5. Draw arrows from food to eater: grass → grasshopper → shrew → owl.
6. Add decomposers: when any of them die, bacteria and fungi break them down, returning nutrients to the soil for the grass.

### Example 2: Following the energy

*Illustrative numbers.* Grass in a field stores 10,000 units of energy. Use the 10 percent rule of thumb.

1. Grasshoppers: 10 percent of 10,000 = 10,000 ÷ 10 = **1,000 units**.
2. Shrews: 10 percent of 1,000 = **100 units**.
3. Owls: 10 percent of 100 = **10 units**.
4. Compare: the owls get 10 out of the original 10,000 units, which is 1 out of every 1,000.
5. **Boundary check:** if instead 15 percent passed on at each step, grasshoppers would get 1,500, shrews 225, and owls about 34 units. The pattern, a sharp drop at each level, is the same even though the numbers change.

This is why there are many more grasshoppers than owls in a field: the top of the chain has the least energy to support it.

### Example 3: What happens if rabbits disappear?

*Illustrative food web.* Grass is eaten by rabbits and mice. Foxes eat rabbits and mice. Hawks eat mice and snakes. Snakes eat mice.

1. Rabbits are removed (for example, by a disease).
2. **Grass:** fewer animals eat it, so the grass will probably grow taller or spread.
3. **Foxes:** they lose one food. They might eat more mice, or some foxes might move away or have fewer young.
4. **Mice:** if foxes eat more of them, mice may decrease. But mice now have more grass, which could help them. The web shows two forces pulling in opposite directions.
5. **Hawks and snakes:** if mice decrease, hawks and snakes have less food too.

Conclusion: removing one population can affect organisms it never directly touched. Because the web has many links, the exact result depends on how big each effect is, so scientists study real ecosystems to find out.

### Example 4: Which biome?

*Illustrative data.* Place A gets 20 cm of rain a year and is hot. Place B gets 50 cm a year, has hot summers and cold winters, and is covered in grasses with trees only along streams.

1. Place A: less than 30 cm a year and hot. **Desert.**
2. Place B: between 25 and 75 cm, strong seasons, grasses with few trees. **Temperate grassland.**
3. Check: rainfall alone is not enough. A cold, dry place could be tundra, so look at temperature too.

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"The arrow means 'eats,' so it points to the food."** In food chains and webs, arrows point from the food to the eater, the way energy moves.
- **"Energy is recycled like matter."** Matter cycles, but energy flows one way. Most of it leaves as heat at each level, and new energy must keep arriving from the Sun.
- **"Decomposers are not important."** Without decomposers, dead material would pile up and nutrients would stay locked inside it. Producers would run out of nutrients.
- **"The top predator is the most common animal."** Usually it is the least common, because the top level has the least energy available.
- **"The 10 percent rule is exact."** It is a rough estimate. Real transfers vary with the kinds of organisms and ecosystems.
- **"Every ecosystem runs on sunlight."** Most do, but not all. In dark caves and around hot-water vents on the deep ocean floor, some bacteria make food using energy from chemicals instead of light, and other organisms feed on them.

## Practice

1. Sort into biotic or abiotic: sunlight, mushroom, rainfall, earthworm, rock, temperature, oak tree.
2. In the chain algae → tadpole → fish → heron, name the producer, the herbivore, and the top consumer.
3. A bear eats berries and salmon. Which role describes the bear?
4. Fix the mistake: a student drew "hawk → mouse → seeds." What is wrong, and what is the correct chain?
5. *Illustrative numbers:* Plants store 5,000 units of energy. Using the 10 percent rule of thumb, how much reaches the herbivores? The carnivores that eat them?
6. In the web in Example 3, what might happen to the grass if foxes were removed instead of rabbits? Explain in two steps.
7. Name one ecosystem service and explain how it helps people.
8. A place has very cold winters, a short growing season, low plants, and frozen ground under the surface all year. Which biome is it?
