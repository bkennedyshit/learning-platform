# APP-04.4 — Matter — Properties & States

**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space. Your desk, the water in a glass, and the air in a balloon are all matter. Light and sound are not matter; they are forms of energy. This lesson describes the states matter comes in, the properties we use to tell materials apart, how matter changes from one state to another, and how mixtures can be separated.

The course plan places this lesson in grades 4-6. The **Adult note** boxes are for the person guiding the learner.

## Prerequisites

From [APP-04.2 — Energy — Forms & Transfers](/app-042-energy-forms-transfers): thermal energy and the idea that it flows from hot to cold. From [APP-02.7 — Measurement & Data](/app-027-measurement-data): grams, milliliters, and volume as length × width × height.

> **Adult note:** A kitchen scale that reads in grams, a measuring cup or graduated cylinder marked in milliliters, a ruler, ice cubes, a sealed zip bag, sand, table salt, a coffee filter, and a magnet cover the activities. One milliliter (mL) holds the same volume as one cubic centimeter (cm³).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson the learner should be able to:

1. Compare solids, liquids, and gases using shape, volume, and how their particles move.
2. Calculate density from mass and volume and use it to predict whether something floats in water.
3. Name the changes of state and explain why mass stays the same during them.
4. Tell mixtures from pure substances and choose a way to separate a mixture.

## Core ideas

### States of matter and the particle model

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms and molecules) that are always moving. How tightly they are held together decides the state.

| State | Shape | Volume | Particles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | Keeps its own shape | Fixed | Packed close, vibrate in place |
| Liquid | Takes the shape of its container | Fixed | Close, but slide past each other |
| Gas | Fills its whole container | Changes to fill the container | Far apart, move fast in all directions |

A fourth state, **plasma**, is a very hot gas containing many electrically charged particles. Plasma is found inside stars, including the Sun, and in other very high-temperature places such as lightning.

### Physical properties

A **physical property** can be observed or measured without changing what the substance is: color, hardness, mass, volume, density, whether it conducts electricity or heat, whether a magnet attracts it, and whether it dissolves in water.

- **Mass** is the amount of matter, measured in grams (g) or kilograms (kg).
- **Volume** is the amount of space taken up, measured in milliliters (mL), liters (L), or cubic centimeters (cm³).
- **Density** is how much mass is packed into each unit of volume:

$$\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}$$

Water's density is about 1 g/cm³. An object with a density **less** than water floats in it; an object with a density **greater** than water sinks. Ice (about 0.92 g/cm³) floats. Iron (about 7.9 g/cm³) sinks. Every piece of the same pure substance has the same density at the same temperature, whether it is a tiny chip or a big block, so density helps identify materials.

### Changes of state

Adding thermal energy makes particles move faster; removing it slows them down.

| Change | From → to | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | solid → liquid | Ice cream on a hot day |
| Freezing | liquid → solid | Water in an ice tray |
| Evaporation / boiling | liquid → gas | A puddle drying; a pot of water boiling |
| Condensation | gas → liquid | Drops forming on a cold glass |
| Sublimation | solid → gas | Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) turning straight into gas |
| Deposition | gas → solid | Frost forming on a cold window |

A change of state is a **physical change**: water is still water whether it is ice, liquid, or vapor. The **mass stays the same**. If a sealed bag holds 100 g of ice, it holds 100 g of water after the ice melts.

### Mixtures and pure substances

- A **pure substance** has a fixed makeup. An **element** (such as iron, oxygen, or gold) cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical changes. A **compound** (such as water or table salt) is two or more elements chemically joined.
- A **mixture** is two or more substances mixed but not chemically joined. In a **heterogeneous** mixture you can see the different parts (sand in water, a salad). In a **homogeneous** mixture, also called a **solution**, it looks the same throughout (salt fully dissolved in water).

Because the parts of a mixture keep their own properties, we can separate them using those properties:

| Method | Property it uses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sieving | Particle size | Pebbles from sand |
| Magnet | Attraction to a magnet | Iron filings from sand |
| Filtration | A solid does not dissolve, so it is caught by a filter | Sand from water |
| Evaporation | The liquid turns to gas; the dissolved solid stays | Salt from salt water |
| Distillation | Liquids boil at different temperatures; the vapor is collected and condensed | Pure water from salt water |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Density of a metal block

*Illustrative example.* A rectangular block measures 2 cm × 3 cm × 4 cm and has a mass of 216 g.

1. Volume = 2 cm × 3 cm × 4 cm = 24 cm³.
2. Density = 216 g ÷ 24 cm³ = 9 g/cm³.
3. Compare with water (1 g/cm³): 9 is greater than 1, so the block sinks.
4. Compare with a reference table: copper is about 9.0 g/cm³, so the block could be copper.
5. Boundary: density alone does not prove what it is. Other materials may have similar densities, so scientists check other properties too.

### Example 2: Volume by water displacement

*Illustrative example.* A rock is too bumpy to measure with a ruler. A graduated cylinder holds 50 mL of water. After the rock is lowered in, the water reads 58 mL. The rock's mass is 21 g.

1. The rock pushed aside its own volume of water: 58 mL − 50 mL = 8 mL.
2. Since 1 mL = 1 cm³, the rock's volume is 8 cm³.
3. Density = 21 g ÷ 8 cm³ = 2.625 g/cm³, about 2.6 g/cm³.
4. That is more than 1 g/cm³, so the rock sinks, which matches what happened when it was lowered in.

### Example 3: Separating sand, salt, and iron filings

*Illustrative procedure.* A mixture contains sand, table salt, and iron filings.

1. **Magnet:** hold a magnet (wrapped in a plastic bag so the filings come off easily) over the mixture. Only the iron is attracted. Remove it.
2. **Add water and stir:** the salt dissolves; the sand does not.
3. **Filter:** pour through a coffee filter. The sand stays in the filter. Salt water passes through.
4. **Evaporate:** leave the salt water in a shallow dish in a sunny spot for a few days. The water evaporates and the salt crystals remain.
5. Why it works: each step uses a different property: magnetism, solubility, particle size, and boiling (evaporation).

## Common misconceptions and boundary cases

- **"Gases have no mass."** Air is matter. A basketball weighs a little more after it is pumped up, because air was added.
- **"Heavy things sink and light things float."** What matters is density compared with the liquid. A huge log floats; a small steel nail sinks.
- **"The bubbles in boiling water are air."** They are water vapor, the gas form of water, forming inside the hot liquid.
- **"When sugar dissolves, it disappears."** The sugar is still there, spread out among the water particles. The water tastes sweet, the total mass is the same, and evaporating the water would leave the sugar behind.
- **"Drops on the outside of a cold glass leaked through."** They condensed from water vapor in the air touching the cold glass. A glass of cold juice gets clear water drops, not juice drops.
- **"Melting and dissolving are the same thing."** Melting is one substance changing from solid to liquid because of heat. Dissolving is one substance spreading out into another, like salt into water.

## Practice

1. Name the state: (a) has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container (b) fills any container completely (c) keeps its own shape.
2. *Illustrative example:* A block is 5 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm and has a mass of 30 g. Find its volume and density. Does it float in water?
3. *Illustrative example:* A cylinder reads 40 mL. After a stone is added it reads 46 mL. The stone's mass is 18 g. What is its density?
4. Name each change: (a) a puddle dries up (b) frost forms on grass overnight (c) butter softens and runs in a hot pan
5. A sealed bag holds 120 g of ice. After it all melts, what is the mass of the water? Why?
6. Is each one an element, a compound, or a mixture? (a) oxygen (b) salt water (c) pure water (d) trail mix
7. How would you separate small pebbles from salt water, and then get the salt back?

<details>
<summary>Answer key and self-check</summary>

1. (a) liquid (b) gas (c) solid
2. Volume = 5 × 2 × 2 = 20 cm³. Density = 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 g/cm³. It sinks, because 1.5 is greater than 1.
3. Volume = 46 − 40 = 6 mL = 6 cm³. Density = 18 ÷ 6 = 3 g/cm³.
4. (a) evaporation (b) deposition (c) melting
5. 120 g. Melting is a change of state; no matter entered or left the sealed bag.
6. (a) element (b) mixture (a solution) (c) compound (d) mixture
7. Pour it through a sieve or filter to catch the pebbles; then evaporate the water to leave the salt behind.

**Adult self-check:** Ask the learner to write the units at every step of questions 2 and 3 (cm × cm × cm gives cm³; g ÷ cm³ gives g/cm³). For questions 4 and 6, ask them to name the property or clue they used.

</details>

> **Safety note:** Let an adult handle anything hot, including boiling water. Use the sunny-window method for evaporation rather than a stove. Do not handle dry ice; it is cold enough to injure skin and should only be observed, if at all, with an adult. Do not taste materials in a science activity, even familiar ones.
