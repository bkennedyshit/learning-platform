# 41.1 — Phonetics, Liaison & The French Sound System

*Back to [[Bill's Vault/05-Knowledge_Foundation/09 - Learning/41 - French/Subject_Plan]] | [[41.2 - Nouns, Articles, Gender & Adjective Agreement|41.2 →]]*

> **Chapter goal:** Crack the French sound-spelling code. Master nasal vowels, silent letters, liaison rules, and elision so that spoken and written French become two sides of the same coin — not two separate systems.

---

## 1. Why Phonetics Must Come First

French is **not phonetically transparent** — unlike Italian or Spanish, you cannot reliably predict pronunciation from spelling without learning the system. English speakers who skip this chapter and proceed directly to grammar develop **permanent fossil errors**: reading *un* as "un" (English), *en* as "en" (English), pronouncing final consonants that should be silent.

The French phonetic system is **highly systematic** once you know the rules. There are roughly 12 rules that account for 95% of pronunciation cases.

---

## 2. The French Vowels (Oral)

French has **pure vowels** — unlike English, no diphthongs (no "glide" from one vowel position to another). Hold the vowel shape fixed throughout.

| Symbol | French example | Approximate English anchor |
|--------|---------------|---------------------------|
| /a/ | *patte*, *là* | "father" (but shorter) |
| /ɑ/ | *pâte*, *bas* | "father" (back of mouth) — merging with /a/ in modern French |
| /e/ | *été*, *parler* | "may" (but pure — no glide to /ɪ/) |
| /ɛ/ | *fête*, *être*, *est* | "bed" |
| /i/ | *vie*, *midi* | "seen" (pure) |
| /o/ | *beau*, *côté* | "go" (but pure — no /ʊ/ glide) |
| /ɔ/ | *or*, *porte* | "sort" |
| /u/ | *doux*, *tout* | "boot" (lips rounded, tongue back) |
| /y/ | *tu*, *une* | No English equivalent — say /i/ with rounded lips |
| /ø/ | *feu*, *nœud* | No English equivalent — say /e/ with rounded lips |
| /œ/ | *peur*, *sœur* | No English equivalent — say /ɛ/ with rounded lips |
| /ə/ | *le*, *me*, *que* | "the" (schwa; often silent in connected speech) |

> **The rounded front vowels (/y/, /ø/, /œ/)** are the hardest for English speakers. Practice: say /i/ and round your lips as if saying /u/ → you get /y/. This is *tu* and *une*.

---

## 3. Nasal Vowels — The Signature French Sounds

Nasal vowels are formed with air flowing through **both mouth and nose** simultaneously. There are **4 nasal vowels** in modern standard French:

| Symbol | Spelling patterns | Example words |
|--------|------------------|--------------|
| /ɑ̃/ | an, am, en, em | *dans, enfant, temps, champion* |
| /ɛ̃/ | in, im, ain, aim, ein, (un, um in some dialects) | *vin, main, sein, brun* |
| /ɔ̃/ | on, om | *bon, ombre, monde* |
| /œ̃/ | un, um | *brun, lundi* — **merging into /ɛ̃/** in Parisian French |

> ⚠️ **Critical rule:** The nasal vowel is **only produced when n/m is at the end of a syllable or before a consonant.** When n/m is followed by a vowel or another n/m, the vowel is **oral** and the n/m is fully pronounced: *bonne* /bɔn/ (not nasal), *venir* /vənir/ (not nasal), but *bon* /bɔ̃/ (nasal).

**Test:** *an* (nasal /ɑ̃/) vs. *Anne* (oral /an/) — the double-n breaks the nasal.

---

## 4. Semi-Vowels (Glides)

| Symbol | Spelling | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| /j/ | i/y before vowel | *yeux*, *bien*, *fille* |
| /w/ | ou before vowel, w in borrowings | *oui*, *Louis*, *wifi* |
| /ɥ/ | u before vowel | *nuit*, *lui*, *puis* |

---

## 5. Consonants — Key French Specifics

### The French R: /ʁ/ (Uvular fricative)
- Produced at the back of the throat (uvula), **not** with the tongue tip like Spanish or Italian
- Practice: gargle gently; that's approximately the sound
- Common across all positions: *rue*, *mère*, *partir*, *rouge*

### Silent Final Consonants
**Default rule: final written consonants are silent.** Exceptions exist (see below).

| Letters usually silent at end | Exceptions (always pronounced) |
|------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| -s, -t, -d, -z, -x, -p | -c (lac /lak/, sac /sak/) |
| -e is always silent | -f (chef /ʃɛf/, bœuf /bœf/) |
| | -l (bal /bal/, sel /sɛl/) |
| | -r after consonant (car /kaʁ/, soir /swaʁ/) |

**CaReFuL** mnemonic: the letters **C, R, F, L** are usually pronounced at the end of a word.

---

## 6. Elision

**Elision** = dropping a final vowel (usually *-e* or *-a*) before a word beginning with a vowel or mute-h, replacing it with an apostrophe.

| Original | Before vowel/mute-h | Example |
|----------|---------------------|---------|
| *le* | *l'* | *l'ami, l'heure* |
| *la* | *l'* | *l'amie, l'histoire* |
| *je* | *j'* | *j'aime, j'habite* |
| *me* | *m'* | *il m'appelle* |
| *de* | *d'* | *d'accord* |
| *que* | *qu'* | *qu'il, qu'est-ce que* |
| *si* | *s'* (only before *il/ils*) | *s'il vous plaît* |

---

## 7. Liaison — The Core French Rule

**Liaison** = when the normally silent final consonant of a word is **pronounced** and attached to the following word that begins with a vowel (or mute-h).

The linking consonant behaves as if it starts the following word: *"les amis"* → /le.z‿a.mi/ (not /le a.mi/).

### 7.1 Obligatory Liaison (always do it)
- Article + noun: *les amis* /lez‿ami/, *des enfants* /dez‿ɑ̃fɑ̃/
- Adjective (prenominal) + noun: *petit enfant* /pəti.t‿ɑ̃fɑ̃/
- Subject pronoun + verb: *nous avons* /nuz‿avɔ̃/, *ils ont* /ilz‿ɔ̃/
- Monosyllabic prepositions + object: *en été* /ɑ̃n‿ete/, *dans un* /dɑ̃z‿œ̃/
- *très/bien/fort* + adjective: *très utile* /trɛz‿ytil/

### 7.2 Forbidden Liaison (never do it)
- After a noun (singular or plural) + verb: *les enfants_ arrivent* (no liaison)
- After *et* (and): *et_ après* (never liaison after *et*)
- Before an aspirate-h: *les haricots* /le a.ʁi.ko/ — aspirate-h blocks liaison
- After a name/proper noun: *Paris_ est belle* (no liaison — though commonly broken by learners)

### 7.3 Optional Liaison (style-dependent)
- Verb + object: *il parle_ à* (spoken: usually no liaison; formal: possible)
- Noun phrase + adjective: *un enfant_ adorable* (formal speech: liaison; casual: no)

### 7.4 Mute-h vs. Aspirate-h
French words beginning with *h* are either:
- **Mute-h (h muet):** behaves as if there's no h; elision + liaison apply: *l'homme*, *les hommes* /lez‿ɔm/
- **Aspirate-h (h aspiré):** blocks elision + liaison: *le hibou* (NOT *l'hibou*), *les haricots* /le a.ʁi.ko/

Dictionary entries mark aspirate-h with an asterisk (*)  or dagger (†). There's no pronunciation difference — it's purely a grammatical category.

---

## 8. The Mute-e (E Caduc / Schwa)

The written *-e* at the end of a word or syllable is often silent in connected speech:

- *"je ne sais pas"* → */ʒ(ə) n(ə) sɛ pa/* (the two /ə/ sounds often drop in casual speech)
- *"une femme"* → /yn fam/ (the -e in *une* may reduce; the -e in *femme* is always silent)

**Rule of 3:** In a sequence of syllables with mute-e, no more than two consecutive mute-e can be dropped. At least one must be retained for pronounceability.

---

## 9. Stress & Intonation

- French stress falls **always on the last syllable of a rhythmic group** (not individual words)
- *"Je vais au cinéma"* → stress on the last syllable: *-ma*
- No secondary stress within words — French sounds syllable-timed (equal weight per syllable), unlike English (stress-timed)
- **Intonation:** declarative sentences fall in pitch at the end; yes/no questions rise; listed items rise until the last, which falls

---

## 10. Common Misconceptions / Where Students Fail

1. **Reading French like Spanish:** Spanish has transparent phonetics; French does not. *eau* = /o/, not "e-a-u". *oi* = /wa/. Learn the digraphs as units.
2. **Forgetting that -s is usually silent:** *"tu parles"* and *"tu parle"* sound identical. Grammar determines meaning, not pronunciation.
3. **Not applying liaison in obligatory contexts:** *"vous avez"* must be /vuz‿ave/ — skipping liaison marks you as a non-speaker immediately.
4. **Treating all h-words the same:** *"l'homme"* (mute-h, correct) vs. *"le hibou"* (aspirate-h, correct) — aspirate-h must be memorized per word.
