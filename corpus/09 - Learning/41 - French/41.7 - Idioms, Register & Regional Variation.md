## 1. High-Frequency French Idioms

These expressions are opaque to literal translation. Knowing them separates intermediate learners from those who sound natural.

### Body-Based Idioms

| Idiom | Literal | Actual meaning |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| *avoir le cafard* | to have the cockroach | to feel down / depressed |
| *avoir le coup de foudre* | to have the lightning bolt | to fall in love at first sight |
| *avoir du pain sur la planche* | to have bread on the board | to have a lot of work to do |
| *avoir les pieds sur terre* | to have feet on the ground | to be down-to-earth |
| *avoir la tête dans les nuages* | to have one's head in the clouds | to be a dreamer / distracted |
| *casser les pieds de qqn* | to break someone's feet | to annoy someone |
| *ne pas avoir la langue dans sa poche* | not to have one's tongue in one's pocket | to be outspoken / never at a loss for words |
| *mettre les pieds dans le plat* | to put one's feet in the dish | to put one's foot in it |
| *avoir le bras long* | to have a long arm | to have influence / connections |
| *coûter les yeux de la tête* | to cost the eyes from the head | to cost an arm and a leg |
| *se creuser la tête* | to dig into one's head | to rack one's brain |
| *tourner autour du pot* | to turn around the pot | to beat around the bush |

### Animal & Object Idioms

| Idiom | Literal | Actual meaning |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| *poser un lapin à qqn* | to stand a rabbit up for someone | to stand someone up |
| *avoir d'autres chats à fouetter* | to have other cats to whip | to have bigger fish to fry |
| *quand les poules auront des dents* | when hens have teeth | when pigs fly |
| *il pleut des cordes* | it's raining ropes | it's raining cats and dogs |
| *prendre la poudre d'escampette* | to take escape powder | to make a run for it / to flee |
| *arriver comme un cheveu sur la soupe* | to arrive like a hair in the soup | to arrive at an awkward moment |
| *chercher la petite bête* | to look for the little beast | to nitpick |
| *se noyer dans un verre d'eau* | to drown in a glass of water | to make a mountain out of a molehill |
| *tomber dans les pommes* | to fall into the apples | to faint |

### Everyday Colloquial Expressions

| Expression | Meaning | Register |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| *C'est pas grave.* | It's not a big deal / No worries. | casual |
| *Ça marche.* | That works / OK / Sounds good. | casual |
| *C'est nul.* | That's rubbish / terrible. | casual |
| *Je m'en fous.* | I don't care / I couldn't care less. | casual (mild vulgarity) |
| *T'as raison.* | You're right. | casual (*tu as*) |
| *Ça y est !* | That's it! / Done! / Finally! | casual |
| *N'importe quoi !* | Whatever! / Nonsense! | casual |
| *C'est nickel.* | It's perfect / spotless. | casual |
| *Se débrouiller* | to manage / get by / figure it out | neutral |
| *Ça ne casse pas trois pattes à un canard.* | It doesn't break three legs off a duck. | It's nothing special. |

## 2. The Register Spectrum

French has a wider and more clearly defined register spectrum than English. Every competent speaker shifts fluidly between levels:

| Level | Name | Characteristics | Context |
|-------|------|----------------|---------|
| **Soutenu / littéraire** | Elevated/literary | Passé simple, subjunctive in all contexts, no contractions, long sentences, *nous* for we | Written literature, formal speeches, legal texts |
| **Standard / soigné** | Standard | Full grammar, ne maintained, futur simple used, *nous* used | News, formal letters, educated conversation |
| **Courant / familier** | Everyday | *ne* often dropped, *on* for *nous*, *aller + inf* for future, some liaisons skipped | Normal adult conversation, friends, casual settings |
| **Familier / populaire** | Casual/popular | Many contractions, Verlan, *t'as/t'es* for *tu as/tu es*, clipped words, *c'est + adj* for emphasis | Among friends, youth, informal media |
| **Argot / vulgaire** | Slang/vulgar | Strong language, Verlan, backslang, code-switching | Specific social groups, informal media |

### Key Spoken vs. Written Divergences

| Written/formal | Spoken/casual | Meaning |
|---------------|--------------|---------|
| *Je ne sais pas.* | *Je sais pas. / Chais pas.* | I don't know |
| *Tu as* | *T'as* | You have |
| *Tu es* | *T'es* | You are |
| *Il y a* | *Y'a* | There is/are |
| *Nous allons* | *On va* | We're going |
| *Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?* | *Tu fais quoi ?* | What are you doing? |
| *Je suis en train de travailler.* | *Je travaille.* | I'm working (right now) |
| *Quelque chose* | *Quelque chose / un truc* | something / a thing |
| *Très bien* | *Super / vachement bien* | Very good |

## 3. Verlan — French Back-Slang

Verlan (*l'envers* = the reverse, itself a Verlan word: *l'en-vers → verlan*) is a slang register formed by inverting syllables. It originated in the French banlieues (suburbs) in the 1970s–80s and is now embedded in mainstream youth French. You will hear it constantly in films, rap music, and informal speech.

**How it works:** Reverse the syllables of a word (often with phonological adjustments):

| Standard | Verlan | Meaning |
|----------|--------|---------|
| *femme* | **meuf** | woman |
| *mec* | **keum** | guy / dude |
| *fou* | **ouf** | crazy / intense |
| *flic* | **keuf** | cop |
| *l'envers* | **verlan** | the reverse |
| *à l'envers* | **laisse béton* (← *laisse tomber*)* | forget it / drop it |
| *bizarre* | **zarbi** | weird |
| *louche* | **chelou** | sketchy / shady |
| *cool* | **looc** | cool |
| *cité* | **téci** | housing project |
| *français* | **céfran** | French |
| *arabe* | **rebeu** | Arab (originally *beur*, then re-verlanised) |

> Many Verlan words have been absorbed into standard colloquial French — *meuf, ouf, chelou* are used by all age groups in casual contexts.

## 4. Québec French

Québec French is a fully developed dialect with its own vocabulary, pronunciation, and grammatical features — not a "corrupted" form of European French, but an independent branch that preserved features of 17th-century Norman and Parisian French.

### Key Differences — Vocabulary

| Québec | Metropolitan French | Meaning |
|--------|-------------------|---------|
| *char* | *voiture* | car |
| *magasiner* | *faire du shopping* | to go shopping |
| *dépanneur* | *épicerie de nuit / supérette* | convenience store |
| *blonde* | *petite amie* | girlfriend |
| *chum* | *copain / petit ami* | boyfriend / buddy |
| *fin de semaine* | *week-end* | weekend |
| *pogner* | *attraper / avoir du succès* | to catch / to be popular |
| *frette* | *froid* | cold |
| *pantoute* (= *pas en tout*) | *pas du tout* | not at all |
| *tantôt* | varies (can mean earlier today or later today) | recently or soon |
| *Chu* (= *je suis*) | *je suis* | I am |
| *T'sais?* (= *tu sais?*) | *tu sais?* | you know? |

### Key Differences — Pronunciation

- **Affrication of /t/ and /d/ before /i/ and /y/:** *tu* = /tsy/, *dire* = /dzir/ — a distinctive feature absent from European French
- **Vowel lengthening:** stressed vowels before voiced consonants are noticeably longer
- **Diphthongization** of some vowels in informal speech
- Preserved the *-r* in many positions where metropolitan French weakens it

### Key Grammar Notes

- *Tu* is used very freely even in relatively formal contexts — the vous/tu distinction is less rigid
- *On* is used for *nous* even in formal writing far more than in France
- Archaisms preserved: *icitte* (ici), *sus* (sur), second person plural forms

## 5. Other Major Francophone Varieties

| Region | Key features | Exposure |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Belgian French** | *septante* (70), *nonante* (90) instead of *soixante-dix, quatre-vingt-dix*; *déjeuner/dîner/souper* shifted by one meal; softer r | InBev/RTBF media |
| **Swiss French** | *huitante* (80) in some cantons; Swiss-German influence on vocabulary; calm, careful articulation | Swiss media, banking/legal documents |
| **Maghrebi French** (Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia) | Heavy Arabic loanwords; code-switching; *darija* influence; very common in France itself | French films set in banlieues |
| **West African French** (Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire) | Highly rhythmic; local language influence; *nouchi* (Ivorian youth slang); often clearer vowels than Parisian | Growing importance in global French-speaking population |

## 6. Tu vs. Vous — The Register Choice

This is a genuine cultural minefield with real social consequences:

| Always *vous* | Use *vous* unless invited to switch | *Tu* is appropriate |
|--------------|-------------------------------------|---------------------|
| Strangers over 30 | Professors, doctors, shopkeepers | Friends, family, children |
| Authority figures | Work colleagues (initially) | Peers who have said *on se tutoie ?* |
| Formal service contexts | Neighbours (until signalled) | Social media interactions, youth |

> In France, switching to *tu* without invitation is considered either rude, presumptuous, or (between adults of different ages) patronising. Québec is far more relaxed about *tu*. Belgium follows French norms.
