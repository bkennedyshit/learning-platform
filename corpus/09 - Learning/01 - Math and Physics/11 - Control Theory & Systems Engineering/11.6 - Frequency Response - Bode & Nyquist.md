# 11.6 — Frequency Response: Bode & Nyquist

> *"The frequency response is the system's fingerprint — it tells you everything about how the system amplifies or attenuates signals at every frequency, and whether feedback will stabilize or destabilize it."*
> — Hendrik W. Bode

Frequency response methods evaluate system behavior by examining how it responds to sinusoidal inputs across all frequencies. Bode plots provide intuitive graphical design tools; the Nyquist criterion gives a rigorous stability test for systems with time delays and non-minimum phase behavior that root locus cannot easily handle.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Compute and sketch Bode magnitude and phase plots using asymptotic approximations.
2. Identify gain margin, phase margin, gain crossover, and phase crossover frequencies.
3. State and apply the Nyquist stability criterion.
4. Count encirclements of the $-1$ point on the Nyquist plot.
5. Determine stability of systems with time delay using frequency response.
6. Design lead and lag compensators using Bode plot specifications.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 11.6.1 — Frequency Response Function

The **frequency response** of an LTI system with transfer function $G(s)$ is obtained by evaluating on the imaginary axis:

$$
G(j\omega) = |G(j\omega)|\, e^{j\angle G(j\omega)}
$$

For a sinusoidal input $u(t) = A\sin(\omega t)$, the steady-state output is:

$$
y_{ss}(t) = A|G(j\omega)|\sin(\omega t + \angle G(j\omega))
$$

### Definition 11.6.2 — Bode Plot

A **Bode plot** consists of two graphs plotted against $\log_{10}\omega$:
1. **Magnitude**: $20\log_{10}|G(j\omega)|$ in decibels (dB)
2. **Phase**: $\angle G(j\omega)$ in degrees

### Definition 11.6.3 — Gain Crossover Frequency

The **gain crossover frequency** $\omega_{gc}$ is where the magnitude equals 0 dB:

$$
|G(j\omega_{gc})| = 1 \quad (0 \text{ dB})
$$

### Definition 11.6.4 — Phase Crossover Frequency

The **phase crossover frequency** $\omega_{pc}$ is where the phase equals $-180°$:

$$
\angle G(j\omega_{pc}) = -180°
$$

### Definition 11.6.5 — Gain Margin (GM)

$$
GM = \frac{1}{|G(j\omega_{pc})|} = -20\log_{10}|G(j\omega_{pc})| \text{ dB}
$$

The gain margin is how much additional gain (in dB) can be added before the system becomes unstable.

### Definition 11.6.6 — Phase Margin (PM)

$$
PM = 180° + \angle G(j\omega_{gc})
$$

The phase margin is how much additional phase lag can be tolerated before instability.

### Definition 11.6.7 — Nyquist Plot

The **Nyquist plot** is the polar plot of $G(j\omega)$ as $\omega$ varies from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ (or equivalently, the image of the Nyquist contour under the mapping $G(s)$).

### Definition 11.6.8 — Nyquist Contour

The **Nyquist contour** $\Gamma$ encloses the entire right half-plane: it consists of the imaginary axis from $-j\infty$ to $+j\infty$ plus a semicircle of infinite radius in the RHP. Indentations are made around any open-loop poles on the $j\omega$-axis.

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 11.6.A1 — Bode Plot Additivity

Since $|G_1 G_2| = |G_1||G_2|$ and $\angle(G_1 G_2) = \angle G_1 + \angle G_2$, in dB:

$$
20\log|G_1 G_2| = 20\log|G_1| + 20\log|G_2|
$$

Bode plots of cascaded systems are obtained by **adding** individual magnitude (dB) and phase plots.

### Axiom 11.6.A2 — Minimum Phase Systems

A system is **minimum phase** if it has no zeros or poles in the RHP and no time delay. For minimum phase systems, the phase is uniquely determined by the magnitude (Bode's gain-phase relationship). Non-minimum phase systems have additional phase lag.

### Axiom 11.6.A3 — Stability Margins as Design Specs

For robust stability, typical design requirements are:
- $GM \geq 6$ dB (factor of 2 in gain)
- $PM \geq 30°$ to $60°$

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas (Bode Plot Building Blocks)

### Lemma 11.6.1 — Constant Gain $K$

$$
|K|_{dB} = 20\log_{10}|K|, \quad \angle K = 0° \text{ (if } K > 0\text{)}, \quad 180° \text{ (if } K < 0\text{)}
$$

### Lemma 11.6.2 — Integrator $1/s$

$$
\left|\frac{1}{j\omega}\right|_{dB} = -20\log_{10}\omega, \quad \angle\frac{1}{j\omega} = -90°
$$

Magnitude: straight line with slope $-20$ dB/decade passing through 0 dB at $\omega = 1$.

### Lemma 11.6.3 — First-Order Pole $\dfrac{1}{1 + s/\omega_b}$

Break frequency: $\omega_b$. Asymptotic approximation:
- $\omega \ll \omega_b$: $|G| \approx 0$ dB, $\angle G \approx 0°$
- $\omega = \omega_b$: $|G| = -3$ dB, $\angle G = -45°$
- $\omega \gg \omega_b$: slope $-20$ dB/dec, $\angle G \to -90°$

Phase transition: from $0°$ to $-90°$ over the decade $0.1\omega_b$ to $10\omega_b$.

### Lemma 11.6.4 — First-Order Zero $1 + s/\omega_b$

Mirror of the pole: $+20$ dB/dec slope above $\omega_b$, phase goes from $0°$ to $+90°$.

### Lemma 11.6.5 — Second-Order Underdamped Factor

$$
\frac{1}{1 + 2\zeta(s/\omega_n) + (s/\omega_n)^2}
$$

- Below $\omega_n$: 0 dB, 0° phase
- At $\omega_n$: resonant peak of $\dfrac{1}{2\zeta}$ (i.e., $-20\log(2\zeta)$ dB), phase = $-90°$
- Above $\omega_n$: $-40$ dB/dec, phase → $-180°$

### Lemma 11.6.6 — Time Delay $e^{-sT}$

$$
|e^{-j\omega T}| = 1 \quad (0 \text{ dB}), \quad \angle e^{-j\omega T} = -\omega T \text{ rad} = -\frac{180\omega T}{\pi} \text{ degrees}
$$

Time delay adds no magnitude change but adds linearly increasing phase lag.

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 11.6.1 — The Nyquist Stability Criterion

Let $L(s) = C(s)G(s)H(s)$ be the open-loop transfer function with $P$ poles in the open RHP. Let $N$ be the number of **clockwise encirclements** of the point $-1 + j0$ by the Nyquist plot of $L(j\omega)$ as $\omega$ traverses the complete Nyquist contour.

Then the number of closed-loop poles in the RHP is:

$$
Z = N + P
$$

The closed-loop system is stable if and only if $Z = 0$, i.e., $N = -P$ (counter-clockwise encirclements equal the number of open-loop RHP poles).

### Theorem 11.6.2 — Simplified Nyquist Criterion (Stable Open-Loop)

If the open-loop system is stable ($P = 0$), the closed-loop system is stable if and only if the Nyquist plot of $L(j\omega)$ does **not** encircle the $-1$ point.

### Theorem 11.6.3 — Bode Stability Criterion (Minimum Phase Systems)

For a minimum-phase open-loop system, the closed-loop is stable if and only if:
- At the gain crossover frequency: $PM > 0°$
- At the phase crossover frequency: $GM > 0$ dB

### Theorem 11.6.4 — Relationship Between Phase Margin and Damping Ratio

For a second-order system, the phase margin is approximately related to damping ratio by:

$$
PM \approx 100\zeta \text{ (degrees)}, \quad \text{for } \zeta < 0.7
$$

More precisely: $\zeta \approx \dfrac{PM}{100}$ for $PM < 70°$.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 — Bode Plot Construction: $G(s) = \dfrac{100}{s(s/10 + 1)}$

**Step 1 — Identify components:**
- Gain: $K = 100$ → $20\log(100) = 40$ dB
- Integrator: $1/s$ → $-20$ dB/dec, $-90°$ constant
- First-order pole: $1/(1 + s/10)$ → break at $\omega_b = 10$ rad/s

**Step 2 — Magnitude plot:**
- For $\omega < 10$: slope = $-20$ dB/dec (from integrator only). At $\omega = 1$: $|G| = 40 - 20\log(1) = 40$ dB. At $\omega = 10$: $|G| = 40 - 20\log(10) = 20$ dB.
- For $\omega > 10$: slope = $-20 + (-20) = -40$ dB/dec. At $\omega = 100$: $|G| = 20 - 40\log(100/10) = 20 - 40 = -20$ dB.

**Step 3 — Gain crossover:** $|G(j\omega_{gc})| = 0$ dB.

In the $-20$ dB/dec region ($\omega < 10$): $40 - 20\log\omega = 0 \implies \omega = 100$ rad/s.

But $\omega = 100 > 10$, so we're in the $-40$ dB/dec region. Recalculate:

At $\omega = 10$: 20 dB. Need to drop 20 dB more at $-40$ dB/dec: $20/40 = 0.5$ decades above 10.

$\omega_{gc} = 10^{1+0.5} = 10^{1.5} \approx 31.6$ rad/s.

**Step 4 — Phase at gain crossover:**

$$
\angle G(j\omega_{gc}) = -90° - \arctan(\omega_{gc}/10) = -90° - \arctan(3.16) = -90° - 72.5° = -162.5°
$$

**Step 5 — Phase margin:**

$$
PM = 180° + (-162.5°) = 17.5°
$$

This is a low phase margin — the system will be very oscillatory.

### 5.2 — Nyquist Criterion Application: System with Time Delay

$L(s) = \dfrac{K e^{-s}}{s+1}$ (first-order system with unit time delay).

**Step 1 — Open-loop poles:** One pole at $s = -1$ (stable, $P = 0$).

**Step 2 — Frequency response:**

$$
L(j\omega) = \frac{K e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega + 1} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2}} e^{j(-\omega - \arctan\omega)}
$$

Magnitude: $|L| = K/\sqrt{1+\omega^2}$

Phase: $\angle L = -\omega - \arctan\omega$ (radians)

**Step 3 — Phase crossover** ($\angle L = -\pi$):

$$
-\omega_{pc} - \arctan(\omega_{pc}) = -\pi
$$

For $K = 2$: numerically, $\omega_{pc} \approx 2.03$ rad/s.

$|L(j\omega_{pc})| = 2/\sqrt{1+4.12} \approx 2/2.26 = 0.885$

$GM = 1/0.885 = 1.13$ → $20\log(1.13) = 1.06$ dB (barely stable!)

**Step 4 — Gain crossover** ($|L| = 1$): $K/\sqrt{1+\omega_{gc}^2} = 1 \implies \omega_{gc} = \sqrt{K^2-1} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$

Phase at $\omega_{gc}$: $-1.73 - \arctan(1.73) = -1.73 - 1.047 = -2.78$ rad $= -159°$

$PM = 180° - 159° = 21°$

**Conclusion:** Time delay severely reduces stability margins. Without delay ($e^{-s} = 1$), $PM$ would be $180° - \arctan(\sqrt{3}) \cdot (180/\pi) = 180° - 60° = 120°$.

### 5.3 — Derivation of Resonant Peak for Second-Order System

For $G(j\omega) = \dfrac{\omega_n^2}{(j\omega)^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n(j\omega) + \omega_n^2} = \dfrac{1}{1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2 + 2j\zeta(\omega/\omega_n)}$

Let $r = \omega/\omega_n$:

$$
|G(jr\omega_n)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}
$$

To find the peak, minimize the denominator. Let $f(r) = (1-r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 r^2$:

$$
\frac{df}{dr} = 2(1-r^2)(-2r) + 8\zeta^2 r = -4r(1-r^2) + 8\zeta^2 r = 4r[-1+r^2+2\zeta^2] = 0
$$

Non-trivial solution: $r^2 = 1 - 2\zeta^2$, so $\omega_{peak} = \omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$ (exists only for $\zeta < 1/\sqrt{2}$).

Peak magnitude:

$$
|G|_{max} = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}
$$

In dB: $M_r = -20\log(2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})$ dB.

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 11.6.1 — Complete Bode Plot with Stability Margins

Sketch the Bode plot and find GM, PM for: $G(s) = \dfrac{50}{s(s+5)(s+10)}$

**Step 1 — Standard form:** Factor out DC gains from each term:

$$
G(s) = \frac{50}{s \cdot 5(s/5+1) \cdot 10(s/10+1)} = \frac{1}{s(1+s/5)(1+s/10)}
$$

So $K = 50/(5\cdot10) = 1$, i.e., $20\log(1) = 0$ dB at $\omega = 1$ on the integrator line.

**Step 2 — Break frequencies:** $\omega_1 = 5$ rad/s, $\omega_2 = 10$ rad/s.

**Step 3 — Asymptotic magnitude:**
- $\omega < 5$: slope $-20$ dB/dec (integrator). At $\omega = 1$: 0 dB. At $\omega = 5$: $-20\log5 = -14$ dB.
- $5 < \omega < 10$: slope $-40$ dB/dec. At $\omega = 10$: $-14 - 40\log(10/5) = -14 - 12 = -26$ dB.
- $\omega > 10$: slope $-60$ dB/dec.

**Step 4 — Gain crossover** (where magnitude = 0 dB): In the $-20$ dB/dec region, $|G| = -20\log\omega$. Set to 0: $\omega_{gc} = 1$ rad/s.

Verify: $|G(j1)| = \dfrac{50}{1\cdot\sqrt{26}\cdot\sqrt{101}} = \dfrac{50}{\sqrt{2626}} = \dfrac{50}{51.2} = 0.977 \approx 0$ dB ✓

**Step 5 — Phase at $\omega_{gc} = 1$:**

$$
\angle G(j1) = -90° - \arctan(1/5) - \arctan(1/10) = -90° - 11.3° - 5.7° = -107°
$$

$$
PM = 180° - 107° = 73°
$$

**Step 6 — Phase crossover** (where phase = $-180°$):

$$
-90° - \arctan(\omega/5) - \arctan(\omega/10) = -180°
$$

$$
\arctan(\omega/5) + \arctan(\omega/10) = 90°
$$

Using $\arctan(a) + \arctan(b) = 90°$ when $ab = 1$: $(\omega/5)(\omega/10) = 1 \implies \omega^2 = 50 \implies \omega_{pc} = \sqrt{50} = 7.07$ rad/s.

**Step 7 — Gain margin:**

$$
|G(j7.07)| = \frac{50}{7.07 \cdot \sqrt{7.07^2+25} \cdot \sqrt{7.07^2+100}} = \frac{50}{7.07 \cdot 8.66 \cdot 12.25} = \frac{50}{750} = 0.0667
$$

$$
GM = -20\log(0.0667) = 23.5 \text{ dB}
$$

**Conclusion:** $PM = 73°$, $GM = 23.5$ dB — very robust stability.

### Example 11.6.2 — Nyquist Plot and Encirclement Counting

$L(s) = \dfrac{K}{s(s+1)(s+2)}$ with $K = 10$.

**Open-loop:** 3 poles at $s = 0, -1, -2$. All in LHP or on boundary. $P = 0$.

**Frequency response at key points:**

At $\omega = 0^+$: $|L| \to \infty$, $\angle L = -90° - 0° - 0° = -90°$ (starts at $-90°$ going to $-\infty$ on imaginary axis)

At $\omega \to \infty$: $|L| \to 0$, $\angle L \to -270°$

At $\omega_{pc}$: $\angle L = -180°$. From Routh: $\omega_{pc} = \sqrt{2}$, $|L(j\sqrt{2})| = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{6}} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{36}} = \dfrac{10}{6} = 1.67$

Since $|L(j\omega_{pc})| = 1.67 > 1$, the Nyquist plot crosses the negative real axis to the **left** of $-1$.

**Encirclements of $-1$:** The plot encircles $-1$ twice (clockwise) → $N = 2$.

$Z = N + P = 2 + 0 = 2$ → **2 closed-loop poles in RHP. Unstable.**

(Routh confirms: for $K = 10$, char. eq. $s^3 + 3s^2 + 2s + 10$, Routh $s^1$ entry: $(6-10)/3 = -4/3 < 0$ → 2 RHP roots.)

---

### Example 11.6.3 — Lead Compensator Design via Bode Plot

**Specs:** For plant $G(s) = \dfrac{10}{s(s+2)}$, design a lead compensator to achieve $PM \geq 45°$ with $K_v = 20$.

**Step 1 — Set gain for $K_v$:** $K_v = \lim_{s\to 0} sL(s) = \lim_{s\to 0} s \cdot K_c \cdot \dfrac{10}{s(s+2)} = 5K_c = 20 \implies K_c = 4$

**Step 2 — Uncompensated system:** $L_0(s) = \dfrac{40}{s(s+2)}$

Find $\omega_{gc}$: $|L_0(j\omega)| = \dfrac{40}{\omega\sqrt{\omega^2+4}} = 1$

$1600 = \omega^2(\omega^2+4)$, let $x = \omega^2$: $x^2 + 4x - 1600 = 0$, $x = (-4+\sqrt{16+6400})/2 = 38.1$, $\omega_{gc} = 6.17$ rad/s.

Phase: $\angle L_0(j6.17) = -90° - \arctan(6.17/2) = -90° - 72.1° = -162.1°$

Uncompensated $PM = 180° - 162.1° = 17.9°$ (insufficient).

**Step 3 — Required phase lead:** Need $45° - 17.9° + 5°$ (safety) $= 32.1°$

**Step 4 — Lead compensator:** $C(s) = \dfrac{1+s/\omega_z}{1+s/\omega_p}$ with $\omega_p > \omega_z$.

Maximum phase lead: $\phi_{max} = \arcsin\left(\dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}\right)$ where $\alpha = \omega_p/\omega_z$.

$\sin(32.1°) = 0.531 = \dfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1} \implies \alpha = \dfrac{1+0.531}{1-0.531} = 3.26$

**Step 5 — Place maximum phase at new $\omega_{gc}$:** The compensator adds $+10\log\alpha = 5.1$ dB at $\phi_{max}$. New crossover shifts right.

New $\omega_{gc}$: where $|L_0| = -10\log\alpha = -5.1$ dB. From the Bode plot, $\omega_{gc,new} \approx 8$ rad/s.

$\omega_z = \omega_{gc,new}/\sqrt{\alpha} = 8/1.81 = 4.43$ rad/s

$\omega_p = \alpha\omega_z = 3.26 \times 4.43 = 14.4$ rad/s

**Lead compensator:** $C(s) = 4 \cdot \dfrac{1 + s/4.43}{1 + s/14.4} = 4 \cdot \dfrac{s + 4.43}{s + 14.4} \cdot \dfrac{14.4}{4.43}$

## 📚 Appendix — Extended Frequency Response Topics

### A.1 — Complete Bode Plot: Third-Order System with Zero

Sketch the Bode plot for $G(s) = \dfrac{20(s+5)}{s(s+2)(s+50)}$.

**Step 1 — Standard form** (normalize each factor):

$$
G(s) = \frac{20 \cdot 5}{2 \cdot 50} \cdot \frac{(s/5+1)}{s \cdot (s/2+1)(s/50+1)} = \frac{1}{s} \cdot \frac{(s/5+1)}{(s/2+1)(s/50+1)}
$$

DC gain factor: $20 \times 5 / (2 \times 50) = 1$. So at $\omega = 1$: magnitude from integrator = $1/1 = 0$ dB.

**Step 2 — Break frequencies:** $\omega_1 = 2$ (pole), $\omega_2 = 5$ (zero), $\omega_3 = 50$ (pole)

**Step 3 — Asymptotic slopes:**
- $\omega < 2$: $-20$ dB/dec (integrator only)
- $2 < \omega < 5$: $-20 + (-20) = -40$ dB/dec (integrator + pole)
- $5 < \omega < 50$: $-40 + 20 = -20$ dB/dec (zero cancels one pole's slope)
- $\omega > 50$: $-20 + (-20) = -40$ dB/dec

**Step 4 — Key magnitude values:**
- At $\omega = 1$: 0 dB (from integrator line)
- At $\omega = 2$: $0 - 20\log(2) = -6$ dB
- At $\omega = 5$: $-6 - 40\log(5/2) = -6 - 15.9 = -21.9$ dB
- At $\omega = 50$: $-21.9 - 20\log(50/5) = -21.9 - 20 = -41.9$ dB

**Step 5 — Phase:**
- $\omega \to 0$: $-90°$ (integrator)
- $\omega = 2$: $-90° - 45° + 0° - 0° = -135°$
- $\omega = 5$: $-90° - \arctan(5/2) + 45° - \arctan(5/50) = -90° - 68.2° + 45° - 5.7° = -118.9°$
- $\omega = 50$: $-90° - \arctan(50/2) + \arctan(50/5) - 45° = -90° - 87.7° + 84.3° - 45° = -138.4°$
- $\omega \to \infty$: $-90° - 90° + 90° - 90° = -180°$

**Step 6 — Gain crossover:** Approximately where $|G| = 0$ dB. From the asymptotic plot, this occurs at $\omega_{gc} \approx 1$ rad/s.

Phase at $\omega = 1$: $-90° - \arctan(0.5) + \arctan(0.2) - \arctan(0.02) = -90° - 26.6° + 11.3° - 1.1° = -106.4°$

$PM = 180° - 106.4° = 73.6°$ — excellent stability margin.

### A.3 — Nyquist Criterion with Open-Loop RHP Poles

$L(s) = \dfrac{K(s+2)}{(s-1)(s+3)}$ (one open-loop pole in RHP: $P = 1$)

For stability: need $N = -P = -1$ (one counter-clockwise encirclement of $-1$).

**At $\omega = 0$:** $L(0) = K(2)/((-1)(3)) = -2K/3$

For the Nyquist plot to encircle $-1$ counter-clockwise, we need $L(0) < -1$:

$$
-\frac{2K}{3} < -1 \implies K > \frac{3}{2}
$$

**Verification via Routh:** Char. eq.: $(s-1)(s+3) + K(s+2) = s^2 + (2+K)s + (2K-3) = 0$

Stability: $2+K > 0$ (always for $K > 0$) and $2K-3 > 0 \implies K > 3/2$ ✓

### A.4 — Bode's Gain-Phase Relationship (Minimum Phase)

For minimum-phase systems, the phase is uniquely determined by the magnitude slope:

$$
\angle G(j\omega_0) \approx n \times (-90°)
$$

where $n$ is the slope of the magnitude plot at $\omega_0$ in units of $-20$ dB/decade.

**Examples:**
- Slope = $-20$ dB/dec → phase $\approx -90°$
- Slope = $-40$ dB/dec → phase $\approx -180°$
- Slope = $-60$ dB/dec → phase $\approx -270°$

**Design implication:** At gain crossover, the magnitude slope should be approximately $-20$ dB/dec to ensure adequate phase margin ($\approx 90°$ away from $-180°$). If the slope is $-40$ dB/dec at crossover, the phase margin will be very small.

**Rule of thumb:** Design the compensated system so that the magnitude plot crosses 0 dB with a slope of $-20$ dB/dec over at least one decade centered at $\omega_{gc}$.

### A.5 — Lag Compensator Design via Bode Plot

**Objective:** Increase low-frequency gain (reduce steady-state error) without significantly affecting phase margin.

**Lag compensator:** $C_{lag}(s) = \dfrac{s + 1/T}{s + 1/(\alpha T)}$ where $\alpha > 1$.

**Effect:**
- DC gain increased by factor $\alpha$ ($20\log\alpha$ dB)
- Phase lag occurs at low frequencies (near $\omega = 1/T$)
- At crossover frequency: negligible phase contribution if $1/T \ll \omega_{gc}$

**Design procedure:**
1. Set gain $K$ for desired $K_v$ (or $K_p$) without compensator
2. Find uncompensated $PM$ — it will be too small due to high gain
3. Find the frequency where uncompensated phase = $-(180° - PM_{desired} - 5°)$. This becomes new $\omega_{gc}$.
4. Determine $\alpha$ = magnitude at new $\omega_{gc}$ (to bring it to 0 dB)
5. Place lag compensator zero at $\omega_{gc}/10$: $1/T = \omega_{gc}/10$
6. Lag pole at $1/(\alpha T)$

**Example:** For $G(s) = \dfrac{K}{s(s+1)(s+5)}$, need $K_v = 10$ and $PM \geq 40°$.

$K_v = K/5 = 10 \implies K = 50$. Uncompensated $PM$ with $K = 50$ is very small.

Find $\omega$ where phase = $-180° + 40° + 5° = -135°$: approximately $\omega = 0.7$ rad/s.

$|G(j0.7)| = 50/(0.7\sqrt{1.49}\sqrt{25.49}) = 50/(0.7\cdot1.22\cdot5.05) = 11.6$ → need $\alpha = 11.6$

Lag zero: $1/T = 0.07$ rad/s, lag pole: $1/(\alpha T) = 0.006$ rad/s.

### Problem 11.6.E1 — Bode Plot of a High-Pass Filter $G(s) = \dfrac{s}{s + \omega_c}$

> **Problem:** Construct the complete Bode plot (magnitude and phase) for the high-pass filter:
>
> $$G(s) = \frac{s}{s + \omega_c}$$
>
> with $\omega_c = 10$ rad/s. Determine the gain at DC, at the corner frequency, and at high frequencies.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Rewrite in Standard Bode Form

$$
G(s) = \frac{s}{s + 10} = \frac{s/10}{s/10 + 1} = \frac{(s/10)}{(1 + s/10)}
$$

This is a **differentiator** ($s/10$) in series with a **first-order lag** ($1/(1+s/10)$).

Alternatively: $G(j\omega) = \dfrac{j\omega}{j\omega + 10}$

#### Step 2: Magnitude

$$
|G(j\omega)| = \frac{|j\omega|}{|j\omega + 10|} = \frac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^2 + 100}}
$$

In dB:

$$
20\log|G| = 20\log\omega - 20\log\sqrt{\omega^2+100} = 20\log\omega - 10\log(\omega^2+100)
$$

**Key values:**
- At $\omega \to 0$: $|G| \approx \omega/10 \to 0$ → $-\infty$ dB (slope: $+20$ dB/dec)
- At $\omega = \omega_c = 10$: $|G| = 10/\sqrt{200} = 10/(10\sqrt{2}) = 1/\sqrt{2}$ → $-3$ dB
- At $\omega \to \infty$: $|G| \approx \omega/\omega = 1$ → $0$ dB

**Asymptotic behavior:**
- For $\omega \ll 10$: slope is $+20$ dB/dec (the differentiator dominates)
- For $\omega \gg 10$: slope is $0$ dB/dec (flat at 0 dB)
- Corner frequency: $\omega_c = 10$ rad/s

#### Step 3: Phase

$$
\angle G(j\omega) = \angle(j\omega) - \angle(j\omega + 10) = 90° - \arctan(\omega/10)
$$

**Key values:**
- At $\omega \to 0$: $\angle G = 90° - 0° = 90°$
- At $\omega = 10$: $\angle G = 90° - 45° = 45°$
- At $\omega \to \infty$: $\angle G = 90° - 90° = 0°$

#### Step 4: Physical Interpretation

This is a **high-pass filter** (also called a "washout filter" in control):
- Blocks DC and low-frequency signals (gain → 0 as $\omega \to 0$)
- Passes high-frequency signals unchanged (gain → 1 as $\omega \to \infty$)
- The $-3$ dB cutoff frequency is $\omega_c = 10$ rad/s

**Control application:** Used in rate feedback (tachometer), AC coupling, and removing DC bias from sensor signals.

</details>

### Problem 11.6.E2 — Bode Plot of a 2nd-Order System Showing Resonant Peak

> **Problem:** For the second-order system:
>
> $$G(s) = \frac{100}{s^2 + 2s + 100}$$
>
> (a) Identify $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$.
> (b) Find the resonant peak magnitude $M_r$ and resonant frequency $\omega_r$.
> (c) Sketch the Bode magnitude plot showing the peak.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Part (a): System Parameters

$$
\omega_n^2 = 100 \implies \omega_n = 10 \text{ rad/s}
$$

$$
2\zeta\omega_n = 2 \implies \zeta = \frac{2}{20} = 0.1
$$

Very lightly damped system ($\zeta = 0.1$).

#### Part (b): Resonant Peak

The frequency response magnitude is:

$$
|G(j\omega)| = \frac{\omega_n^2}{\sqrt{(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}}
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-(\omega/\omega_n)^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega/\omega_n)^2}}
$$

Let $u = \omega/\omega_n$ (normalized frequency):

$$
|G(ju\omega_n)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-u^2)^2 + (2\zeta u)^2}}
$$

**Resonant frequency:** Maximize $|G|$ by minimizing the denominator. Take derivative of $(1-u^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u^2$ with respect to $u^2$:

$$
\frac{d}{d(u^2)}[(1-u^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u^2] = -2(1-u^2) + 4\zeta^2 = 0
$$

$$
1 - u^2 = 2\zeta^2 \implies u^2 = 1 - 2\zeta^2
$$

$$
\omega_r = \omega_n\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2} = 10\sqrt{1-0.02} = 10\sqrt{0.98} = 9.90 \text{ rad/s}
$$

**Resonant peak magnitude:**

$$
M_r = \frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} = \frac{1}{2(0.1)\sqrt{1-0.01}} = \frac{1}{0.2(0.995)} = \frac{1}{0.199} = 5.025
$$

In dB: $20\log(5.025) = 14.0$ dB

#### Part (c): Bode Plot Key Points

| $\omega$ (rad/s) | $|G|$ (dB) | Phase |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 1 | $\approx 0$ dB | $\approx -1.1°$ |
| 5 | $+1.7$ dB | $-7.6°$ |
| 9 | $+10.5$ dB | $-47°$ |
| 9.9 ($\omega_r$) | $+14.0$ dB (peak) | $-84°$ |
| 10 ($\omega_n$) | $+14.0$ dB | $-90°$ |
| 11 | $+10.0$ dB | $-100°$ |
| 20 | $-9.6$ dB | $-166°$ |
| 100 | $-40$ dB | $-179°$ |

**Asymptotic behavior:**
- $\omega \ll \omega_n$: $0$ dB, $0°$ phase
- $\omega = \omega_n$: $20\log(1/(2\zeta)) = 20\log(5) = 14$ dB, $-90°$ phase
- $\omega \gg \omega_n$: $-40$ dB/dec slope, $-180°$ phase

**Design implication:** A resonant peak of 14 dB means the system amplifies signals near $\omega_r$ by a factor of 5. In a closed-loop system, this corresponds to approximately $M_p \approx 60\%$ overshoot. The relationship between $M_r$ and $M_p$ is: $M_r \approx 1/(2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})$ and $M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$.

</details>

### Problem 11.6.E3 — Nyquist Contour and Encirclement Count for a Marginally Stable Plant

> **Problem:** Consider the open-loop transfer function:
>
> $$L(s) = \frac{K}{s^2(s+2)}$$
>
> This has a double pole at the origin (Type 2 system).
>
> (a) Explain why the standard Nyquist contour must be modified.
> (b) Describe the modified contour and sketch the Nyquist plot.
> (c) Determine stability for $K = 4$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Part (a): Why Modification is Needed

The standard Nyquist contour is the entire $j\omega$-axis plus a semicircular arc at infinity closing in the RHP. However, $L(s)$ has poles AT $s = 0$ (on the $j\omega$-axis), so the contour passes through a singularity.

We must **indent around** the poles on the imaginary axis with small semicircular detours of radius $\epsilon \to 0$ into the RHP.

#### Part (b): Modified Contour and Nyquist Plot

The modified Nyquist contour $\Gamma$ consists of:
1. $s = j\omega$, $\omega$ from $+\epsilon$ to $+R$ (upper $j\omega$-axis)
2. Semicircle at infinity: $s = Re^{j\theta}$, $\theta$ from $+90°$ to $-90°$ (maps to origin)
3. $s = j\omega$, $\omega$ from $-R$ to $-\epsilon$ (lower $j\omega$-axis)
4. Small semicircle around origin: $s = \epsilon e^{j\theta}$, $\theta$ from $-90°$ to $+90°$ (RHP indent)

**Segment 4 (indent around double pole):**

$$
L(\epsilon e^{j\theta}) = \frac{K}{\epsilon^2 e^{j2\theta}(\epsilon e^{j\theta}+2)} \approx \frac{K}{2\epsilon^2 e^{j2\theta}} = \frac{K}{2\epsilon^2}e^{-j2\theta}
$$

As $\theta$ goes from $-90°$ to $+90°$ (total $180°$), the angle $-2\theta$ goes from $+180°$ to $-180°$ (total $-360°$).

This means the indent maps to a **huge arc** (radius $K/(2\epsilon^2) \to \infty$) sweeping $360°$ clockwise (from angle $+180°$ to $-180°$).

**Segment 1 (upper $j\omega$-axis):**

$$
L(j\omega) = \frac{K}{(j\omega)^2(j\omega+2)} = \frac{K}{-\omega^2(j\omega+2)} = \frac{-K}{\omega^2(j\omega+2)}
$$

$$
= \frac{-K}{\omega^2} \cdot \frac{1}{j\omega+2} = \frac{-K}{\omega^2} \cdot \frac{2-j\omega}{\omega^2+4}
$$

$$
= \frac{-K(2-j\omega)}{\omega^2(\omega^2+4)} = \frac{-2K}{\omega^2(\omega^2+4)} + j\frac{K}{\omega(\omega^2+4)}
$$

At $\omega = \epsilon^+$: Real part $\to -\infty$, Imaginary part $\to +\infty$ (starts in Q2 at large radius)

At $\omega \to \infty$: Both parts $\to 0$ (approaches origin)

The plot crosses the negative real axis when $\text{Im}(L) = 0$, which occurs only at $\omega \to \infty$ (the imaginary part $K/(\omega(\omega^2+4))$ is always positive for $\omega \gt  0$).

#### Part (c): Stability Analysis for $K = 4$

**Open-loop poles:** $s = 0$ (double), $s = -2$. With the RHP indent, the poles at $s = 0$ are excluded from the RHP. So $P = 0$ (no open-loop RHP poles).

**Counting encirclements of $-1$:**

The Nyquist plot starts at angle $+180°$ (from the indent), sweeps through Q2 and Q3 as $\omega$ increases from $0^+$ to $\infty$, approaching the origin. The real-axis crossing from the indent is at $-\infty$, and the plot stays in Q2/Q3 (real part always negative, imaginary part positive for $\omega \gt  0$).

The critical question: does the plot pass to the left or right of $-1$?

At $\omega$ where the real part equals $-1$: $\dfrac{-2K}{\omega^2(\omega^2+4)} = -1$

$$
\omega^2(\omega^2+4) = 2K = 8
$$

$$
\omega^4 + 4\omega^2 - 8 = 0 \implies \omega^2 = \frac{-4+\sqrt{16+32}}{2} = \frac{-4+\sqrt{48}}{2} = \frac{-4+6.93}{2} = 1.46
$$

$\omega = 1.21$ rad/s. At this frequency, $\text{Im}(L) = 4/(1.21(1.46+4)) = 4/(6.61) = 0.605 \gt  0$.

So the plot passes through $(-1, +0.605j)$ — it passes ABOVE the point $-1$ on the real axis.

Combined with the infinite arc from the indent (which sweeps $360°$ clockwise through $-1$), the total encirclement count is $N = 2$ (clockwise).

$Z = N + P = 2 + 0 = 2$ → **Two closed-loop poles in RHP. UNSTABLE.**

**Conclusion:** The Type 2 system with $K = 4$ is unstable. In fact, for this system, Routh analysis of $s^3 + 2s^2 + K = 0$ shows the $s^1$ entry is $(2\cdot0 - K)/2 = -K/2 \lt  0$ for all $K \gt  0$. The system is **unstable for all positive gains** — a fundamental limitation of Type 2 systems without additional compensation.

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 — The Nyquist Stability Criterion: Proof Outline via the Argument Principle

The Nyquist criterion is one of the most elegant results in control theory. It connects the geometry of a frequency response plot to the stability of a closed-loop system through a deep result from complex analysis.

#### The Argument Principle (from Complex Analysis)

**Theorem (Cauchy's Argument Principle):** Let $f(s)$ be a meromorphic function (ratio of analytic functions) inside and on a simple closed contour $\Gamma$. If $f$ has $Z$ zeros and $P$ poles inside $\Gamma$ (counted with multiplicity), and $f$ has no zeros or poles ON $\Gamma$, then:

$$
\frac{1}{2\pi j}\oint_\Gamma \frac{f'(s)}{f(s)}\,ds = Z - P
$$

Equivalently, as $s$ traverses $\Gamma$ once counterclockwise, the image $f(s)$ encircles the origin $N = Z - P$ times counterclockwise.

#### Application to the Characteristic Function

Define $f(s) = 1 + L(s)$ where $L(s) = G(s)C(s)H(s)$ is the open-loop transfer function.

- **Zeros of $f(s)$** = roots of $1 + L(s) = 0$ = **closed-loop poles**
- **Poles of $f(s)$** = poles of $L(s)$ = **open-loop poles**

Let $\Gamma$ be the standard Nyquist contour (enclosing the entire RHP). Then:

- $Z$ = number of closed-loop poles in the RHP (what we want to find)
- $P$ = number of open-loop poles in the RHP (known from the plant)

The argument principle gives:

$$
N = Z - P
$$

where $N$ = number of clockwise encirclements of the origin by $f(s) = 1 + L(s)$ as $s$ traverses $\Gamma$.

#### The Key Geometric Shift

Encirclements of the **origin** by $1 + L(s)$ are equivalent to encirclements of the point $\mathbf{-1}$ by $L(s)$ (just shift the plot by $-1$).

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{Z = N + P}
$$

where:
- $N$ = number of clockwise encirclements of $-1$ by the Nyquist plot of $L(j\omega)$
- $P$ = number of open-loop RHP poles
- $Z$ = number of closed-loop RHP poles

**Stability condition:** $Z = 0$ (no closed-loop RHP poles), which requires $N = -P$.

- If $P = 0$ (open-loop stable): need $N = 0$ (no encirclements of $-1$)
- If $P > 0$ (open-loop unstable): need $N = -P$ (exactly $P$ counter-clockwise encirclements)

#### Why the Infinite Semicircle Contributes Nothing

On the large semicircle $s = Re^{j\theta}$ with $R \to \infty$: for any proper rational $L(s)$ (degree of numerator < degree of denominator):

$$
L(Re^{j\theta}) \to 0 \text{ as } R \to \infty
$$

So the image of the infinite semicircle is a single point at the origin — it contributes no encirclements.

#### Connection to Gain and Phase Margins

The Nyquist plot crosses the negative real axis at the **phase crossover frequency** $\omega_{pc}$ where $\angle L(j\omega_{pc}) = -180°$. At this point:

- If $|L(j\omega_{pc})| < 1$: the plot passes to the RIGHT of $-1$ → no encirclement → stable
- If $|L(j\omega_{pc})| > 1$: the plot passes to the LEFT of $-1$ → encirclement → unstable
- If $|L(j\omega_{pc})| = 1$: the plot passes THROUGH $-1$ → marginally stable

The **gain margin** is $GM = 1/|L(j\omega_{pc})|$ — how much the gain can increase before the plot reaches $-1$.

The **phase margin** is the angular distance from the point where $|L| = 1$ to the $-180°$ line — how much additional phase lag can be tolerated.

### 9.2 — Bode's Integral Constraints (Sensitivity Waterbed Effect)

#### Bode's Sensitivity Integral

For any stable open-loop system with at least 2 more poles than zeros:

$$
\int_0^\infty \ln|S(j\omega)|\,d\omega = 0
$$

where $S(j\omega) = 1/(1+L(j\omega))$ is the sensitivity function.

**Interpretation:** The "area" of sensitivity reduction (where $|S| < 1$, i.e., $\ln|S| < 0$) must exactly equal the "area" of sensitivity amplification (where $|S| > 1$, i.e., $\ln|S| > 0$). You cannot reduce sensitivity everywhere — pushing it down at some frequencies forces it up at others. This is the **waterbed effect**.

#### With Unstable Open-Loop Poles

If the open-loop has RHP poles at $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k$:

$$
\int_0^\infty \ln|S(j\omega)|\,d\omega = \pi\sum_{i=1}^k \text{Re}(p_i)
$$

The right side is positive, meaning the sensitivity amplification area EXCEEDS the reduction area. Unstable plants are fundamentally harder to control — they impose a "tax" on the sensitivity integral.

#### Design Implications

1. **Bandwidth limitation:** If you want $|S| < \epsilon$ for $\omega < \omega_B$, then $|S|$ must exceed 1 for some $\omega > \omega_B$. The peak of $|S|$ (denoted $M_s$) is bounded below.

2. **RHP zeros:** A zero at $s = z$ (with $\text{Re}(z) > 0$) imposes $S(z) = 1$ (the sensitivity equals 1 at the zero location), further constraining the achievable performance.

3. **Time delay:** Equivalent to infinitely many RHP zeros, making the constraints progressively tighter as bandwidth increases.

### 9.3 — Nichols Chart: Combining Magnitude and Phase in One Plot

The **Nichols chart** plots $|L(j\omega)|$ (in dB) vs. $\angle L(j\omega)$ (in degrees) on a single graph, with contours of constant closed-loop magnitude $|T|$ and phase $\angle T$ overlaid.

#### Why It's Useful

On a Nichols chart:
- The **critical point** $(-180°, 0\text{ dB})$ corresponds to $L = -1$
- **Gain margin** = vertical distance from the plot to the critical point at $-180°$
- **Phase margin** = horizontal distance from the plot to $-180°$ at $0$ dB
- **Closed-loop resonant peak** $M_r$ = the smallest $|T|$-contour that the plot is tangent to

The Nichols chart allows simultaneous visualization of open-loop and closed-loop properties, making it the preferred tool for many practicing control engineers.

#### Relationship Between $M_r$ and Stability Margins

For a typical well-designed system:
- $M_r < 1.3$ (2.3 dB) corresponds to $PM > 50°$ and $GM > 12$ dB
- $M_r < 2.0$ (6 dB) corresponds to $PM > 30°$ and $GM > 6$ dB
- $M_r > 3.0$ (9.5 dB) indicates poor stability margins and excessive oscillation
