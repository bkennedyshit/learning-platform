# 4.4 — Central Forces & Keplerian Orbits

> *"The orbit of every planet is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus."* — Johannes Kepler, *Astronomia Nova*, 1609

Central force problems — where the force depends only on the distance between two bodies and acts along the line joining them — are among the most important exactly solvable systems in physics. They encompass planetary motion, atomic physics (Coulomb problem), and satellite dynamics.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Reduce the two-body central force problem to an equivalent one-body problem.
2. Exploit conservation of angular momentum to reduce the problem to one dimension.
3. Derive the **orbit equation** $r(\theta)$ for the inverse-square force.
4. Classify orbits (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) by energy and eccentricity.
5. Derive and apply all three of **Kepler's laws** from Newton's gravitational law.
6. Compute orbital parameters: semi-major axis, period, eccentricity, apsidal distances.
7. Analyze the **effective potential** and identify turning points.
8. Calculate escape velocity and Hohmann transfer orbit parameters.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 4.4.1 — Central Force

A force is **central** if it acts along the line joining two bodies and depends only on the distance between them: $\mathbf{F} = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, where $r = |\mathbf{r}|$.

### Definition 4.4.2 — Effective Potential

For a particle with angular momentum $L$ in a central potential $U(r)$, the **effective potential** is:

$$
V_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2},
$$

where $\mu$ is the reduced mass. The term $L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ is the **centrifugal barrier**.

### Definition 4.4.3 — Orbital Eccentricity

The **eccentricity** $e$ of a conic section orbit is:

$$
e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{\mu k^2}},
$$

where $E$ is total energy, $L$ is angular momentum, and $k = G m_1 m_2$ (gravitational) or $k = Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ (Coulomb).

### Definition 4.4.4 — Semi-latus Rectum

The **semi-latus rectum** $p$ is the orbital parameter:

$$
p = \frac{L^2}{\mu k}.
$$

The orbit equation in polar form is $r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta}$.

### Definition 4.4.5 — Apsidal Distances

The **periapsis** (closest approach) and **apoapsis** (farthest distance) are:

$$
r_{\min} = \frac{p}{1+e}, \quad r_{\max} = \frac{p}{1-e} \quad (e < 1).
$$

---

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 4.4.A1 — Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

Every pair of point masses $m_1, m_2$ attracts each other with force:

$$
\mathbf{F} = -\frac{Gm_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \quad U(r) = -\frac{Gm_1 m_2}{r}.
$$

### Axiom 4.4.A2 — Isotropy of Space

Space is isotropic (no preferred direction), guaranteeing that central force motion is confined to a plane (the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$).

---

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 4.4.1 — Planar Motion

For any central force, the motion is confined to the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$.

**Proof.** Since $\mathbf{F} = f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, the torque $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r}\times f(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}} = f(r)(\mathbf{r}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \mathbf{0}$. Therefore $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ is constant. Since $\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{L} = 0$ always, $\mathbf{r}$ remains in the plane perpendicular to the fixed vector $\mathbf{L}$. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 4.4.2 — Kepler's Second Law (Equal Areas)

The radius vector sweeps equal areas in equal times: $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2\mu} = \text{const}$.

**Proof.** The area element swept in time $dt$ is $dA = \frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta$. Therefore $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\dot\theta = \frac{L}{2\mu}$ (since $L = \mu r^2\dot\theta$). Since $L$ is conserved, $dA/dt$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 4.4.3 — Reduction to One Dimension

Using $L = \mu r^2\dot\theta$ to eliminate $\dot\theta$, the radial energy equation becomes:

$$
E = \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + V_{\text{eff}}(r), \quad V_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.
$$

This is equivalent to a one-dimensional particle of mass $\mu$ moving in potential $V_{\text{eff}}$.

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 4.4.1 — Orbit Equation for Inverse-Square Force

For $U(r) = -k/r$, the orbit is a conic section:

$$
r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)},
$$

where $p = L^2/(\mu k)$ and $e = \sqrt{1 + 2EL^2/(\mu k^2)}$.

### Theorem 4.4.2 — Kepler's Third Law

For elliptical orbits ($E < 0$), the period $\tau$ and semi-major axis $a$ satisfy:

$$
\tau^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 \mu}{k}a^3 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1+m_2)}a^3.
$$

### Theorem 4.4.3 — Orbit Classification by Energy

| Energy $E$ | Eccentricity $e$ | Orbit Type |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $E < 0$ | $0 \leq e < 1$ | Ellipse (bound) |
| $E = 0$ | $e = 1$ | Parabola (marginally unbound) |
| $E > 0$ | $e > 1$ | Hyperbola (unbound) |

### Theorem 4.4.4 — Virial Theorem for Gravitational Orbits

For bound Keplerian orbits: $\langle T \rangle = -\frac{1}{2}\langle U \rangle = -\frac{E}{1}$, and $E = -\frac{k}{2a}$.

---


## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Derivation of the Orbit Equation (Binet's Method)

**Goal:** Find $r(\theta)$ for $U = -k/r$.

**Step 1:** Substitute $u = 1/r$. Then $r = 1/u$ and using $L = \mu r^2\dot\theta$:

$$
\dot{r} = \frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{dr}{d\theta}\dot\theta = -\frac{1}{u^2}\frac{du}{d\theta}\cdot\frac{L u^2}{\mu} = -\frac{L}{\mu}\frac{du}{d\theta}.
$$

**Step 2:** Differentiate again:

$$
\ddot{r} = -\frac{L}{\mu}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}\dot\theta = -\frac{L}{\mu}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}\cdot\frac{Lu^2}{\mu} = -\frac{L^2u^2}{\mu^2}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}.
$$

**Step 3:** The radial equation of motion $\mu\ddot{r} - \mu r\dot\theta^2 = f(r)$ becomes (with $f = -k/r^2 = -ku^2$):

$$
\mu\left(-\frac{L^2u^2}{\mu^2}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}\right) - \mu\frac{1}{u}\left(\frac{Lu^2}{\mu}\right)^2 = -ku^2.
$$

$$
-\frac{L^2u^2}{\mu}\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} - \frac{L^2u^3}{\mu} = -ku^2.
$$

Divide by $-L^2u^2/\mu$:

$$
\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}.
$$

**Step 4:** This is a simple harmonic oscillator with a constant forcing. The general solution is:

$$
u(\theta) = \frac{\mu k}{L^2} + A\cos(\theta - \theta_0).
$$

**Step 5:** Convert back to $r = 1/u$:

$$
r = \frac{1}{\frac{\mu k}{L^2}(1 + e\cos(\theta-\theta_0))}, \quad \text{where } e = \frac{AL^2}{\mu k}.
$$

$$
r(\theta) = \frac{L^2/(\mu k)}{1 + e\cos(\theta-\theta_0)} = \frac{p}{1+e\cos(\theta-\theta_0)}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.2 Derivation of Kepler's Third Law

**Proof.** The total area of an ellipse is $A_{\text{ellipse}} = \pi a b$, where $b = a\sqrt{1-e^2}$. From Kepler's second law: $A = \frac{L}{2\mu}\tau$. Equating:

$$
\pi a b = \frac{L\tau}{2\mu} \implies \tau = \frac{2\pi\mu ab}{L}.
$$

Now use $p = a(1-e^2) = L^2/(\mu k)$, so $L^2 = \mu k a(1-e^2)$ and $b^2 = a^2(1-e^2)$, giving $L = \sqrt{\mu k a(1-e^2)} = \sqrt{\mu k}\cdot b/\sqrt{a}$.

Substitute: $\tau = \frac{2\pi\mu ab}{\sqrt{\mu k}\cdot b/\sqrt{a}} = \frac{2\pi\mu a^{3/2}\sqrt{a}\cdot b}{\sqrt{\mu k}\cdot b} = 2\pi a^{3/2}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{k}}$.

$$
\tau^2 = \frac{4\pi^2\mu}{k}a^3. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.3 Escape Velocity Derivation

At the surface of a body of mass $M$ and radius $R$, a particle has $E = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{esc}}^2 - \frac{GMm}{R}$. For escape: $E = 0$ (parabolic orbit):

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{GMm}{R} \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}.
$$

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 4.4.1 — Halley's Comet Orbital Parameters

**Problem:** Halley's Comet has perihelion $r_{\min} = 0.586$ AU and aphelion $r_{\max} = 35.1$ AU. Find $a$, $e$, and the period.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Semi-major axis: $a = \frac{r_{\min}+r_{\max}}{2} = \frac{0.586+35.1}{2} = 17.84$ AU.

**Step 2:** Eccentricity: $e = \frac{r_{\max}-r_{\min}}{r_{\max}+r_{\min}} = \frac{35.1-0.586}{35.1+0.586} = \frac{34.514}{35.686} = 0.967$.

**Step 3:** Period (Kepler's third law, with $\tau_\oplus = 1$ yr, $a_\oplus = 1$ AU):

$$
\tau = \tau_\oplus\left(\frac{a}{a_\oplus}\right)^{3/2} = (17.84)^{3/2} = 75.3 \text{ years}.
$$

</details>

### Example 4.4.2 — Hohmann Transfer Orbit

**Problem:** Calculate the $\Delta v$ for a Hohmann transfer from a circular orbit of radius $r_1$ to radius $r_2 > r_1$ around a body of mass $M$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** The transfer orbit is an ellipse with $r_{\min} = r_1$ and $r_{\max} = r_2$. Semi-major axis: $a_t = (r_1+r_2)/2$.

**Step 2:** Circular orbit speed: $v_c = \sqrt{GM/r}$. At $r_1$: $v_1 = \sqrt{GM/r_1}$.

**Step 3:** Speed at periapsis of transfer orbit (vis-viva equation $v^2 = GM(2/r - 1/a)$):

$$
v_{t1} = \sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)} = \sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{2}{r_1+r_2}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM r_2}{r_1(r_1+r_2)}}.
$$

**Step 4:** First burn: $\Delta v_1 = v_{t1} - v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}} - 1\right)$.

**Step 5:** Speed at apoapsis of transfer: $v_{t2} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM r_1}{r_2(r_1+r_2)}}$. Circular speed at $r_2$: $v_2 = \sqrt{GM/r_2}$.

**Step 6:** Second burn: $\Delta v_2 = v_2 - v_{t2} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}}\left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{2r_1}{r_1+r_2}}\right)$.

**Total:** $\Delta v = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2$.

</details>

### Example 4.4.3 — Orbit from Initial Conditions

**Problem:** A satellite at distance $r_0 = 2R_\oplus$ from Earth's center has speed $v_0 = \sqrt{GM_\oplus/r_0}$ directed at $45°$ to the radial direction. Find the eccentricity.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Angular momentum: $L = \mu r_0 v_0 \sin 45° = \mu r_0 v_0/\sqrt{2}$.

**Step 2:** Energy: $E = \frac{1}{2}\mu v_0^2 - \frac{k}{r_0}$. With $v_0^2 = GM/r_0 = k/(\mu r_0)$:

$$
E = \frac{1}{2}\mu\cdot\frac{k}{\mu r_0} - \frac{k}{r_0} = \frac{k}{2r_0} - \frac{k}{r_0} = -\frac{k}{2r_0}.
$$

**Step 3:** Eccentricity:

$$
e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{\mu k^2}}.
$$

$L^2 = \mu^2 r_0^2 v_0^2/2 = \mu^2 r_0^2 \cdot \frac{k}{\mu r_0}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{\mu k r_0}{2}$.

$$
\frac{2EL^2}{\mu k^2} = \frac{2\cdot(-k/(2r_0))\cdot\mu k r_0/2}{\mu k^2} = \frac{-\mu k^2/(2)}{\mu k^2} = -\frac{1}{2}.
$$

$$
e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707.
$$

</details>

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Determining Orbital Eccentricity from Energy and Angular Momentum

**Problem:** A satellite of mass $m$ orbits Earth ($M_\oplus$) with total energy $E = -\frac{GMm}{4R_\oplus}$ and angular momentum $L = m\sqrt{GMR_\oplus}$. Determine the eccentricity, semi-major axis, periapsis, and apoapsis distances. Classify the orbit.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Recall the eccentricity formula

For a Keplerian orbit with gravitational parameter $k = GMm$ (force $F = -k/r^2$), the eccentricity is:

$$
e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}}.
$$

Here we use the reduced mass $\mu \approx m$ (since $m \ll M_\oplus$), so $k = GMm$ and the formula becomes:

$$
e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{m(GMm)^2}} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{m^3 G^2 M^2}}.
$$

#### Step 2: Substitute the given values

$E = -\frac{GMm}{4R_\oplus}$, $L = m\sqrt{GMR_\oplus}$, so $L^2 = m^2 GMR_\oplus$.

$$
\frac{2EL^2}{m^3 G^2 M^2} = \frac{2\cdot\left(-\frac{GMm}{4R_\oplus}\right)\cdot m^2 GMR_\oplus}{m^3 G^2 M^2}.
$$

Simplify numerator: $2\cdot(-\frac{GMm}{4R_\oplus})\cdot m^2 GMR_\oplus = -\frac{2m^3 G^2 M^2 R_\oplus}{4R_\oplus} = -\frac{m^3 G^2 M^2}{2}$.

$$
\frac{2EL^2}{m^3 G^2 M^2} = \frac{-m^3 G^2 M^2/2}{m^3 G^2 M^2} = -\frac{1}{2}.
$$

$$
e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707.
$$

#### Step 3: Semi-major axis

From $E = -\frac{k}{2a} = -\frac{GMm}{2a}$:

$$
-\frac{GMm}{4R_\oplus} = -\frac{GMm}{2a} \implies a = 2R_\oplus.
$$

#### Step 4: Periapsis and apoapsis

$$
r_{\min} = a(1-e) = 2R_\oplus\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = 2R_\oplus\cdot 0.293 = 0.586\,R_\oplus.
$$

$$
r_{\max} = a(1+e) = 2R_\oplus\left(1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = 2R_\oplus\cdot 1.707 = 3.414\,R_\oplus.
$$

#### Step 5: Classification

Since $E \lt  0$ and $0 \lt  e \lt  1$, this is a **bound elliptical orbit**. However, note that $r_{\min} = 0.586\,R_\oplus \lt  R_\oplus$, meaning the orbit intersects Earth's surface — this orbit is not physically realizable (the satellite would crash).

**Final Answer:**

$$
e = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad a = 2R_\oplus, \quad r_{\min} = 0.586\,R_\oplus, \quad r_{\max} = 3.414\,R_\oplus
$$

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Hohmann Transfer: Earth to Mars ΔV Calculation

**Problem:** Calculate the total $\Delta v$ for a Hohmann transfer from Earth's orbit ($r_1 = 1.0$ AU) to Mars's orbit ($r_2 = 1.524$ AU). Use $GM_\odot = 4\pi^2$ AU³/yr² (from Kepler's third law with $\tau_\oplus = 1$ yr). Also find the transfer time. (See [[10.3]] for extended orbital mechanics applications.)

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Circular orbit speeds

$$
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{GM_\odot}{r_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2}{1.0}} = 2\pi \text{ AU/yr} \approx 29.78 \text{ km/s}.
$$

$$
v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{GM_\odot}{r_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2}{1.524}} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1.524}} = \frac{2\pi}{1.2345} \approx 5.089 \text{ AU/yr} \approx 24.13 \text{ km/s}.
$$

#### Step 2: Transfer orbit parameters

Semi-major axis of transfer ellipse: $a_t = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2} = \frac{1.0 + 1.524}{2} = 1.262$ AU.

#### Step 3: Speeds on the transfer orbit (vis-viva equation)

At perihelion ($r = r_1$):

$$
v_{t1} = \sqrt{GM_\odot\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)} = \sqrt{4\pi^2\left(2 - \frac{1}{1.262}\right)} = \sqrt{4\pi^2(2 - 0.7924)} = \sqrt{4\pi^2\cdot 1.2076}.
$$

$$
v_{t1} = 2\pi\sqrt{1.2076} = 2\pi\cdot 1.0989 = 6.904 \text{ AU/yr} \approx 32.73 \text{ km/s}.
$$

At aphelion ($r = r_2$):

$$
v_{t2} = \sqrt{GM_\odot\left(\frac{2}{r_2} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)} = \sqrt{4\pi^2\left(\frac{2}{1.524} - \frac{1}{1.262}\right)} = \sqrt{4\pi^2(1.3123 - 0.7924)}.
$$

$$
v_{t2} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.5199} = 2\pi\cdot 0.7210 = 4.530 \text{ AU/yr} \approx 21.48 \text{ km/s}.
$$

#### Step 4: Delta-v calculations

First burn (leave Earth's orbit):

$$
\Delta v_1 = v_{t1} - v_1 = 32.73 - 29.78 = 2.95 \text{ km/s}.
$$

Second burn (circularize at Mars's orbit):

$$
\Delta v_2 = v_2 - v_{t2} = 24.13 - 21.48 = 2.65 \text{ km/s}.
$$

Total:

$$
\Delta v_{\text{total}} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2 = 2.95 + 2.65 = 5.60 \text{ km/s}.
$$

#### Step 5: Transfer time

The transfer time is half the period of the transfer ellipse:

$$
\tau_t = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\tau_\oplus\left(\frac{a_t}{a_\oplus}\right)^{3/2} = \frac{1}{2}(1.262)^{3/2} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 1.4175 = 0.709 \text{ yr} \approx 259 \text{ days}.
$$

**Final Answer:**

$$
\Delta v_{\text{total}} \approx 5.60 \text{ km/s}, \quad t_{\text{transfer}} \approx 259 \text{ days}
$$

</details>

### Example 8.3 — Bound vs. Unbound Orbit Classification

**Problem:** A comet passes the Sun at distance $r_0 = 0.5$ AU with speed $v_0 = 50$ km/s. Determine whether the orbit is bound (ellipse), marginally bound (parabola), or unbound (hyperbola). Find the eccentricity.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Compute the total specific energy

The specific orbital energy (energy per unit mass):

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{v_0^2}{2} - \frac{GM_\odot}{r_0}.
$$

Convert to consistent units. Use $GM_\odot = 1.327\times 10^{11}$ km³/s² and $r_0 = 0.5$ AU $= 7.48\times 10^7$ km.

$$
\frac{GM_\odot}{r_0} = \frac{1.327\times 10^{11}}{7.48\times 10^7} = 1774 \text{ km}^2/\text{s}^2.
$$

$$
\frac{v_0^2}{2} = \frac{50^2}{2} = 1250 \text{ km}^2/\text{s}^2.
$$

$$
\varepsilon = 1250 - 1774 = -524 \text{ km}^2/\text{s}^2.
$$

#### Step 2: Classify the orbit

Since $\varepsilon \lt  0$: the orbit is **bound (elliptical)**.

Classification rules:
- $\varepsilon \lt  0$: ellipse ($0 \leq e \lt  1$)
- $\varepsilon = 0$: parabola ($e = 1$)
- $\varepsilon \gt  0$: hyperbola ($e \gt  1$)

#### Step 3: Find the escape speed at this distance for comparison

$$
v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_\odot}{r_0}} = \sqrt{2\times 1774} = \sqrt{3548} = 59.6 \text{ km/s}.
$$

Since $v_0 = 50 \lt  59.6 = v_{\text{esc}}$, the comet is bound. ✓

#### Step 4: Compute eccentricity

We need the angular momentum. Assume the velocity is entirely tangential at $r_0$ (perihelion passage): $L/m = r_0 v_0 = 7.48\times 10^7 \times 50 = 3.74\times 10^9$ km²/s.

Semi-major axis: $a = -\frac{GM_\odot}{2\varepsilon} = -\frac{1.327\times 10^{11}}{2(-524)} = 1.266\times 10^8$ km $= 0.846$ AU.

Semi-latus rectum: $p = \frac{(L/m)^2}{GM_\odot} = \frac{(3.74\times 10^9)^2}{1.327\times 10^{11}} = \frac{1.399\times 10^{19}}{1.327\times 10^{11}} = 1.054\times 10^8$ km.

Eccentricity from $p = a(1-e^2)$:

$$
1-e^2 = \frac{p}{a} = \frac{1.054\times 10^8}{1.266\times 10^8} = 0.833.
$$

$$
e = \sqrt{1-0.833} = \sqrt{0.167} = 0.409.
$$

Alternatively, if $r_0$ is perihelion: $r_{\min} = a(1-e)$, so $e = 1 - r_0/a = 1 - 0.5/0.846 = 0.409$. ✓

**Final Answer:**

$$
\varepsilon \lt  0 \implies \text{Bound elliptical orbit}, \quad e = 0.409, \quad a = 0.846 \text{ AU}
$$

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 Derivation of Kepler's Third Law from Newton's Law of Gravitation

Kepler's third law ($\tau^2 \propto a^3$) was originally an empirical observation. Here we derive it rigorously from Newton's gravitational force law and the geometry of elliptical orbits.

**Step 1: Area swept and the period.** By Kepler's second law (conservation of angular momentum), the areal velocity is constant:

$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2\mu},
$$

where $L$ is the angular momentum and $\mu$ is the reduced mass. The total area of an ellipse is $A = \pi ab$, where $b = a\sqrt{1-e^2}$ is the semi-minor axis. Therefore the period is:

$$
\tau = \frac{A}{dA/dt} = \frac{\pi ab}{L/(2\mu)} = \frac{2\pi\mu ab}{L}.
$$

**Step 2: Relate $L$ to orbital parameters.** The semi-latus rectum is $p = a(1-e^2) = b^2/a$. From the orbit equation, $p = L^2/(\mu k)$ where $k = Gm_1 m_2$. Therefore:

$$
L^2 = \mu k\cdot p = \mu k\cdot a(1-e^2) = \mu k\cdot\frac{b^2}{a}.
$$

So $L = \sqrt{\mu k b^2/a} = b\sqrt{\mu k/a}$.

**Step 3: Substitute into the period formula.**

$$
\tau = \frac{2\pi\mu ab}{b\sqrt{\mu k/a}} = \frac{2\pi\mu a}{\sqrt{\mu k/a}} = 2\pi a\sqrt{\frac{\mu a}{k}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu a^3}{k}}.
$$

**Step 4: Square to get Kepler's third law.**

$$
\tau^2 = \frac{4\pi^2\mu}{k}a^3 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1+m_2)}a^3,
$$

since $k/\mu = G(m_1+m_2)$. For a planet of mass $m \ll M$ (the Sun):

$$
\tau^2 \approx \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3.
$$

This is **Kepler's third law**: the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, with the proportionality constant depending only on the central mass. The law is exact for circular orbits and holds for all conic sections (ellipses) with the same $a$.

**Numerical check for Earth:** $a = 1$ AU, $\tau = 1$ yr, $GM_\odot = 4\pi^2$ AU³/yr². Then $\tau^2 = 4\pi^2/(4\pi^2)\cdot 1 = 1$ yr². ✓

*References: Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, §3.7; Feynman Lectures Vol. I, Ch. 7; Marion & Thornton, Ch. 8.*

### 9.2 The Laplace-Runge-Lenz Vector

The Kepler problem possesses a hidden symmetry beyond energy and angular momentum conservation: the **Laplace-Runge-Lenz (LRL) vector** is conserved. This additional conserved quantity explains why Keplerian orbits close (no precession) and is connected to the $SO(4)$ symmetry of the hydrogen atom in quantum mechanics.

**Definition:** For a particle of mass $\mu$ in a gravitational potential $V = -k/r$, the LRL vector is:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{L} - \mu k\hat{\mathbf{r}},
$$

where $\mathbf{p} = \mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}$ is the linear momentum, $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ is the angular momentum, and $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r$.

**Proof of conservation:** Compute $\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt}$:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{p}}\times\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{p}\times\dot{\mathbf{L}} - \mu k\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt}.
$$

Since $\mathbf{L}$ is conserved ($\dot{\mathbf{L}} = 0$) and $\dot{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{F} = -\frac{k}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{p}}\times\mathbf{L} = -\frac{k}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}) = -\frac{k}{r^2}[\mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r})].
$$

Using $\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r} = r$ and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{p} = \mu\dot{r}$:

$$
= -\frac{k}{r^2}[\mu\dot{r}\mathbf{r} - r\mathbf{p}] = -\frac{k\mu\dot{r}}{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{k}{r}\mathbf{p}/\mu\cdot\mu = \frac{k\mathbf{p}}{r} - \frac{k\mu\dot{r}}{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}}.
$$

Wait — let me redo this more carefully. Using the BAC-CAB rule $\mathbf{A}\times(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{C}) - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B})$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{r}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{r}\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{r} - \mathbf{p}\cdot r.
$$

So $\dot{\mathbf{p}}\times\mathbf{L} = -\frac{k}{r^2}\left[\frac{\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p})}{r} - r\mathbf{p}\right] = -\frac{k(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p})}{r^3}\mathbf{r} + \frac{k}{r}\mathbf{p}$.

Now compute $\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r} = \frac{\dot{\mathbf{r}}}{r} - \frac{\dot{r}}{r^2}\mathbf{r} = \frac{\mathbf{p}}{\mu r} - \frac{(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p})}{\mu r^3}\mathbf{r}$.

Therefore:

$$
\mu k\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} = \frac{k\mathbf{p}}{r} - \frac{k(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p})}{r^3}\mathbf{r}.
$$

Comparing: $\frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{p}}\times\mathbf{L} - \mu k\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{dt} = 0$. $\blacksquare$

**Geometric meaning:** $\mathbf{A}$ points from the focus to the periapsis of the ellipse, and its magnitude is $|\mathbf{A}| = \mu ke$, where $e$ is the eccentricity. The direction of $\mathbf{A}$ fixes the orientation of the ellipse in the orbital plane — this is why the orbit doesn't precess.

**Connection to orbit equation:** Taking $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{r}$:

$$
\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{r} = (\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{L})\cdot\mathbf{r} - \mu kr = \mathbf{L}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}) - \mu kr = L^2 - \mu kr.
$$

Also $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{r} = Ar\cos\theta = \mu ke\cdot r\cos\theta$. Therefore:

$$
\mu ke\,r\cos\theta = L^2 - \mu kr \implies r = \frac{L^2/(\mu k)}{1 + e\cos\theta} = \frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta}.
$$

This is the **conic section orbit equation**, derived purely from the conservation of $\mathbf{A}$.

**Why orbits precess when $V \neq -k/r$:** For any potential other than the pure $1/r$ Coulomb/gravitational potential, the LRL vector is no longer conserved. The orbit still closes instantaneously (it's still an ellipse at each moment), but the ellipse slowly rotates — this is orbital precession. Mercury's perihelion precession (43"/century from GR) is precisely the breakdown of LRL conservation due to relativistic corrections to the $1/r$ potential.

*References: Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, §3.9; Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §15; MIT 8.01 Advanced Topics.*

### 9.3 Effective Potential and Orbit Classification

The effective potential method reduces the two-dimensional orbital problem to an equivalent one-dimensional radial problem. This provides a powerful graphical tool for classifying orbits without solving the full equations of motion.

**Setup:** For a particle of reduced mass $\mu$ with angular momentum $L$ in a central potential $V(r)$, the total energy is:

$$
E = \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + V_{\text{eff}}(r), \quad \text{where } V_{\text{eff}}(r) = V(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.
$$

The term $L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ is the **centrifugal barrier** — it prevents the particle from reaching $r = 0$ when $L \neq 0$.

**For the gravitational potential** $V(r) = -k/r$ (with $k = GMm$):

$$
V_{\text{eff}}(r) = -\frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.
$$

**Finding the minimum:** $\frac{dV_{\text{eff}}}{dr} = \frac{k}{r^2} - \frac{L^2}{\mu r^3} = 0 \implies r_0 = \frac{L^2}{\mu k}$.

This is the radius of the circular orbit. The energy at this radius:

$$
V_{\text{eff}}(r_0) = -\frac{k}{r_0} + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r_0^2} = -\frac{\mu k^2}{2L^2} = E_{\text{min}}.
$$

**Orbit classification by energy:**

| Energy $E$ | Orbit type | Eccentricity | Bounded? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $E = E_{\text{min}} = -\mu k^2/(2L^2)$ | Circle | $e = 0$ | Yes |
| $E_{\text{min}} < E < 0$ | Ellipse | $0 < e < 1$ | Yes |
| $E = 0$ | Parabola | $e = 1$ | No (marginally) |
| $E > 0$ | Hyperbola | $e > 1$ | No |

**Turning points:** The radial turning points (where $\dot{r} = 0$) satisfy $E = V_{\text{eff}}(r)$:

$$
E = -\frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2} \implies \mu Er^2 + \mu kr - \frac{L^2}{2} = 0.
$$

For $E < 0$ (bound orbits), this quadratic in $r$ has two positive roots: $r_{\min}$ (periapsis) and $r_{\max}$ (apoapsis).

**Apsidal angle:** The angle swept between successive periapsis passages is:

$$
\Delta\phi = 2\int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\frac{(L/r^2)\,dr}{\sqrt{2\mu(E - V_{\text{eff}}(r))}} = 2\int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\frac{L\,dr}{r^2\sqrt{2\mu E + 2\mu k/r - L^2/r^2}}.
$$

For the $1/r$ potential, this integral evaluates to exactly $\pi$, giving $\Delta\phi = 2\pi$ — the orbit closes after one revolution. For any other potential, $\Delta\phi \neq 2\pi$ and the orbit precesses.

*References: Goldstein, §3.3; Taylor, Classical Mechanics, §8.4–8.6; MIT 8.01 Lecture 24.*
