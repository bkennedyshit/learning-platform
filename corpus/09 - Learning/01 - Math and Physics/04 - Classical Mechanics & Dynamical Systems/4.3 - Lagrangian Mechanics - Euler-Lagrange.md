# 4.3 — Lagrangian Mechanics: Euler-Lagrange

> *"Mechanics is the paradise of the mathematical sciences because by means of it one comes to the fruits of mathematics."* — Joseph-Louis Lagrange, 1788

Lagrangian mechanics reformulates dynamics in terms of generalized coordinates and energies rather than forces and accelerations. The power of this approach lies in its coordinate-independence, its natural handling of constraints, and its direct connection to symmetries and conservation laws via Noether's theorem.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Identify generalized coordinates for systems with holonomic constraints.
2. Construct the Lagrangian $L = T - U$ in generalized coordinates.
3. Derive and solve the Euler-Lagrange equations for multi-degree-of-freedom systems.
4. Handle velocity-dependent potentials (e.g., electromagnetic Lagrangian).
5. State and prove **Noether's theorem** connecting symmetries to conservation laws.
6. Apply Lagrange multipliers for non-holonomic and semi-holonomic constraints.
7. Analyze coupled oscillators via the Lagrangian and find normal modes using [[2.6 - Eigenvalues Eigenvectors & Diagonalization]].

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 4.3.1 — Generalized Coordinates

For a system of $N$ particles subject to $k$ holonomic constraints, the **generalized coordinates** $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$ (where $n = 3N - k$) are any set of $n$ independent parameters that completely specify the configuration. They need not be Cartesian.

### Definition 4.3.2 — Holonomic Constraint

A constraint is **holonomic** if it can be expressed as $f(\mathbf{r}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{r}_N, t) = 0$. Examples: rigid rod ($|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|^2 - \ell^2 = 0$), bead on wire, particle on surface.

### Definition 4.3.3 — The Lagrangian

The **Lagrangian** of a mechanical system is:

$$
L(q_1, \ldots, q_n, \dot{q}_1, \ldots, \dot{q}_n, t) = T - U,
$$

where $T$ is kinetic energy and $U$ is potential energy, both expressed in generalized coordinates.

### Definition 4.3.4 — Generalized Momentum (Conjugate Momentum)

The **generalized momentum** conjugate to coordinate $q_k$ is:

$$
p_k = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}.
$$

### Definition 4.3.5 — Cyclic (Ignorable) Coordinate

A coordinate $q_k$ is **cyclic** if $L$ does not depend on it explicitly: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} = 0$. The Euler-Lagrange equation then gives $\dot{p}_k = 0$, so $p_k$ is conserved.

### Definition 4.3.6 — Generalized Force

The **generalized force** associated with $q_k$ is:

$$
Q_k = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{F}_i \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_k}.
$$

For conservative forces derivable from a potential: $Q_k = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial q_k}$.

---

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 4.3.A1 — D'Alembert's Principle

The virtual work done by constraint forces vanishes for any virtual displacement consistent with the constraints:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^N (\mathbf{F}_i - m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i) \cdot \delta\mathbf{r}_i = 0.
$$

This bridges Newtonian mechanics to the Lagrangian formulation by eliminating constraint forces from the equations of motion.

### Axiom 4.3.A2 — Lagrangian Equivalence

Two Lagrangians $L$ and $L' = L + \frac{d}{dt}f(q, t)$ yield identical equations of motion. The Euler-Lagrange equations are invariant under addition of a total time derivative.

---

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 4.3.1 — Kinetic Energy in Generalized Coordinates

If $\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{r}_i(q_1, \ldots, q_n, t)$, then:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N m_i |\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^n M_{jk}(q)\,\dot{q}_j\dot{q}_k + \sum_{j=1}^n a_j(q,t)\dot{q}_j + b(q,t),
$$

where $M_{jk} = \sum_i m_i \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_k}$ is the **mass matrix** (symmetric, positive-definite).

**Proof.** By the chain rule: $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i = \sum_j \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}\dot{q}_j + \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial t}$. Squaring and summing gives the quadratic form in $\dot{q}$. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 4.3.2 — Euler-Lagrange from D'Alembert

Starting from D'Alembert's principle and expressing virtual displacements in generalized coordinates $\delta\mathbf{r}_i = \sum_k \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_k}\delta q_k$, one obtains:

$$
\sum_k \left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\right) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_k} - Q_k\right]\delta q_k = 0.
$$

Since the $\delta q_k$ are independent, each bracket vanishes, yielding the Euler-Lagrange equations.

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 4.3.1 — Euler-Lagrange Equations of Motion

For a system with Lagrangian $L(q, \dot{q}, t)$, the equations of motion are:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} = 0, \quad k = 1, \ldots, n.
$$

### Theorem 4.3.2 — Noether's Theorem

If the action $S = \int L\,dt$ is invariant under a continuous transformation $q_k \to q_k + \varepsilon\,\xi_k(q,t)$ (to first order in $\varepsilon$), then the quantity:

$$
I = \sum_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\xi_k
$$

is conserved along physical trajectories ($dI/dt = 0$).

**Corollaries:**
- Time-translation invariance ($\xi = 0$, $t \to t + \varepsilon$) → energy conservation: $H = \sum_k p_k\dot{q}_k - L = \text{const}$.
- Spatial translation invariance → linear momentum conservation.
- Rotational invariance → angular momentum conservation.

### Theorem 4.3.3 — Energy Function (Jacobi Integral)

Define the energy function:

$$
h = \sum_k \dot{q}_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k} - L.
$$

If $\frac{\partial L}{\partial t} = 0$, then $\frac{dh}{dt} = 0$ (energy is conserved). When the constraints are scleronomic (time-independent) and $T$ is a homogeneous quadratic in $\dot{q}$, then $h = T + U = E$.

### Theorem 4.3.4 — Normal Modes of Coupled Oscillators

For small oscillations about stable equilibrium, the Lagrangian takes the form $L = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^T M \dot{\mathbf{q}} - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^T K \mathbf{q}$. The normal mode frequencies $\omega_k$ satisfy:

$$
\det(K - \omega^2 M) = 0.
$$

This is a generalized eigenvalue problem (see [[2.6 - Eigenvalues Eigenvectors & Diagonalization]]).

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Proof of Noether's Theorem

**Proof.** Consider an infinitesimal transformation $q_k \to q_k + \varepsilon\,\xi_k(q,t)$ with $\varepsilon \ll 1$. The action changes by:

$$
\delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left[\sum_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}\varepsilon\xi_k + \sum_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\varepsilon\dot\xi_k\right]dt.
$$

Integrate the second term by parts. Set $u = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}$ and $dv = \dot\xi_k\,dt$, so $v = \xi_k$:

$$
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\dot\xi_k\,dt = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\xi_k\right]_{t_1}^{t_2} - \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\right)\xi_k\,dt.
$$

Substituting back:

$$
\delta S = \varepsilon\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum_k\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} - \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\right)\right]\xi_k\,dt + \varepsilon\left[\sum_k\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_k}\xi_k\right]_{t_1}^{t_2}.
$$

On a physical trajectory, the Euler-Lagrange equations hold, so the integral vanishes. If the action is invariant ($\delta S = 0$), the boundary term must also vanish for arbitrary $t_1, t_2$:

$$
\left[\sum_k p_k\,\xi_k\right]_{t_1}^{t_2} = 0 \implies \frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_k p_k\,\xi_k\right) = 0.
$$

Therefore $I = \sum_k p_k\,\xi_k$ is conserved. $\blacksquare$

### 5.2 Derivation — Double Pendulum Equations of Motion

Consider two masses $m_1, m_2$ connected by rigid rods of lengths $\ell_1, \ell_2$. Generalized coordinates: angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$.

**Step 1: Positions.**

$$
x_1 = \ell_1\sin\theta_1, \quad y_1 = -\ell_1\cos\theta_1.
$$

$$
x_2 = \ell_1\sin\theta_1 + \ell_2\sin\theta_2, \quad y_2 = -\ell_1\cos\theta_1 - \ell_2\cos\theta_2.
$$

**Step 2: Velocities.**

$$
\dot{x}_1 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\cos\theta_1, \quad \dot{y}_1 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\sin\theta_1.
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\cos\theta_1 + \ell_2\dot\theta_2\cos\theta_2, \quad \dot{y}_2 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\sin\theta_1 + \ell_2\dot\theta_2\sin\theta_2.
$$

**Step 3: Kinetic energy.**

$$
T_1 = \frac{1}{2}m_1\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2.
$$

$$
T_2 = \frac{1}{2}m_2\left[\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + 2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\right].
$$

The cross term arises from expanding $|\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2|^2 = \dot{x}_2^2 + \dot{y}_2^2$ and using $\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)$.

**Step 4: Potential energy.**

$$
U = -m_1 g\ell_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2 g(\ell_1\cos\theta_1 + \ell_2\cos\theta_2).
$$

**Step 5: Lagrangian.** $L = T_1 + T_2 - U$.

**Step 6: Euler-Lagrange for $\theta_1$.** After computing all partial derivatives:

$$
(m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2\ddot\theta_1 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\ddot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_2^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + (m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\sin\theta_1 = 0.
$$

**Step 7: Euler-Lagrange for $\theta_2$:**

$$
m_2\ell_2^2\ddot\theta_2 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\ddot\theta_1\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) - m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + m_2 g\ell_2\sin\theta_2 = 0.
$$

These coupled nonlinear ODEs exhibit chaotic behavior for large amplitudes (see [[4.8 - Non-linear Oscillators & Chaos Theory]]).

### 5.3 Derivation — Normal Modes of Two Coupled Oscillators

Two identical masses $m$ connected by springs of constant $k$ (to walls) and $\kappa$ (between masses). Small displacements $x_1, x_2$ from equilibrium.

**Step 1:** $T = \frac{1}{2}m(\dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{x}_2^2)$, $U = \frac{1}{2}k x_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}\kappa(x_2-x_1)^2 + \frac{1}{2}k x_2^2$.

**Step 2:** In matrix form: $M = mI_2$, $K = \begin{pmatrix}k+\kappa & -\kappa \\ -\kappa & k+\kappa\end{pmatrix}$.

**Step 3:** Solve $\det(K - \omega^2 M) = 0$:

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}k+\kappa-m\omega^2 & -\kappa \\ -\kappa & k+\kappa-m\omega^2\end{pmatrix} = (k+\kappa-m\omega^2)^2 - \kappa^2 = 0.
$$

$$
k+\kappa-m\omega^2 = \pm\kappa.
$$

$$
\omega_1^2 = \frac{k}{m} \quad (\text{symmetric mode}), \quad \omega_2^2 = \frac{k+2\kappa}{m} \quad (\text{antisymmetric mode}).
$$

**Step 4:** Eigenvectors: $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1)^T$ (in-phase), $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1,-1)^T$ (out-of-phase).

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 4.3.1 — Bead on a Rotating Hoop

**Problem:** A bead of mass $m$ slides frictionlessly on a circular hoop of radius $R$ that rotates about a vertical diameter with constant angular velocity $\Omega$. Find the equation of motion and equilibrium positions.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Generalized coordinate: angle $\theta$ from the bottom of the hoop. Position in cylindrical coordinates: $\rho = R\sin\theta$, $z = -R\cos\theta$.

**Step 2:** Kinetic energy. The bead has velocity from $\dot\theta$ along the hoop and from rotation $\Omega$ about the vertical:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}m\left(R^2\dot\theta^2 + R^2\sin^2\theta\,\Omega^2\right).
$$

**Step 3:** Potential energy: $U = -mgR\cos\theta$.

**Step 4:** Lagrangian:

$$
L = \frac{1}{2}mR^2\dot\theta^2 + \frac{1}{2}mR^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta + mgR\cos\theta.
$$

**Step 5:** Euler-Lagrange equation:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta} = mR^2\dot\theta, \quad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta} = mR^2\ddot\theta.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} = mR^2\Omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta - mgR\sin\theta.
$$

$$
mR^2\ddot\theta = mR^2\Omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta - mgR\sin\theta.
$$

$$
\ddot\theta = \Omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta - \frac{g}{R}\sin\theta = \sin\theta\left(\Omega^2\cos\theta - \frac{g}{R}\right).
$$

**Step 6:** Equilibria ($\ddot\theta = 0$): either $\sin\theta = 0$ (bottom $\theta=0$ or top $\theta=\pi$), or $\cos\theta_0 = \frac{g}{R\Omega^2}$ (exists only if $\Omega^2 \gt  g/R$).

</details>

### Example 4.3.2 — Charged Particle in Electromagnetic Field

**Problem:** Show that the Lagrangian $L = \frac{1}{2}m|\dot{\mathbf{r}}|^2 + q\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A} - q\phi$ yields the Lorentz force law.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** The Lagrangian is $L = \frac{1}{2}m(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2) + q(\dot{x}A_x + \dot{y}A_y + \dot{z}A_z) - q\phi$.

**Step 2:** Euler-Lagrange for $x$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} = m\dot{x} + qA_x.
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot{x} + qA_x) = m\ddot{x} + q\frac{dA_x}{dt} = m\ddot{x} + q\left(\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial t} + \dot{x}\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \dot{y}\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} + \dot{z}\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}\right).
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = q\left(\dot{x}\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \dot{y}\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} + \dot{z}\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}\right) - q\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}.
$$

**Step 3:** The Euler-Lagrange equation gives:

$$
m\ddot{x} = -q\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} - q\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial t} + q\left[\dot{y}\left(\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}\right) + \dot{z}\left(\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}\right)\right].
$$

Recognizing $E_x = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial t}$ and $(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})_x = \dot{y}B_z - \dot{z}B_y = \dot{y}(\partial_x A_y - \partial_y A_x) + \dot{z}(\partial_x A_z - \partial_z A_x)$:

$$
m\ddot{x} = q(E_x + (\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})_x).
$$

This is the $x$-component of the **Lorentz force**: $m\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$.

</details>

### Example 4.3.3 — Atwood Machine via Lagrangian

**Problem:** Re-derive the Atwood machine (masses $m_1, m_2$ on a massless string over a pulley) using Lagrangian mechanics.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** One degree of freedom. Let $x$ = displacement of $m_1$ downward from some reference. Then $m_2$ moves up by $x$. Generalized coordinate: $x$.

**Step 2:** $T = \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)\dot{x}^2$, $U = -m_1 gx + m_2 gx = -(m_1-m_2)gx$.

**Step 3:** $L = \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)\dot{x}^2 + (m_1-m_2)gx$.

**Step 4:** Euler-Lagrange: $(m_1+m_2)\ddot{x} = (m_1-m_2)g$, giving $a = \frac{(m_1-m_2)g}{m_1+m_2}$.

This matches the Newtonian result from [[4.1 - Newtonian Dynamics & Conservation Laws]] with far less effort — no need to solve for the constraint force (tension).

</details>

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — The Double Pendulum: Equations of Motion

**Problem:** Derive the full equations of motion for a double pendulum (two point masses $m_1, m_2$ on massless rods of lengths $\ell_1, \ell_2$) using the Lagrangian method. Identify the nonlinear coupling terms.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Choose generalized coordinates

Let $\theta_1$ = angle of first rod from vertical, $\theta_2$ = angle of second rod from vertical. Two degrees of freedom.

#### Step 2: Position vectors

Mass 1: $x_1 = \ell_1\sin\theta_1$, $y_1 = -\ell_1\cos\theta_1$.

Mass 2: $x_2 = \ell_1\sin\theta_1 + \ell_2\sin\theta_2$, $y_2 = -\ell_1\cos\theta_1 - \ell_2\cos\theta_2$.

#### Step 3: Velocities

$$
\dot{x}_1 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\cos\theta_1, \quad \dot{y}_1 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\sin\theta_1.
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\cos\theta_1 + \ell_2\dot\theta_2\cos\theta_2, \quad \dot{y}_2 = \ell_1\dot\theta_1\sin\theta_1 + \ell_2\dot\theta_2\sin\theta_2.
$$

#### Step 4: Kinetic energy

$$
T_1 = \frac{1}{2}m_1(\dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{y}_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}m_1\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2.
$$

$$
T_2 = \frac{1}{2}m_2(\dot{x}_2^2 + \dot{y}_2^2).
$$

Expand $\dot{x}_2^2 + \dot{y}_2^2$:

$$
= \ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\cos^2\theta_1 + 2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2\cos^2\theta_2
$$

$$
+ \ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\sin^2\theta_1 + 2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 + \ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2\sin^2\theta_2.
$$

Using $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ and $\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)$:

$$
\dot{x}_2^2 + \dot{y}_2^2 = \ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + 2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2).
$$

Total kinetic energy:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2\ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2).
$$

#### Step 5: Potential energy

$$
V = m_1 g y_1 + m_2 g y_2 = -(m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2 g\ell_2\cos\theta_2.
$$

#### Step 6: Lagrangian

$$
L = T - V = \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2\ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)
$$

$$
+ (m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g\ell_2\cos\theta_2.
$$

#### Step 7: Euler-Lagrange for $\theta_1$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta_1} = (m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2).
$$

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta_1} = (m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2\ddot\theta_1 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\ddot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) - m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_2(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2).
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_1} = -m_2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) - (m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\sin\theta_1.
$$

Setting $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta_1} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_1} = 0$:

$$
(m_1+m_2)\ell_1\ddot\theta_1 + m_2\ell_2\ddot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) + m_2\ell_2\dot\theta_2^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + (m_1+m_2)g\sin\theta_1 = 0.
$$

#### Step 8: Euler-Lagrange for $\theta_2$

By analogous computation:

$$
m_2\ell_2\ddot\theta_2 + m_2\ell_1\ddot\theta_1\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) - m_2\ell_1\dot\theta_1^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + m_2 g\sin\theta_2 = 0.
$$

#### Step 9: Identify nonlinear coupling

The terms $\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)$, $\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2)$, $\dot\theta_1^2\sin(\cdot)$, and $\dot\theta_2^2\sin(\cdot)$ make these equations **nonlinear and coupled**. This system exhibits chaotic behavior for large amplitudes — see [[4.8 - Non-linear Oscillators & Chaos Theory]].

**Final Answer:**

$$
(m_1+m_2)\ell_1\ddot\theta_1 + m_2\ell_2\ddot\theta_2\cos\Delta + m_2\ell_2\dot\theta_2^2\sin\Delta + (m_1+m_2)g\sin\theta_1 = 0
$$

$$
\ell_2\ddot\theta_2 + \ell_1\ddot\theta_1\cos\Delta - \ell_1\dot\theta_1^2\sin\Delta + g\sin\theta_2 = 0
$$

where $\Delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2$.

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Bead on a Rotating Wire (Non-Inertial Frame Effects)

**Problem:** A bead of mass $m$ slides without friction on a straight wire that rotates about a vertical axis at constant angular velocity $\omega$. The wire makes angle $\alpha$ with the vertical. Find the equation of motion for the bead's position along the wire.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Set up coordinates

Let $q$ be the distance of the bead from the pivot along the wire. The wire rotates at angular velocity $\omega$ about the vertical ($z$) axis. The bead's position in cylindrical coordinates:

$$
\rho = q\sin\alpha, \quad z = q\cos\alpha, \quad \phi = \omega t.
$$

#### Step 2: Kinetic energy

In cylindrical coordinates: $T = \frac{1}{2}m(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\phi^2 + \dot{z}^2)$.

Since $\rho = q\sin\alpha$, $z = q\cos\alpha$, $\phi = \omega t$:

$$
\dot\rho = \dot{q}\sin\alpha, \quad \dot{z} = \dot{q}\cos\alpha, \quad \dot\phi = \omega.
$$

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}m\left(\dot{q}^2\sin^2\alpha + q^2\sin^2\alpha\cdot\omega^2 + \dot{q}^2\cos^2\alpha\right) = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2\sin^2\alpha.
$$

#### Step 3: Potential energy

$$
V = mgz = mgq\cos\alpha.
$$

#### Step 4: Lagrangian

$$
L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 q^2\sin^2\alpha - mgq\cos\alpha.
$$

#### Step 5: Euler-Lagrange equation

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = m\omega^2 q\sin^2\alpha - mg\cos\alpha.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}} = m\dot{q} \implies \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}} = m\ddot{q}.
$$

Equation of motion:

$$
m\ddot{q} = m\omega^2 q\sin^2\alpha - mg\cos\alpha.
$$

$$
\ddot{q} = \omega^2\sin^2\alpha\cdot q - g\cos\alpha.
$$

#### Step 6: Analyze the solution

This is a linear ODE: $\ddot{q} - \omega^2\sin^2\alpha\cdot q = -g\cos\alpha$.

The homogeneous solution grows exponentially: $q_h = Ae^{\omega\sin\alpha\cdot t} + Be^{-\omega\sin\alpha\cdot t}$.

Particular solution (constant): $q_p = \frac{g\cos\alpha}{\omega^2\sin^2\alpha}$.

This is an **equilibrium position** where the centrifugal force component along the wire balances gravity:

$$
q_{\text{eq}} = \frac{g\cos\alpha}{\omega^2\sin^2\alpha}.
$$

The equilibrium is **unstable** (the exponential growth in $q_h$ means any perturbation grows). The bead slides outward if displaced beyond $q_{\text{eq}}$.

**Final Answer:**

$$
\ddot{q} = \omega^2\sin^2\alpha\cdot q - g\cos\alpha, \quad q_{\text{eq}} = \frac{g\cos\alpha}{\omega^2\sin^2\alpha}
$$

**Problem:** Three masses $m_1$, $m_2$, $m_3$ are connected by inextensible strings over two pulleys as shown: $m_1$ hangs on the left of pulley 1, a string connects over pulley 1 to pulley 2 (which hangs freely), and $m_2$, $m_3$ hang on either side of pulley 2. All pulleys are massless and frictionless. Find the accelerations using Lagrangian mechanics.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Identify constraints and generalized coordinates

Let $x$ = downward displacement of $m_1$ from some reference. The string over pulley 1 has fixed length, so pulley 2 moves up by $x$.

Let $y$ = downward displacement of $m_2$ relative to pulley 2. Then $m_3$ moves up by $y$ relative to pulley 2.

Two generalized coordinates: $(x, y)$.

#### Step 2: Absolute positions (downward positive)

- $m_1$: position $= x$ (downward from reference)
- Pulley 2: position $= -x$ (moves up when $m_1$ goes down)
- $m_2$: position $= -x + y$ (pulley 2 position + relative displacement)
- $m_3$: position $= -x - y$

#### Step 3: Velocities

$$
\dot{z}_1 = \dot{x}, \quad \dot{z}_2 = -\dot{x}+\dot{y}, \quad \dot{z}_3 = -\dot{x}-\dot{y}.
$$

#### Step 4: Kinetic energy

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}m_1\dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2(-\dot{x}+\dot{y})^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_3(-\dot{x}-\dot{y})^2.
$$

Expand:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}m_1\dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2(\dot{x}^2 - 2\dot{x}\dot{y} + \dot{y}^2) + \frac{1}{2}m_3(\dot{x}^2 + 2\dot{x}\dot{y} + \dot{y}^2).
$$

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}(m_1+m_2+m_3)\dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}(m_2+m_3)\dot{y}^2 + (m_3-m_2)\dot{x}\dot{y}.
$$

#### Step 5: Potential energy (taking reference at initial positions)

$$
V = -m_1 gx - m_2 g(-x+y) - m_3 g(-x-y) = -m_1 gx + (m_2+m_3)gx - (m_2-m_3)gy.
$$

$$
V = (m_2+m_3-m_1)gx - (m_2-m_3)gy.
$$

#### Step 6: Lagrangian and Euler-Lagrange equations

$L = T - V$. For coordinate $x$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}} = (m_1+m_2+m_3)\dot{x} + (m_3-m_2)\dot{y}.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = -(m_2+m_3-m_1)g = (m_1-m_2-m_3)g.
$$

EL equation for $x$:

$$
(m_1+m_2+m_3)\ddot{x} + (m_3-m_2)\ddot{y} = (m_1-m_2-m_3)g. \tag{1}
$$

For coordinate $y$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{y}} = (m_2+m_3)\dot{y} + (m_3-m_2)\dot{x}.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = (m_2-m_3)g.
$$

EL equation for $y$:

$$
(m_2+m_3)\ddot{y} + (m_3-m_2)\ddot{x} = (m_2-m_3)g. \tag{2}
$$

#### Step 7: Solve the linear system

From (2): $\ddot{y} = \frac{(m_2-m_3)g - (m_3-m_2)\ddot{x}}{m_2+m_3} = \frac{(m_2-m_3)(g+\ddot{x})}{m_2+m_3}$.

Substitute into (1):

$$
(m_1+m_2+m_3)\ddot{x} + (m_3-m_2)\cdot\frac{(m_2-m_3)(g+\ddot{x})}{m_2+m_3} = (m_1-m_2-m_3)g.
$$

$$
(m_1+m_2+m_3)\ddot{x} - \frac{(m_2-m_3)^2}{m_2+m_3}\ddot{x} = (m_1-m_2-m_3)g + \frac{(m_2-m_3)^2}{m_2+m_3}g.
$$

$$
\ddot{x}\left[\frac{(m_1+m_2+m_3)(m_2+m_3) - (m_2-m_3)^2}{m_2+m_3}\right] = g\left[\frac{(m_1-m_2-m_3)(m_2+m_3) + (m_2-m_3)^2}{m_2+m_3}\right].
$$

Simplify numerator of LHS: $m_1(m_2+m_3) + (m_2+m_3)^2 - (m_2-m_3)^2 = m_1(m_2+m_3) + 4m_2 m_3$.

Simplify numerator of RHS: $m_1 m_2 + m_1 m_3 - (m_2+m_3)^2 + (m_2-m_3)^2 = m_1(m_2+m_3) - 4m_2 m_3$.

$$
\ddot{x} = \frac{m_1(m_2+m_3) - 4m_2 m_3}{m_1(m_2+m_3) + 4m_2 m_3}\,g.
$$

**Final Answer:**

$$
\ddot{x} = \frac{m_1(m_2+m_3) - 4m_2 m_3}{m_1(m_2+m_3) + 4m_2 m_3}\,g
$$

**Check:** If $m_2 = m_3 = m$: $\ddot{x} = \frac{2m_1 m - 4m^2}{2m_1 m + 4m^2} = \frac{m_1 - 2m}{m_1 + 2m}\,g$. This is the standard Atwood result with effective mass $2m$ on one side. ✓

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 D'Alembert's Principle and the Derivation of Lagrange's Equations

D'Alembert's principle bridges Newtonian mechanics and Lagrangian mechanics. It provides the conceptual foundation for why the Euler-Lagrange equations work, especially for constrained systems.

**Newton's second law for particle $i$:**

$$
\mathbf{F}_i^{(a)} + \mathbf{f}_i = m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i,
$$

where $\mathbf{F}_i^{(a)}$ is the applied (physical) force and $\mathbf{f}_i$ is the constraint force.

**D'Alembert's Principle:** The constraint forces do no virtual work:

$$
\sum_i (\mathbf{F}_i^{(a)} - m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_i = 0,
$$

where $\delta\mathbf{r}_i$ is a virtual displacement consistent with the constraints (an infinitesimal displacement at fixed time).

**Step 1: Transform to generalized coordinates.** Let $\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{r}_i(q_1, \ldots, q_n, t)$. Then:

$$
\delta\mathbf{r}_i = \sum_j \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}\delta q_j.
$$

**Step 2: Substitute into D'Alembert's principle:**

$$
\sum_i\sum_j\left(\mathbf{F}_i^{(a)}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j} - m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}\right)\delta q_j = 0.
$$

**Step 3: Define the generalized force:**

$$
Q_j = \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i^{(a)}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}.
$$

**Step 4: Transform the inertial term.** Using the identities:

$$
\frac{\partial\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i}{\partial\dot{q}_j} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j}, \quad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{\partial\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i}{\partial q_j},
$$

one can show (after careful algebra — see Goldstein §1.4):

$$
\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{q}_j} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j}.
$$

**Step 5: D'Alembert's principle becomes:**

$$
\sum_j\left[Q_j - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{q}_j} + \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j}\right]\delta q_j = 0.
$$

For independent generalized coordinates (holonomic constraints), the $\delta q_j$ are independent, so each bracket must vanish:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{q}_j} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j} = Q_j.
$$

**Step 6: For conservative forces** ($Q_j = -\partial V/\partial q_j$ with $V$ independent of $\dot{q}_j$):

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial(T-V)}{\partial\dot{q}_j} - \frac{\partial(T-V)}{\partial q_j} = 0 \implies \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_j} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = 0.
$$

These are **Lagrange's equations**.

**For non-conservative forces** (e.g., friction, velocity-dependent forces), the generalized force $Q_j$ remains on the right side:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_j} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = Q_j^{\text{nc}},
$$

where $Q_j^{\text{nc}}$ includes only the non-conservative part. This is how dissipation (Rayleigh dissipation function) enters the Lagrangian framework.

*References: Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, §1.4–1.6; Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §1–2; Feynman Lectures Vol. I, Ch. 19.*

### 9.2 Noether's Theorem: Symmetries and Conservation Laws

Emmy Noether's theorem (1918) is one of the deepest results in theoretical physics: every continuous symmetry of the action corresponds to a conserved quantity.

**Statement:** If the Lagrangian $L(q, \dot{q}, t)$ is invariant under a continuous transformation $q_i \to q_i + \varepsilon\,K_i(q, t)$ (to first order in $\varepsilon$), then the quantity:

$$
I = \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}K_i
$$

is conserved along trajectories satisfying the Euler-Lagrange equations.

**Proof:** Under the transformation, $\delta q_i = \varepsilon K_i$ and $\delta\dot{q}_i = \varepsilon\dot{K}_i$. Invariance means:

$$
\delta L = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\varepsilon K_i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}\varepsilon\dot{K}_i\right) = 0.
$$

Using the Euler-Lagrange equation $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}$:

$$
\sum_i\left(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}\cdot K_i + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}\cdot\dot{K}_i\right) = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_i}K_i = 0.
$$

Therefore $I = \sum_i p_i K_i$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

**Examples:**
- **Translation symmetry** ($K_i = \text{const}$): conserves linear momentum $p_i$.
- **Rotation symmetry** ($K_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}q_j$): conserves angular momentum $L_k$.
- **Time translation** (if $\partial L/\partial t = 0$): conserves energy $H = \sum_i p_i\dot{q}_i - L$.

*References: Goldstein, Ch. 13; Susskind, The Theoretical Minimum, Lecture 7; Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §2.*
