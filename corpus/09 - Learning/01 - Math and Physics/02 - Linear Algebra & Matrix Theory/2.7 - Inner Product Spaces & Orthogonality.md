# 2.7 — Inner Product Spaces & Orthogonality

> *"Orthogonality is the single most fruitful idea in applied mathematics."* — Gilbert Strang

Every chapter so far has been about **algebraic structure** — linear combinations, kernels, determinants, eigenvectors. This chapter adds **geometric structure**: the notions of length, angle, and perpendicularity. Once you can measure angles, you can project, decompose, and approximate — and that triple of operations powers least-squares regression, Fourier analysis, QR decomposition, and the geometric heart of quantum mechanics.

The central engine is the **inner product**: an operation that eats two vectors and returns a number, generalizing the dot product of high-school physics to abstract vector spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ (and eventually $\mathbb{C}$).

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Define an inner product abstractly and verify the three axioms on a given space.
2. Compute norms and angles from an inner product.
3. State and prove the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the triangle inequality.
4. Define orthogonality, orthonormal bases, and orthogonal complements.
5. Execute the Gram-Schmidt algorithm and prove it produces an orthonormal basis.
6. Perform QR decomposition and explain what $Q$ and $R$ each represent geometrically.
7. Derive and apply the normal equations $A^\top A\hat{x} = A^\top b$ for least-squares.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 2.7.1 — Inner Product

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$. An **inner product** on $V$ is a function $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying, for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$:

**(IP1) Symmetry:**

$$
\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} \rangle.
$$

**(IP2) Linearity in the first argument (bilinearity):**

$$
\langle c\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = c\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle + \langle \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{v} \rangle.
$$

Combined with symmetry, this gives linearity in the second argument as well, making $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ **bilinear**.

**(IP3) Positive definiteness:**

$$
\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle \geq 0, \quad \text{and} \quad \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = 0 \iff \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}.
$$

A vector space equipped with an inner product is called an **inner product space**.

**Standard example.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$, the **dot product** $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$ satisfies all three axioms. This is the inner product we work with unless otherwise stated.

**Non-standard example.** On the space $C[a,b]$ of continuous functions on $[a,b]$:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f(x)\, g(x)\, dx.
$$

This satisfies all three axioms and is the inner product underlying Fourier series.


### Definition 2.7.2 — Norm Induced by an Inner Product

Given an inner product space $(V, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$, the **norm** (length) of a vector $\mathbf{v}$ is:

$$
\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle}.
$$

This satisfies the three norm axioms: (i) $\|\mathbf{v}\| \geq 0$ with equality iff $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ (from IP3); (ii) $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\|\mathbf{v}\|$ (from IP2); (iii) triangle inequality (proved as Theorem 2.7.2).

### Definition 2.7.3 — Orthogonality

Two vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$ are **orthogonal**, written $\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}$, if

$$
\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = 0.
$$

Note that $\mathbf{0}$ is orthogonal to every vector (by linearity: $\langle \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle 0 \cdot \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = 0 \cdot \langle \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = 0$).

### Definition 2.7.4 — Orthonormal Set / Basis

A set $\{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_k\}$ is:
- **Orthogonal** if $\langle q_i, q_j \rangle = 0$ for all $i \neq j$.
- **Orthonormal** if additionally $\|q_i\| = 1$ for all $i$, i.e., $\langle q_i, q_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$ (Kronecker delta).

An **orthonormal basis** for $V$ is an orthonormal set that spans $V$.

### Definition 2.7.5 — Orthogonal Complement

Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$. The **orthogonal complement** of $W$ is:

$$
W^\perp = \{ \mathbf{v} \in V : \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \rangle = 0 \text{ for all } \mathbf{w} \in W \}.
$$

$W^\perp$ is always a subspace of $V$ (closed under addition and scalar multiplication, contains $\mathbf{0}$).

**Fundamental theorem of orthogonal complements.** If $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
\mathbb{R}^n = W \oplus W^\perp, \quad \dim W + \dim W^\perp = n.
$$

That is, every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ decomposes **uniquely** as $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{w}^\perp$ with $\mathbf{w} \in W$ and $\mathbf{w}^\perp \in W^\perp$.

### Definition 2.7.6 — Orthogonal Matrix

A square matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is **orthogonal** if its columns form an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$, equivalently:

$$
Q^\top Q = I \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad Q^\top = Q^{-1} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad QQ^\top = I.
$$

For a **non-square** matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $m > n$ whose columns are orthonormal: $Q^\top Q = I_n$ (the $n \times n$ identity), but $QQ^\top \neq I_m$ in general — it is the **projection matrix** onto the column space of $Q$.

---

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

The three inner product axioms (IP1–IP3) in Definition 2.7.1 are the fundamental postulates. Everything else is derived from them plus the axioms of a vector space (from Ch. 2.3).

**Key consequence of IP1+IP2.** For any $c \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
\langle \mathbf{u}, c\mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle c\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} \rangle = c\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} \rangle = c\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle.
$$

So the inner product is linear in both arguments (bilinear) over $\mathbb{R}$.

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 2.7.1 — Expansion in an Orthonormal Basis

If $\{q_1, \ldots, q_n\}$ is an orthonormal basis for $V$, then every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ satisfies:

$$
\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n \langle \mathbf{v}, q_i \rangle\, q_i.
$$

**Proof.** Since $\{q_i\}$ is a basis, write $\mathbf{v} = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j q_j$ for some scalars $c_j$. Take the inner product of both sides with $q_i$:

$$
\langle \mathbf{v}, q_i \rangle = \left\langle \sum_j c_j q_j,\, q_i \right\rangle = \sum_j c_j \langle q_j, q_i \rangle = \sum_j c_j \delta_{ji} = c_i.
$$

Hence $c_i = \langle \mathbf{v}, q_i \rangle$ for each $i$. $\blacksquare$


### Lemma 2.7.2 — Orthogonal Sets Are Linearly Independent

If $\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$ is an orthogonal set with no zero vector, then the set is linearly independent.

**Proof.** Suppose $\sum_{i=1}^k c_i v_i = \mathbf{0}$. Take the inner product of both sides with $v_j$ for an arbitrary fixed $j$:

$$
0 = \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^k c_i v_i,\, v_j \right\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \langle v_i, v_j \rangle = c_j \langle v_j, v_j \rangle = c_j \|v_j\|^2.
$$

Since $v_j \neq \mathbf{0}$, we have $\|v_j\|^2 > 0$, so $c_j = 0$. Since $j$ was arbitrary, all $c_j = 0$. $\blacksquare$

## 👑 4. Major Theorems

### Theorem 2.7.1 — Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ in an inner product space:

$$
|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{v}\|,
$$

with equality iff $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are linearly dependent (one is a scalar multiple of the other).

### Theorem 2.7.2 — Triangle Inequality

For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ in an inner product space:

$$
\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|.
$$

### Theorem 2.7.3 — Pythagorean Theorem

If $\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}$, then $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$.

### Theorem 2.7.4 — Orthogonal Projection Formula

Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ with orthonormal basis $\{q_1, \ldots, q_k\}$ collected as columns of $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$. The **orthogonal projection** of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $W$ is:

$$
\text{proj}_W(\mathbf{b}) = QQ^\top \mathbf{b}.
$$

The projection matrix $P = QQ^\top$ satisfies $P^2 = P$ (idempotent) and $P^\top = P$ (symmetric).

### Theorem 2.7.5 — Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization

Given any basis $\{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_k\}$ for a subspace $W$, the algorithm below produces an orthonormal basis $\{q_1, \ldots, q_k\}$ for the same subspace $W$.

**Algorithm:**
1. $\mathbf{v}_1 = a_1$, then $q_1 = \mathbf{v}_1 / \|\mathbf{v}_1\|$.
2. For $j = 2, \ldots, k$: remove the components of $a_j$ in the directions already spanned:

$$
\mathbf{v}_j = a_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} \langle a_j, q_i \rangle\, q_i, \qquad q_j = \mathbf{v}_j / \|\mathbf{v}_j\|.
$$

### Theorem 2.7.6 — QR Decomposition

Every matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with linearly independent columns ($m \geq n$) has a factorization:

$$
A = QR,
$$

where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has orthonormal columns ($Q^\top Q = I_n$) and $R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries.

### Theorem 2.7.7 — Normal Equations (Least Squares)

Given $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (with $m > n$, full column rank) and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the least-squares solution minimizing $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2$ satisfies the **normal equations**:

$$
A^\top A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top \mathbf{b}.
$$

The unique solution is $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf{b}$, and the residual $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is perpendicular to the column space $C(A)$.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Proof of the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

**Case 1: $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.** Then both sides equal zero. ✓

**Case 2: $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$.** Consider the function $f(t) = \|\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v}\|^2 \geq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Expand using inner product linearity:

$$
f(t) = \langle \mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v},\, \mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v} \rangle = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 - 2t\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle + t^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2.
$$

This is a **quadratic in $t$** that is always non-negative. A non-negative quadratic $at^2 + bt + c \geq 0$ has discriminant $b^2 - 4ac \leq 0$. Identifying $a = \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$, $b = -2\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$, $c = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2$:

$$
(-2\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle)^2 - 4\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \leq 0.
$$

$$
4 \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle^2 \leq 4\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \|\mathbf{v}\|^2.
$$

Take the square root of both sides (both are non-negative):

$$
|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{v}\|. \quad \blacksquare
$$

**Equality condition.** $f(t) = 0$ for the minimizer $t^* = \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle / \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ iff $\mathbf{u} = t^* \mathbf{v}$, i.e., $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are proportional.


### 5.2 Proof of the Triangle Inequality

**Expand $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2$:**

$$
\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \langle \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v},\, \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \rangle = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2.
$$

**Apply Cauchy-Schwarz** to bound the middle term: $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle \leq |\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$:

$$
\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = (\|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|)^2.
$$

Take the square root (valid since both sides are non-negative):

$$
\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.3 Proof of the Pythagorean Theorem

Suppose $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = 0$. Then:

$$
\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 0 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.4 Proof of the Orthogonal Projection Formula

Let $Q = [q_1 | \cdots | q_k]$ with $Q^\top Q = I$. We want the vector $\mathbf{p} \in W$ minimizing $\|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}\|$. Since $\mathbf{p} \in W = C(Q)$, we can write $\mathbf{p} = Q\hat{\mathbf{c}}$ for some coefficient vector $\hat{\mathbf{c}} \in \mathbb{R}^k$.

The geometric condition: the error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$ must be perpendicular to every vector in $W$. In particular, $\mathbf{e} \perp q_j$ for each $j$, i.e., $Q^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
Q^\top(\mathbf{b} - Q\hat{\mathbf{c}}) = \mathbf{0} \implies Q^\top \mathbf{b} = Q^\top Q \hat{\mathbf{c}} = I \hat{\mathbf{c}} \implies \hat{\mathbf{c}} = Q^\top \mathbf{b}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{p} = Q\hat{\mathbf{c}} = QQ^\top \mathbf{b}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

**Projection matrix properties.** $P = QQ^\top$:
- **Idempotent:** $P^2 = (QQ^\top)(QQ^\top) = Q(Q^\top Q)Q^\top = QIQ^\top = QQ^\top = P$.
- **Symmetric:** $P^\top = (QQ^\top)^\top = (Q^\top)^\top Q^\top = QQ^\top = P$.

### 5.5 Proof That Gram-Schmidt Produces an Orthonormal Basis

**Claim 1: $\{q_1, \ldots, q_k\}$ is orthonormal.** We prove $\langle q_j, q_i \rangle = \delta_{ij}$ by induction on $j$.

*Base case $j=1$:* $q_1 = v_1/\|v_1\|$, so $\|q_1\| = 1$. ✓

*Inductive step:* Assume $q_1, \ldots, q_{j-1}$ are orthonormal. The new vector is:

$$
v_j = a_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} \langle a_j, q_i \rangle\, q_i.
$$

For any $\ell < j$, compute $\langle v_j, q_\ell \rangle$:

$$
\langle v_j, q_\ell \rangle = \langle a_j, q_\ell \rangle - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} \langle a_j, q_i \rangle \langle q_i, q_\ell \rangle = \langle a_j, q_\ell \rangle - \langle a_j, q_\ell \rangle \cdot 1 = 0.
$$

(Using $\langle q_i, q_\ell \rangle = \delta_{i\ell}$ by induction hypothesis.) Hence $v_j \perp q_\ell$ for all $\ell < j$. Setting $q_j = v_j / \|v_j\|$ gives $\|q_j\| = 1$ and preserves orthogonality.

**Claim 2: $\text{span}\{q_1, \ldots, q_j\} = \text{span}\{a_1, \ldots, a_j\}$ for each $j$.**

By construction, $q_j$ is a linear combination of $a_1, \ldots, a_j$ (the projection subtracts components in $\text{span}\{q_1, \ldots, q_{j-1}\} = \text{span}\{a_1, \ldots, a_{j-1}\}$). Inversely, $a_j = v_j + \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} \langle a_j, q_i \rangle q_i \in \text{span}\{q_1, \ldots, q_j\}$. By induction, the spans agree. $\blacksquare$

### 5.6 Proof of the Normal Equations (Least-Squares Derivation)

We want to minimize $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2$ over all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

**Geometric argument.** The set $\{A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\} = C(A)$ is the column space. The closest point in $C(A)$ to $\mathbf{b}$ is the orthogonal projection $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$. The error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ must be perpendicular to every column of $A$:

$$
A^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0} \implies A^\top(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0} \implies A^\top A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top \mathbf{b}.
$$

**Algebraic argument (completing the square).** Decompose:

$$
\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|A(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}) + (A\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{b})\|^2.
$$

The two terms are orthogonal (the second is $-\mathbf{e} \in C(A)^\perp$ and the first is in $C(A)$), so by Pythagoras:

$$
= \|A(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}})\|^2 + \|A\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{b}\|^2 \geq \|A\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{b}\|^2,
$$

with equality iff $\mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}$. $\blacksquare$

When $A$ has full column rank, $A^\top A$ is positive definite (hence invertible), and $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf{b}$.

## 🎯 6. Worked Examples

### Example 2.7.E1 — Dot Product Properties

Let $\mathbf{u} = (3, -1, 2)^\top$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 4, -2)^\top$. Compute $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$, $\|\mathbf{u}\|$, $\|\mathbf{v}\|$, and verify Cauchy-Schwarz.

**Step 1.** Dot product:

$$
\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = (3)(1) + (-1)(4) + (2)(-2) = 3 - 4 - 4 = -5.
$$

**Step 2.** Norms:

$$
\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{9 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{14}, \qquad \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{1 + 16 + 4} = \sqrt{21}.
$$

**Step 3.** Cauchy-Schwarz: $|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| = 5$ and $\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{14 \cdot 21} = \sqrt{294} \approx 17.15$. Indeed $5 \leq 17.15$. ✓

### Example 2.7.E2 — Angle Between Vectors

Find the angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ from Example E1.

The angle formula (derived from Cauchy-Schwarz, since $|\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle| = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$):

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \frac{-5}{\sqrt{294}}.
$$

$$
\theta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{-5}{\sqrt{294}}\right) \approx \arccos(-0.292) \approx 107°.
$$


### Example 2.7.E3 — Orthogonal Projection onto a Line

Project $\mathbf{b} = (2, 3)^\top$ onto the line spanned by $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2)^\top$.

**Step 1.** The projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto the direction $\mathbf{a}$ is:

$$
\text{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\langle \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{a} \rangle}{\langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{a} \rangle} \mathbf{a} = \frac{(2)(1) + (3)(2)}{(1)^2 + (2)^2} \mathbf{a} = \frac{8}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8/5 \\ 16/5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 2.** Verify the error is perpendicular:

$$
\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \text{proj} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 8/5 \\ 3 - 16/5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2/5 \\ -1/5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

$$
\langle \mathbf{e}, \mathbf{a} \rangle = (2/5)(1) + (-1/5)(2) = 2/5 - 2/5 = 0. \checkmark
$$

### Example 2.7.E4 — Gram-Schmidt for 3 Vectors

Apply Gram-Schmidt to $a_1 = (1,1,0)^\top$, $a_2 = (1,0,1)^\top$, $a_3 = (0,1,1)^\top$.

**Step 1:** $v_1 = a_1 = (1,1,0)^\top$. $\|v_1\| = \sqrt{2}$. So $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1,0)^\top$.

**Step 2:** Remove the $q_1$ component from $a_2$:

$$
\langle a_2, q_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[(1)(1)+(0)(1)+(1)(0)] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.
$$

$$
v_2 = a_2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\, q_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/2\\-1/2\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$\|v_2\| = \sqrt{1/4+1/4+1} = \sqrt{3/2}$. So $q_2 = \sqrt{2/3}\,(1/2,-1/2,1)^\top = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,-1,2)^\top$.

**Step 3:** Remove $q_1$ and $q_2$ components from $a_3$:

$$
\langle a_3, q_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[0+1+0] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad \langle a_3, q_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}[0 - 1 + 2] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}.
$$

$$
v_3 = a_3 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q_1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}q_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-2/3\\2/3\\2/3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$\|v_3\| = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \sqrt{3}/\sqrt{3} = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$. Normalize: $q_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,1,1)^\top$.

**Verify:** $\langle q_1, q_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{12}}(1-1+0) = 0$. ✓

### Example 2.7.E5 — QR Decomposition of a 3×2 Matrix

Decompose $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ into $A = QR$.

Using Gram-Schmidt on the columns $a_1 = (1,1,0)^\top$, $a_2 = (1,0,1)^\top$ (steps from E4):

$$
q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad q_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The $R$ entries: $r_{11} = \langle a_1, q_1 \rangle = \sqrt{2}$, $r_{12} = \langle a_2, q_1 \rangle = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $r_{22} = \langle a_2, q_2 \rangle = \|v_2\| = \sqrt{3/2} = \sqrt{6}/2$.

$$
Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{6} \\ 0 & 2/\sqrt{6} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & \sqrt{6}/2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Verify:** $Q^\top Q = I_2$ (check the dot products) and $QR = A$ (multiply it out). ✓

### Example 2.7.E6 — Least-Squares Fit to Data Points

Fit a line $y = c_0 + c_1 t$ to the data $(t,y) = \{(0,1),(1,3),(2,2),(3,5)\}$.

**Step 1.** Set up $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ (overdetermined):

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\2\\5\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}c_0\\c_1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 2.** Normal equations $A^\top A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top \mathbf{b}$:

$$
A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix}4&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^\top \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}11\\22\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 3.** Solve: $\det(A^\top A) = 4(14) - 6(6) = 56 - 36 = 20$.

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix}14&-6\\-6&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}11\\22\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix}154-132\\-66+88\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix}22\\22\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1.1\\1.1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The best-fit line is $y = 1.1 + 1.1t$.

### Example 2.7.E7 — Orthogonal Complement in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Find $W^\perp$ where $W = \text{span}\{(1,2,3)^\top\}$.

$W^\perp$ consists of all $(x,y,z)^\top$ with $x + 2y + 3z = 0$. This is a 2-dimensional subspace (a plane through the origin). Two independent vectors in $W^\perp$: take $(x,y,z)^\top = (-2,1,0)^\top$ and $(-3,0,1)^\top$. Check: $-2+2+0=0$ ✓ and $-3+0+3=0$ ✓.


### Example 2.7.E8 — Projection Matrix Verification

For $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^\top$, verify that $P = q_1 q_1^\top$ satisfies $P^2 = P$ and $P^\top = P$.

$$
P = q_1 q_1^\top = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$$
P^2 = \frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = P. \checkmark
$$

$P^\top = P$ is obvious (symmetric matrix). ✓

## ⚠️ 8. Common Pitfalls

> These are the mistakes that appear on every problem set and exam. Read them now so you recognize the pattern when it appears.

**Pitfall 1 — Forgetting to normalize in Gram-Schmidt.**
The algorithm produces the direction $v_j$ first, then you must divide by $\|v_j\|$ to get $q_j$. If you forget, subsequent projection subtractions will not zero out previous components and the whole procedure breaks. *Check $\|q_j\| = 1$ after every step.*

**Pitfall 2 — Confusing $QQ^\top$ and $Q^\top Q$.**
For a non-square $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $m > n$ and orthonormal columns:
- $Q^\top Q = I_n$ (the $n \times n$ identity — this is just the definition of orthonormal columns).
- $QQ^\top \neq I_m$ in general — it is the **projection matrix** $P$ onto $C(Q)$, an $m \times m$ matrix of rank $n < m$.

The confusion costs points: "$Q$ is orthogonal" means $Q$ is square and $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$. For tall skinny $Q$, say "Q has orthonormal columns."

**Pitfall 3 — Misidentifying the projection matrix.**
The projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto the **span of a single vector $\mathbf{a}$** is $P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top}{\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{a}}$ (scalar denominator). When you have multiple orthonormal columns assembled into $Q$, it simplifies to $P = QQ^\top$. Don't mix the two formulas up by dropping the denominator in the single-vector case.

**Pitfall 4 — Normal equations require full column rank.**
The formula $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf{b}$ requires $A^\top A$ to be invertible, which happens iff $A$ has full column rank. If columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, the normal equations still have a solution (the perpendicularity condition still holds) but $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is not unique; you need the pseudoinverse (Ch. 2.8).

**Pitfall 5 — The Cauchy-Schwarz equality case.**
Equality $|\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle| = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$ holds iff $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are proportional, *not* just when they are parallel in the ordinary sense. In the function inner product $\langle f,g\rangle = \int_0^1 f g\,dx$, equality means $f = cg$ as functions — much stronger than "same shape."

### Problem 2.7.P1

Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with the weighted inner product $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle_W = 2u_1v_1 + 3u_2v_2$. Verify all three inner product axioms, compute $\|\mathbf{e}_1\|_W$, and find the angle between $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ under this inner product.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Axioms:**

IP1 (Symmetry): $\langle \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_W = 2u_1v_1+3u_2v_2 = 2v_1u_1+3v_2u_2 = \langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\rangle_W$. ✓

IP2 (Bilinearity): $\langle c\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle_W = 2(cu_1+w_1)v_1+3(cu_2+w_2)v_2 = c\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_W+\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle_W$. ✓

IP3 (Positive definiteness): $\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle_W = 2v_1^2+3v_2^2 \geq 0$ with equality iff $v_1=v_2=0$. ✓

**Norm:** $\|\mathbf{e}_1\|_W = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_1\rangle_W} = \sqrt{2\cdot 1+3\cdot 0} = \sqrt{2}$.

**Angle between $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$:**

$$
\langle \mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2 \rangle_W = 2(1)(0)+3(0)(1) = 0.
$$

They are **orthogonal** under this weighted inner product (just as they are under the standard dot product, since the weight matrix is diagonal). The angle is $90°$.

</details>

### Problem 2.7.P2

Prove that if $P$ is an orthogonal projection matrix ($P^2 = P$, $P^\top = P$), then $I - P$ is also an orthogonal projection matrix, and find what subspace it projects onto.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Claim:** $Q = I - P$ satisfies $Q^2 = Q$ and $Q^\top = Q$.

**Idempotent:**

$$
Q^2 = (I-P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P = Q. \checkmark
$$

**Symmetric:**

$$
Q^\top = (I-P)^\top = I^\top - P^\top = I - P = Q. \checkmark
$$

**Subspace:** $P$ projects onto $C(P)$. The complementary projection $I-P$ projects onto $C(P)^\perp$. Reason: if $P\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$ (so $\mathbf{v} \in C(P)$), then $(I-P)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. If $P\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ (so $\mathbf{v} \in C(P)^\perp$), then $(I-P)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$.

</details>

### Problem 2.7.P3

Apply Gram-Schmidt to $a_1=(1,0,0,1)^\top$, $a_2=(1,1,0,0)^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and verify $q_1 \perp q_2$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** $v_1 = a_1 = (1,0,0,1)^\top$. $\|v_1\|=\sqrt{2}$. $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,0,0,1)^\top$.

**Step 2:**

$$
\langle a_2, q_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[(1)(1)+(1)(0)+(0)(0)+(0)(1)] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.
$$

$$
v_2 = a_2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}q_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}1/2\\0\\0\\1/2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/2\\1\\0\\-1/2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$\|v_2\| = \sqrt{1/4+1+0+1/4}=\sqrt{3/2}$. $q_2 = \sqrt{2/3}(1/2,1,0,-1/2)^\top = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,2,0,-1)^\top$.

**Verify:** $\langle q_1,q_2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}[(1)(1)+(0)(2)+(0)(0)+(1)(-1)] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{12}}(1-1)=0$. ✓

</details>

### Problem 2.7.P4

Show that $A^\top A$ is positive definite when $A$ has full column rank.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

We need to show: for all $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{x}^\top(A^\top A)\mathbf{x} \gt  0$.

**Rewrite:**

$$
\mathbf{x}^\top(A^\top A)\mathbf{x} = (A\mathbf{x})^\top(A\mathbf{x}) = \|A\mathbf{x}\|^2.
$$

Since $A$ has full column rank, the only solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ (by definition of linear independence of columns). Therefore for $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$, $A\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$, so $\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2 \gt  0$. ✓

Also, $A^\top A$ is always symmetric: $(A^\top A)^\top = A^\top (A^\top)^\top = A^\top A$. So $A^\top A$ is **symmetric positive definite** when $A$ has full column rank. $\blacksquare$

</details>

### Problem 2.7.P5

Find the least-squares solution to $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ where $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b}=(1,2,1)^\top$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Normal equations:** $A^\top A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$.

$$
A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$$
A^\top\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Solve:** $\det = 4-1=3$.

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/3\\4/3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Residual:** $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (1,2,1)^\top - (1/3,4/3,5/3)^\top = (2/3,2/3,-2/3)^\top$. Check $A^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
A^\top\mathbf{e} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2/3\\2/3\\-2/3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2/3-2/3\\2/3-2/3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}. \checkmark
$$

</details>


### Problem 2.7.P6

For the function inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^1 f(x)g(x)\,dx$, apply one step of Gram-Schmidt to $\{1, x\}$ to produce $q_1$ and $q_2$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** $v_1 = 1$ (the constant function).

$$
\|v_1\|^2 = \int_0^1 1^2\,dx = 1 \implies q_1 = 1.
$$

**Step 2:** $a_2 = x$. Remove the $q_1$ component:

$$
\langle x, 1 \rangle = \int_0^1 x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}.
$$

$$
v_2 = x - \frac{1}{2}\cdot 1 = x - \frac{1}{2}.
$$

$$
\|v_2\|^2 = \int_0^1 \left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 dx = \int_0^1 \left(x^2 - x + \frac{1}{4}\right)dx = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12}.
$$

$$
q_2 = \frac{v_2}{\|v_2\|} = \frac{x - 1/2}{1/(2\sqrt{3})} = 2\sqrt{3}\left(x - \frac{1}{2}\right).
$$

These are the first two (normalized) Legendre polynomials on $[0,1]$!

</details>

### Problem 2.7.P7

Prove: if $\{q_1, \ldots, q_n\}$ is an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $Q = [q_1|\cdots|q_n]$, then $QQ^\top = I_n$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

Since $Q$ is square $n \times n$ with $Q^\top Q = I_n$ (orthonormal columns), and $I_n$ is invertible, we can multiply both sides of $Q^\top Q = I_n$ on the left by $(Q^\top)^{-1}$ — but we don't know that yet. Instead, use the fact that $Q$ is square:

**$Q^\top Q = I_n$ means $Q^\top = Q^{-1}$ (since $Q$ is square).** Then:

$$
QQ^\top = Q(Q^{-1}) = I_n. \blacksquare
$$

Alternatively, expand $(QQ^\top)_{ij} = \sum_k Q_{ik}Q_{jk} = \sum_k (q_i)_k(q_j)_k = \langle q_i, q_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$, so $QQ^\top = I_n$.

**Key point:** This only works because $Q$ is square. For rectangular $Q$ with $m \gt  n$, $QQ^\top$ is $m \times m$ of rank $n$, not the identity.

</details>

### Problem 2.7.P8

Given $Q$ from the QR decomposition of a matrix $A$ (with $A^\top A$ invertible), show that solving the normal equations via $QR$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^{-1}Q^\top\mathbf{b}$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

Substitute $A = QR$ into the normal equations $A^\top A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$:

$$
(QR)^\top(QR)\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (QR)^\top\mathbf{b}.
$$

$$
R^\top Q^\top Q R\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^\top Q^\top \mathbf{b}.
$$

Since $Q^\top Q = I_n$:

$$
R^\top R\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^\top Q^\top \mathbf{b}.
$$

Multiply on the left by $(R^\top)^{-1}$ (valid since $R$ has positive diagonal, hence $\det R \gt  0$ and $R^\top$ is invertible):

$$
R\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = Q^\top\mathbf{b}.
$$

Since $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal, it is invertible. Therefore:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^{-1}Q^\top\mathbf{b}. \blacksquare
$$

This is how QR is used in practice to solve least-squares: back-substitution on $R\hat{\mathbf{x}} = Q^\top\mathbf{b}$ is numerically stable and avoids forming $A^\top A$ explicitly (which can amplify rounding errors).

</details>
