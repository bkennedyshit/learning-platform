# 1.6 — Vector Fields, Divergence & Curl

> *"The equations of electrodynamics are not equations about numbers — they are equations about fields. Fields are the protagonists. Everything else is just a way of detecting them."* — paraphrased from Richard Feynman, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. II.

Every force in classical physics — gravity, electricity, magnetism, fluid pressure — is described not by a single number but by a **vector field**: an assignment of a directed arrow to every point in space. Mastering the language of vector fields is the gateway to Maxwell's equations, fluid mechanics, general relativity, and quantum field theory. This chapter builds that language from the ground up: what a vector field *is*, how to measure whether it *spins* (curl) or *spreads* (divergence), how to integrate it along a curve (line integral), and when a field is secretly just the gradient of some hidden potential.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Define a vector field and identify its component functions in 2D and 3D.
2. Compute the divergence and curl of a vector field using partial derivatives.
3. Evaluate line integrals $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ along parameterized curves.
4. Test whether a vector field is conservative and find its potential function.
5. State and prove the four major theorems: curl-free ⟺ conservative, div(curl) = 0, curl(grad) = 0, path-independence ⟺ conservative.
6. Compute 2D flux integrals $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds$.
7. Explain why curl = 0 does *not* imply conservative on non-simply-connected domains.
8. Connect divergence and curl to their physical interpretations in electromagnetism and fluid dynamics.

---

## 🔥 Why the Fuck Does This Matter?



Maxwell's four equations — the complete theory of electricity and magnetism — are written entirely in the language of divergence and curl:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}.
$$

These four vector equations encode *all* classical electromagnetic phenomena — light, radio waves, electric motors, every circuit ever built. The first says electric charges are sources of the electric field (div). The third says there are no magnetic monopoles (div = 0). The second and fourth say changing fields create circulating fields (curl). Without the machinery in this chapter, those equations are just four lines of symbols. With it, they are the most precise, most tested laws in all of science.

In fluid dynamics, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ means an incompressible fluid (no sources or sinks), and $\nabla \times \mathbf{v}$ measures local rotation — the vorticity of a tornado. In general relativity, the covariant generalization of divergence and curl underlies the curvature tensor. In quantum field theory, the gauge invariance of the Standard Model is written in terms of field strength tensors that are the relativistic curl.

**Bottom line:** if you want to understand any of modern physics, you need this chapter. Everything else is commentary.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 1.6.1 — Vector Field

A **vector field** on a domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a function

$$
\mathbf{F} : D \to \mathbb{R}^n
$$



that assigns to each point in $D$ a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. In physics, $D$ is usually $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$, and $\mathbf{F}$ is written in component form.

**In 2D:** $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = P(x,y)\,\hat{i} + Q(x,y)\,\hat{j} = \langle P, Q \rangle$, where $P$ and $Q$ are scalar-valued functions called the **component functions**.

**In 3D:** $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = P(x,y,z)\,\hat{i} + Q(x,y,z)\,\hat{j} + R(x,y,z)\,\hat{k} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$.

**Physical examples:**
- **Gravitational field:** $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r}) = -GM\,\mathbf{r}/|\mathbf{r}|^3$ — points toward the mass, magnitude decreasing with distance squared.
- **Electric field:** $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\mathbf{r}/|\mathbf{r}|^3$ — Coulomb's law as a vector field.
- **Fluid velocity field:** $\mathbf{v}(x,y,z,t)$ — the velocity of fluid at each point in space and time.

### Definition 1.6.2 — Divergence

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$ be a differentiable vector field on a domain in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The **divergence** of $\mathbf{F}$ is the scalar field

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}.
$$

In 2D with $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q \rangle$:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}.
$$

**Physical interpretation.** The divergence measures the **net outward flux per unit volume** at a point — whether the field is *spreading out* (positive divergence, a source) or *converging in* (negative divergence, a sink). The precise statement is Green's/Divergence Theorem (Chapter 1.7).

### Definition 1.6.3 — Curl

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$ be a differentiable vector field on a domain in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The **curl** of $\mathbf{F}$ is the vector field

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ P & Q & R \end{vmatrix} = \left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}\right)\hat{i} + \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}\right)\hat{j} + \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\hat{k}.
$$

**Expanding the determinant explicitly** (row-by-row cofactor expansion along the first row):

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (R_y - Q_z)\,\hat{i} - (R_x - P_z)\,\hat{j} + (Q_x - P_y)\,\hat{k}.
$$

Equivalently written component by component:

$$
(\nabla \times \mathbf{F})_x = R_y - Q_z, \qquad (\nabla \times \mathbf{F})_y = P_z - R_x, \qquad (\nabla \times \mathbf{F})_z = Q_x - P_y.
$$

**In 2D:** For $\mathbf{F} = \langle P(x,y), Q(x,y) \rangle$ (with $R = 0$ and no $z$-dependence), the curl reduces to the scalar

$$
\text{curl}_z\,\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}.
$$

**Physical interpretation.** The curl measures the **local rotation density** of the field — how much and in which direction the field is "swirling" around a point. Think of a tiny paddle-wheel placed in a fluid current: if the wheel spins, the fluid has nonzero curl at that point. The axis of the curl vector $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ is the axis of rotation (right-hand rule), and its magnitude is proportional to the angular speed of rotation.

### Definition 1.6.4 — Line Integral of a Vector Field

Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a continuous vector field defined on a domain containing a smooth oriented curve $C$. Let $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $a \leq t \leq b$, be a smooth parameterization of $C$ (with the orientation of $C$ corresponding to increasing $t$). The **line integral** of $\mathbf{F}$ along $C$ is

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt.
$$

**Expansion.** With $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$ and $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$:

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \bigl[P\,x'(t) + Q\,y'(t) + R\,z'(t)\bigr]\,dt = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz.
$$

**Physical meaning.** If $\mathbf{F}$ is a force field, $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is the **work** done by the force on a particle moving along $C$. The dot product $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)$ at each point extracts the component of force *along* the direction of travel.



### Definition 1.6.5 — Conservative Vector Field and Potential Function

A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ defined on a domain $D$ is **conservative** (or **irrotational** in physics) if there exists a scalar function $f : D \to \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
\mathbf{F} = \nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right\rangle.
$$

The function $f$ is called the **scalar potential function** (or just potential) for $\mathbf{F}$.

**Key consequence.** If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, then the line integral along *any* smooth curve $C$ from point $A$ to point $B$ depends only on the endpoints:

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(B) - f(A).
$$

This is the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals (proved in §5 below). It is the line-integral analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

### Definition 1.6.6 — Path Independence

A line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is **path-independent** on $D$ if, for any two points $A, B \in D$, the value of the integral is the same for *every* piecewise-smooth oriented curve $C$ in $D$ from $A$ to $B$.

Equivalently, $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is path-independent on $D$ if and only if $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$ for every closed curve $C$ in $D$.

### Definition 1.6.7 — Simply-Connected Domain

A domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **simply connected** if it is connected and every closed loop in $D$ can be continuously contracted (shrunk) to a single point while remaining inside $D$. Informally: $D$ has no holes.

**Examples:**
- $\mathbb{R}^2$ is simply connected. The open disk $\{(x,y): x^2+y^2 < 1\}$ is simply connected.
- $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$ (the plane with the origin removed) is **not** simply connected — a circle around the origin cannot be shrunk to a point.
- $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}$ is not simply connected.

The simple-connectedness hypothesis is critical in the theorem that curl = 0 implies conservative, as the counterexample in §8 (Common Pitfalls) will show explicitly.

### Definition 1.6.8 — 2D Flux Integral

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q \rangle$ be a vector field and $C$ a smooth oriented closed curve in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (positively oriented = counterclockwise). The **outward flux** of $\mathbf{F}$ through $C$ is

$$
\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds = \oint_C P\,dy - Q\,dx,
$$

where $\hat{n}$ is the outward unit normal to $C$ and $ds$ is the arc-length element. For a curve parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t) \rangle$, the outward normal is $\hat{n}\,ds = \langle y'(t), -x'(t) \rangle\,dt$ (rotated 90° clockwise from the tangent direction), so

$$
\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds = \int_a^b \bigl[P(x(t),y(t))\,y'(t) - Q(x(t),y(t))\,x'(t)\bigr]\,dt.
$$

**Physical meaning.** The flux integral measures the net rate at which the field passes *through* the curve per unit time — if $\mathbf{F}$ is a fluid velocity, this is the net volumetric flow rate outward across $C$.

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

The following standard analysis properties are assumed (inherited from Chapter 1.1 and 1.2).

### Axiom 1.6.A — Clairaut's Theorem (Mixed Partials)

If $f : D \to \mathbb{R}$ has continuous second partial derivatives on the open set $D$, then the mixed partial derivatives are equal:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x}, \quad \text{and similarly for all pairs } (x,z),\,(y,z).
$$

This symmetry of second derivatives is the engine behind both "curl of gradient = 0" and the integrability conditions for finding a potential function.

### Axiom 1.6.B — Equality of Continuous Functions on Connected Sets

If two continuous scalar functions on a connected domain $D$ agree on an open set inside $D$, they are identical on all of $D$. This is used in potential function reconstruction to pin down constants of integration.

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 1.6.1 — Linearity of the Line Integral

For continuous vector fields $\mathbf{F}$, $\mathbf{G}$ on $D$ and constants $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
\int_C (\alpha\mathbf{F} + \beta\mathbf{G}) \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \alpha \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} + \beta \int_C \mathbf{G} \cdot d\mathbf{r}.
$$

**Proof.** By Definition 1.6.4, $\int_C (\alpha\mathbf{F} + \beta\mathbf{G}) \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b (\alpha\mathbf{F} + \beta\mathbf{G}) \cdot \mathbf{r}'\,dt$. Since the dot product distributes over addition and scalar multiplication:

$$
= \int_a^b [\alpha (\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}') + \beta(\mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{r}')]\,dt = \alpha \int_a^b \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}'\,dt + \beta \int_a^b \mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{r}'\,dt = \alpha \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} + \beta \int_C \mathbf{G} \cdot d\mathbf{r}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Lemma 1.6.2 — Reversal of Orientation

If $-C$ denotes the curve $C$ traversed in the opposite direction, then

$$
\int_{-C} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}.
$$

**Proof.** Let $C$ be parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $a \leq t \leq b$. Then $-C$ is parameterized by $\mathbf{s}(t) = \mathbf{r}(a + b - t)$, $a \leq t \leq b$. We have $\mathbf{s}'(t) = -\mathbf{r}'(a+b-t)$. Substituting $u = a+b-t$ (so $du = -dt$):

$$
\int_{-C} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{s}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{s}'(t)\,dt = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(a+b-t)) \cdot (-\mathbf{r}'(a+b-t))\,dt = -\int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(u)\,du = -\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

## 👑 4. Major Theorems

### Theorem 1.6.1 — Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals

Let $f$ be a differentiable scalar function on an open domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, and let $C$ be a smooth curve in $D$ parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $a \leq t \leq b$. Then

$$
\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)).
$$

That is, the line integral of the gradient depends only on the values of $f$ at the endpoints.

### Theorem 1.6.2 — Curl of Gradient is Zero

Let $f$ be a $C^2$ scalar function on a domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$. Then

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}.
$$

### Theorem 1.6.3 — Divergence of Curl is Zero

Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a $C^2$ vector field on a domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$. Then

$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0.
$$

### Theorem 1.6.4 — Conservative Field Criterion (on Simply-Connected Domains)

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$ be a $C^1$ vector field on a simply-connected domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$. Then $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative (i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ for some $f$) if and only if

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \quad \text{on } D.
$$

In 2D: $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q \rangle$ is conservative on a simply-connected domain iff $\partial P/\partial y = \partial Q/\partial x$.

### Theorem 1.6.5 — Path Independence Equivalences

Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a continuous vector field on an open connected domain $D$. The following are equivalent:
1. $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative on $D$ (i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ for some potential $f$).
2. $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is path-independent on $D$.
3. $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$ for every closed curve $C$ in $D$.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Proof of Theorem 1.6.1 — Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals

**Claim.** If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ and $C$ is parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $a \leq t \leq b$, then $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a))$.

**Proof.** Write out the integrand explicitly. With $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z \rangle$ and $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle x'(t), y'(t), z'(t) \rangle$:

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \nabla f(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \int_a^b \left[f_x\,x'(t) + f_y\,y'(t) + f_z\,z'(t)\right]dt.
$$

The integrand is precisely the **chain rule** expansion of $\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[f(\mathbf{r}(t))\bigr]$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[f(x(t), y(t), z(t))\bigr] = f_x\,x'(t) + f_y\,y'(t) + f_z\,z'(t).
$$

Therefore:

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[f(\mathbf{r}(t))\bigr]\,dt = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)),
$$

where the last step is just the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Chapter 1.3) applied to the scalar function $g(t) = f(\mathbf{r}(t))$. The result depends only on the endpoints $\mathbf{r}(a)$ and $\mathbf{r}(b)$ — the specific path parameterization $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is irrelevant. $\blacksquare$

### 5.2 Proof of Theorem 1.6.2 — Curl of Gradient is Zero

**Claim.** For any $C^2$ scalar $f$, $\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$.

**Proof.** Compute $\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z \rangle$ and apply the curl formula (Definition 1.6.3) with $P = f_x$, $Q = f_y$, $R = f_z$:

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ f_x & f_y & f_z \end{vmatrix}.
$$

Expanding:

$$
= (f_{zy} - f_{yz})\,\hat{i} + (f_{xz} - f_{zx})\,\hat{j} + (f_{yx} - f_{xy})\,\hat{k}.
$$

By Clairaut's Theorem (Axiom 1.6.A), since $f$ is $C^2$, all mixed partials are equal:

$$
f_{zy} = f_{yz}, \qquad f_{xz} = f_{zx}, \qquad f_{yx} = f_{xy}.
$$

Therefore each component is zero:

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla f) = (0)\,\hat{i} + (0)\,\hat{j} + (0)\,\hat{k} = \mathbf{0}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

**Physical interpretation.** A static electric field $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ (where $V$ is the electric potential) automatically satisfies $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0$ — no work is done by $\mathbf{E}$ around a closed path. This is Faraday's law in the static case.



### 5.3 Proof of Theorem 1.6.3 — Divergence of Curl is Zero

**Claim.** For any $C^2$ vector field $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$, $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0$.

**Proof.** First write out $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ in full:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \langle R_y - Q_z,\; P_z - R_x,\; Q_x - P_y \rangle.
$$

Now take the divergence (sum of partial derivatives of each component with respect to the corresponding variable):

$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(R_y - Q_z) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(P_z - R_x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(Q_x - P_y).
$$

Expand each term:

$$
= R_{xy} - Q_{xz} + P_{yz} - R_{yx} + Q_{zx} - P_{zy}.
$$

Now group by pair:

$$
= (R_{xy} - R_{yx}) + (P_{yz} - P_{zy}) + (Q_{zx} - Q_{xz}).
$$

Since $\mathbf{F}$ is $C^2$, Clairaut's Theorem (Axiom 1.6.A) guarantees $R_{xy} = R_{yx}$, $P_{yz} = P_{zy}$, and $Q_{zx} = Q_{xz}$. Therefore every term vanishes:

$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0. \quad \blacksquare
$$

**Physical interpretation.** In electromagnetism, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ (no magnetic monopoles). Since $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) = 0$ automatically, we can always write $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}$ for some **vector potential** $\mathbf{A}$ (in a simply-connected domain). This is the construction underlying electromagnetic gauge theory.

### 5.4 Proof of Theorem 1.6.4 — Conservative Field Criterion (⟹ Direction)

**Claim.** If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ (conservative), then $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$.

**Proof.** This is exactly Theorem 1.6.2: $\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$. See §5.2. $\blacksquare$

### 5.5 Proof of Theorem 1.6.4 — Conservative Field Criterion (⟸ Direction on Simply-Connected Domains)

**Claim.** If $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ on a simply-connected domain $D$, then $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative on $D$.

**Proof (potential construction by line integral).** Fix a base point $\mathbf{r}_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0) \in D$. For any point $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z) \in D$, define

$$
f(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{C_{\mathbf{r}_0 \to \mathbf{r}}} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r},
$$

where $C_{\mathbf{r}_0 \to \mathbf{r}}$ is any piecewise-smooth path in $D$ from $\mathbf{r}_0$ to $\mathbf{r}$.

**Step 1: $f$ is well-defined (path-independent).** Since $D$ is simply connected and $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$, Stokes' Theorem (Chapter 1.7) implies that $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$ for every closed curve $C$ in $D$. (This is the key place where simple connectedness is used — Stokes' theorem requires a surface spanning the loop to lie entirely in $D$.) Therefore $f(\mathbf{r})$ does not depend on which path we choose from $\mathbf{r}_0$ to $\mathbf{r}$.

**Step 2: $\nabla f = \mathbf{F}$.** We show this component by component. Consider the $x$-component. Fix $(y, z)$ and vary $x$. Choose the path to $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)$ by going first from $\mathbf{r}_0$ to $(x_1, y, z)$ (any path), then from $(x_1, y, z)$ along the straight segment $\mathbf{r}(t) = (t, y, z)$, $t$ from $x_1$ to $x$. Then

$$
f(x, y, z) = \text{const} + \int_{x_1}^{x} P(t, y, z)\,dt,
$$

since on this last segment $dy = dz = 0$ so $\mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = P\,dx = P(t,y,z)\,dt$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (applied to the parameter $x$):

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = P(x, y, z).
$$

An identical argument (varying $y$ with $x, z$ fixed, or $z$ with $x, y$ fixed) gives $\partial f/\partial y = Q$ and $\partial f/\partial z = R$. Therefore $\nabla f = \langle P, Q, R \rangle = \mathbf{F}$. $\blacksquare$

### 5.6 Proof of Theorem 1.6.5 — Path Independence Equivalences

We prove the three-way equivalence $(1) \Leftrightarrow (2) \Leftrightarrow (3)$.

**$(1) \Rightarrow (2)$:** If $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, then by Theorem 1.6.1, $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a))$ depends only on the endpoints, not the path. So the integral is path-independent.

**$(2) \Rightarrow (3)$:** Let $C$ be any closed curve from $A$ to $A$. By path independence, $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ from $A$ to $A$ equals $f(A) - f(A) = 0$. (Or more directly: split $C$ into two paths $C_1$ from $A$ to $B$ and $C_2$ from $B$ to $A$. Then $\oint_C = \int_{C_1} + \int_{-C_2^{\text{rev}}}$... a cleaner version: if the integral is path-independent, then for any closed curve, take $C_1$: any path from $A$ to $B$, and $C_2$: any other path from $A$ to $B$. Then $\oint_{C_1 \cup (-C_2)} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{C_1} - \int_{C_2} = 0$ by path independence. Since every closed curve is of this form, (3) holds.)

**$(3) \Rightarrow (1)$:** Suppose $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$ for every closed $C$ in $D$. Define $f(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{\mathbf{r}_0}^{\mathbf{r}} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ (path-independent by hypothesis). The same computation as in §5.5 Step 2 shows $\nabla f = \mathbf{F}$. $\blacksquare$

## 🎯 6. Worked Examples

### Example 1.6.E1 — Sketch/Describe the Rotation Field $\mathbf{F} = -y\hat{i} + x\hat{j}$

**Given.** $\mathbf{F}(x, y) = \langle -y, x \rangle$.

**Step 1: Identify a few sample vectors.**

| Point $(x, y)$ | $\mathbf{F}(x,y)$ | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| $(1, 0)$ | $\langle 0, 1 \rangle$ | straight up |
| $(0, 1)$ | $\langle -1, 0 \rangle$ | straight left |
| $(-1, 0)$ | $\langle 0, -1 \rangle$ | straight down |
| $(0, -1)$ | $\langle 1, 0 \rangle$ | straight right |
| $(1, 1)$ | $\langle -1, 1 \rangle$ | upper-left |

**Step 2: Identify the pattern.** The vector at $(x, y)$ is $(-y, x)$, which is the vector $(x,y)$ rotated $90°$ counterclockwise. The magnitude is $|\mathbf{F}| = \sqrt{(-y)^2 + x^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, which equals the distance from the origin. So every arrow is tangent to the circle through its base point, pointing counterclockwise, with magnitude equal to the circle's radius.

**Step 3: Compute divergence and curl.**

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(x)}{\partial y} = 0 + 0 = 0.
$$

$$
\text{curl}_z\,\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial(x)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial y} = 1 - (-1) = 2.
$$

**Interpretation.** The field has zero divergence (no sources or sinks — mass/fluid is conserved) but constant nonzero curl $= 2$ everywhere. A paddle-wheel placed anywhere in this field would spin counterclockwise with angular velocity $\omega = \text{curl}/2 = 1$ rad/s. This field represents **rigid-body rotation** at uniform angular velocity $\omega = 1$.

### Example 1.6.E2 — Divergence and Curl of a 3D Field

**Given.** $\mathbf{F} = xyz\,\hat{i} + e^{xy}\,\hat{j} + \sin z\,\hat{k}$, so $P = xyz$, $Q = e^{xy}$, $R = \sin z$.

**Divergence:**

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial(xyz)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(e^{xy})}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(\sin z)}{\partial z}.
$$

Compute each partial:

- $\partial(xyz)/\partial x = yz$ (treat $y, z$ as constants).
- $\partial(e^{xy})/\partial y = xe^{xy}$ (chain rule: derivative of $xy$ with respect to $y$ is $x$).
- $\partial(\sin z)/\partial z = \cos z$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = yz + xe^{xy} + \cos z.}
$$

**Curl:** Apply the determinant formula:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (R_y - Q_z)\,\hat{i} + (P_z - R_x)\,\hat{j} + (Q_x - P_y)\,\hat{k}.
$$

Compute each required partial:
- $R_y = \partial(\sin z)/\partial y = 0$, $\quad Q_z = \partial(e^{xy})/\partial z = 0$.
- $P_z = \partial(xyz)/\partial z = xy$, $\quad R_x = \partial(\sin z)/\partial x = 0$.
- $Q_x = \partial(e^{xy})/\partial x = ye^{xy}$, $\quad P_y = \partial(xyz)/\partial y = xz$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (0 - 0)\,\hat{i} + (xy - 0)\,\hat{j} + (ye^{xy} - xz)\,\hat{k} = xy\,\hat{j} + (ye^{xy} - xz)\,\hat{k}.}
$$

### Example 1.6.E3 — Line Integral Along a Parabola

**Given.** $\mathbf{F} = \langle y, x \rangle$, curve $C$: $y = x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

**Step 1: Parameterize the curve.** Let $x = t$, $y = t^2$, so $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2 \rangle$, $t \in [0, 1]$. Then $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 1, 2t \rangle$.

**Step 2: Evaluate $\mathbf{F}$ on the curve.** Substitute $x = t$, $y = t^2$:

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle y, x \rangle\big|_{(t, t^2)} = \langle t^2, t \rangle.
$$

**Step 3: Form the dot product.**

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle t^2, t \rangle \cdot \langle 1, 2t \rangle = t^2 \cdot 1 + t \cdot 2t = t^2 + 2t^2 = 3t^2.
$$

**Step 4: Integrate.**

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^1 3t^2\,dt = \Bigl[t^3\Bigr]_0^1 = 1 - 0 = \boxed{1}.
$$

*Note: We will see in the next example that this field is conservative with potential $f(x,y) = xy$, so the answer should be $f(1,1) - f(0,0) = 1 - 0 = 1$. Confirmed.*



### Example 1.6.E4 — Show $\mathbf{F} = \langle y, x \rangle$ is Conservative; Find Its Potential

**Step 1: Apply the curl test.** With $P = y$ and $Q = x$:

$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial(x)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(y)}{\partial y} = 1 - 1 = 0.
$$

Since the domain $\mathbb{R}^2$ is simply connected and curl = 0, Theorem 1.6.4 guarantees $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.

**Step 2: Find the potential $f$ such that $\nabla f = \mathbf{F}$.** We need:

$$
f_x = P = y \qquad \text{and} \qquad f_y = Q = x.
$$

**From $f_x = y$:** Integrate with respect to $x$ (treating $y$ as a constant):

$$
f(x, y) = \int y\,dx = xy + g(y),
$$

where $g(y)$ is an arbitrary function of $y$ only (the "constant of integration" with respect to $x$).

**From $f_y = x$:** Differentiate the expression for $f$ with respect to $y$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[xy + g(y)] = x + g'(y).
$$

Set this equal to $Q = x$:

$$
x + g'(y) = x \implies g'(y) = 0 \implies g(y) = C \text{ (a constant)}.
$$

**Conclusion.** The potential function is $f(x, y) = xy + C$ for any constant $C$. We can verify: $\nabla(xy) = \langle y, x \rangle = \mathbf{F}$. $\checkmark$

### Example 1.6.E5 — Show $\mathbf{F} = \langle y^2, x \rangle$ is NOT Conservative

**Step 1: Apply the curl test.** With $P = y^2$ and $Q = x$:

$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial(x)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(y^2)}{\partial y} = 1 - 2y.
$$

This is **not identically zero** (e.g., it equals $1 - 2(1) = -1$ at $y=1$). Therefore the curl is nonzero and $\mathbf{F}$ is **not conservative**.

**Implication.** There is no potential function $f$ such that $\nabla f = \langle y^2, x \rangle$. If we naively tried, we would get $f_x = y^2 \Rightarrow f = xy^2 + g(y)$, then $f_y = 2xy + g'(y) = x$ would require $2xy + g'(y) = x$ for all $x, y$ — impossible since the left side has an $xy$ term and the right side doesn't.

### Example 1.6.E6 — Path-Dependence of a Non-Conservative Field

**Given.** $\mathbf{F} = \langle y^2, x \rangle$ (same non-conservative field from E5). Compute $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ along two different paths and observe that the results differ.

**Path 1:** Straight line $y = x$, parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t \rangle$, $t \in [0,1]$, $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 1, 1 \rangle$.

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle t^2, t \rangle, \qquad \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = t^2 \cdot 1 + t \cdot 1 = t^2 + t.
$$

$$
\int_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^1 (t^2 + t)\,dt = \left[\frac{t^3}{3} + \frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{5}{6}.
$$

**Path 2:** Parabola $y = x^2$, parameterized by $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2 \rangle$, $t \in [0,1]$, $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 1, 2t \rangle$.

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle (t^2)^2, t \rangle = \langle t^4, t \rangle, \qquad \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = t^4 \cdot 1 + t \cdot 2t = t^4 + 2t^2.
$$

$$
\int_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^1 (t^4 + 2t^2)\,dt = \left[\frac{t^5}{5} + \frac{2t^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{5} + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{3}{15} + \frac{10}{15} = \frac{13}{15}.
$$

**Comparison.**

$$
\int_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \frac{5}{6} \neq \frac{13}{15} = \int_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}.
$$

The line integral is path-dependent, as expected for a non-conservative field. The difference $\frac{13}{15} - \frac{5}{6} = \frac{26}{30} - \frac{25}{30} = \frac{1}{30}$ would equal the circulation around the closed loop $C_2 \cup (-C_1)$.

### Example 1.6.E7 — Flux of $\mathbf{F} = \langle x, y \rangle$ Outward Through the Unit Circle

**Given.** $\mathbf{F} = \langle x, y \rangle$, $C$: unit circle parameterized counterclockwise by $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$.

**Step 1: Write the flux formula.** The outward flux is $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds = \oint_C P\,dy - Q\,dx$.

**Step 2: Compute $dx$ and $dy$.**

$$
x(t) = \cos t \Rightarrow dx = -\sin t\,dt, \qquad y(t) = \sin t \Rightarrow dy = \cos t\,dt.
$$

**Step 3: Evaluate $P$ and $Q$ on the curve.**

$$
P = x = \cos t, \qquad Q = y = \sin t.
$$

**Step 4: Form the integral.**

$$
\oint_C P\,dy - Q\,dx = \int_0^{2\pi} [\cos t \cdot \cos t\,dt - \sin t \cdot (-\sin t\,dt)] = \int_0^{2\pi}[\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t]\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dt = 2\pi.
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Outward flux} = 2\pi.}
$$

**Sanity check via Green's Theorem** (Chapter 1.7): $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds = \iint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\,dA$. Since $\nabla \cdot \langle x, y \rangle = 1 + 1 = 2$ and $D$ is the unit disk with area $\pi(1)^2 = \pi$, the flux is $2 \cdot \pi = 2\pi$. $\checkmark$



### Example 1.6.E8 — Find the Potential for a 3D Conservative Field

**Given.** $\mathbf{F} = \langle 2xy + z^2,\; x^2,\; 2xz \rangle$. So $P = 2xy + z^2$, $Q = x^2$, $R = 2xz$.

**Step 1: Verify the field is conservative (check all curl components = 0).**

- $R_y - Q_z = \partial(2xz)/\partial y - \partial(x^2)/\partial z = 0 - 0 = 0$. ✓
- $P_z - R_x = \partial(2xy+z^2)/\partial z - \partial(2xz)/\partial x = 2z - 2z = 0$. ✓
- $Q_x - P_y = \partial(x^2)/\partial x - \partial(2xy+z^2)/\partial y = 2x - 2x = 0$. ✓

All curl components vanish. Since $\mathbb{R}^3$ is simply connected, $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.

**Step 2: Find $f$ from $f_x = P = 2xy + z^2$.** Integrate with respect to $x$:

$$
f(x, y, z) = \int (2xy + z^2)\,dx = x^2 y + xz^2 + g(y, z),
$$

where $g(y, z)$ is an unknown function of $y$ and $z$.

**Step 3: Use $f_y = Q = x^2$ to find $g$.** Differentiate the expression for $f$ with respect to $y$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}[x^2 y + xz^2 + g(y,z)] = x^2 + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}.
$$

Set equal to $Q = x^2$:

$$
x^2 + g_y(y, z) = x^2 \implies g_y(y, z) = 0 \implies g(y, z) = h(z),
$$

a function of $z$ only.

**Step 4: Use $f_z = R = 2xz$ to find $h$.** Differentiate with respect to $z$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}[x^2 y + xz^2 + h(z)] = 2xz + h'(z).
$$

Set equal to $R = 2xz$:

$$
2xz + h'(z) = 2xz \implies h'(z) = 0 \implies h(z) = C.
$$

**Conclusion.** The potential function is:

$$
\boxed{f(x, y, z) = x^2 y + xz^2 + C.}
$$

**Verification.** $\nabla(x^2 y + xz^2) = \langle 2xy + z^2,\; x^2,\; 2xz \rangle = \mathbf{F}$. $\checkmark$

### Problem 1.6.P1 — Div, Curl, and Potential of a 3D Polynomial Field

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle yz, xz, xy \rangle$.

**(a)** Compute $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$.
**(b)** Compute $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$.
**(c)** Show that $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative and find its potential function.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**(a) Divergence:**

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial(yz)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(xz)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(xy)}{\partial z} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0.
$$

**(b) Curl:** With $P=yz$, $Q=xz$, $R=xy$:

- $R_y - Q_z = \partial(xy)/\partial y - \partial(xz)/\partial z = x - x = 0$.
- $P_z - R_x = \partial(yz)/\partial z - \partial(xy)/\partial x = y - y = 0$.
- $Q_x - P_y = \partial(xz)/\partial x - \partial(yz)/\partial y = z - z = 0$.

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}.
$$

**(c) Potential:** Since $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ (simply connected), $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.

From $f_x = yz$: integrate with respect to $x$:

$$
f = xyz + g(y, z).
$$

From $f_y = xz$: $\partial_y(xyz + g) = xz + g_y = xz$, so $g_y = 0$, meaning $g = h(z)$.

From $f_z = xy$: $\partial_z(xyz + h(z)) = xy + h'(z) = xy$, so $h'(z) = 0$, thus $h(z) = C$.

$$
\boxed{f(x,y,z) = xyz + C.}
$$

</details>

### Problem 1.6.P2 — Line Integral, Two Parameterizations, One Result

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle 2x, 2y \rangle$ and let $C$ go from $(0,0)$ to $(2,1)$.

**(a)** Verify that $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative and find its potential.
**(b)** Use the potential to evaluate $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ instantly.
**(c)** Verify by direct computation along the straight-line path.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**(a)** $\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y = 0 - 0 = 0$. Conservative. Potential: $f_x = 2x \Rightarrow f = x^2 + g(y)$; $f_y = g'(y) = 2y \Rightarrow g = y^2 + C$. So $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 + C$.

**(b)** By the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals:

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(2,1) - f(0,0) = (4+1) - (0+0) = 5.
$$

**(c)** Straight line: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2t, t \rangle$, $t \in [0,1]$, $\mathbf{r}' = \langle 2, 1 \rangle$. $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle 4t, 2t \rangle$.

$$
\int_0^1 \langle 4t, 2t \rangle \cdot \langle 2,1 \rangle\,dt = \int_0^1 (8t + 2t)\,dt = \int_0^1 10t\,dt = 5. \quad \checkmark
$$

</details>

### Problem 1.6.P3 — The Classic Vortex Field (Non-Simply-Connected Domain)

Let $\mathbf{F} = \left\langle \dfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\, \dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right\rangle$ on $D = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$.

**(a)** Compute the 2D curl of $\mathbf{F}$.
**(b)** Compute $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ where $C$ is the unit circle traversed counterclockwise.
**(c)** Explain why the results of (a) and (b) do not contradict Theorem 1.6.4.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**(a)** With $P = -y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $Q = x/(x^2+y^2)$, let $r^2 = x^2+y^2$:

$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{r^2 - x \cdot 2x}{r^4} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{r^4}, \qquad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{-r^2 + y \cdot 2y}{r^4} = \frac{y^2 - r^2}{r^4} = \frac{-(x^2-y^2)}{r^4}.
$$

Wait — let's be careful. $\partial Q/\partial x$: $Q = x/r^2$. By quotient rule:

$$
Q_x = \frac{r^2 \cdot 1 - x \cdot 2x}{r^4} = \frac{r^2 - 2x^2}{r^4}.
$$

$P_y$: $P = -y/r^2$. By quotient rule:

$$
P_y = \frac{-r^2 - (-y)(2y)}{r^4} = \frac{-r^2 + 2y^2}{r^4}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
Q_x - P_y = \frac{r^2 - 2x^2}{r^4} - \frac{-r^2 + 2y^2}{r^4} = \frac{r^2 - 2x^2 + r^2 - 2y^2}{r^4} = \frac{2r^2 - 2(x^2+y^2)}{r^4} = \frac{2r^2 - 2r^2}{r^4} = 0.
$$

So $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 0$ everywhere on $D$.

**(b)** Parameterize $C$: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$. On the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$, so:

$$
\mathbf{F} = \langle -\sin t, \cos t \rangle, \qquad \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t \rangle.
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = (-\sin t)(-\sin t) + (\cos t)(\cos t) = \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1.
$$

$$
\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dt = 2\pi \neq 0.
$$

**(c)** Theorem 1.6.4 requires the domain to be **simply connected**. But $D = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$ has a hole at the origin — it is **not simply connected**. The loop $C$ around the origin cannot be contracted to a point while staying in $D$. Stokes' theorem cannot be applied (the obvious spanning disk passes through the excluded origin). The curl vanishes everywhere the field is defined, but the field is **not globally conservative**: there is no single-valued potential function on all of $D$. (Locally, near any simply-connected subdomain not surrounding the origin, you can write $\mathbf{F} = \nabla(\arctan(y/x))$, but $\arctan(y/x)$ is not single-valued around the origin.)

This is the fundamental counterexample to the naive statement "curl = 0 ⟹ conservative."

</details>

### Problem 1.6.P5 — Identify the Potential from Physical Units

The gravitational field near Earth's surface is $\mathbf{g} = \langle 0, -g \rangle$ (constant downward), where $g \approx 9.8$ m/s². Find the gravitational potential energy per unit mass $\phi$ such that $\mathbf{g} = -\nabla \phi$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

We need $-\nabla \phi = \langle 0, -g \rangle$, i.e., $\nabla \phi = \langle 0, g \rangle$.

So $\phi_x = 0$ and $\phi_y = g$.

From $\phi_x = 0$: $\phi = h(y)$ (no $x$ dependence).

From $\phi_y = g$: $h'(y) = g$, so $h(y) = gy + C$.

Therefore $\phi(x,y) = gy + C$. Taking $C = 0$ and restoring the physical meaning: $\phi = gh$ (height times $g$) — the familiar gravitational potential energy per unit mass. The minus sign in $\mathbf{g} = -\nabla \phi$ reflects the convention that potential energy increases upward while gravity points downward.

</details>



### Problem 1.6.P6 — Compute Div and Curl of a Mixed Exponential Field

Let $\mathbf{F} = \langle e^x \sin y,\; e^x \cos y,\; z^2 \rangle$.

**(a)** Compute $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$.
**(b)** Compute $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$.
**(c)** Is $\mathbf{F}$ conservative in $\mathbb{R}^3$?

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**(a)** With $P = e^x \sin y$, $Q = e^x \cos y$, $R = z^2$:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = e^x \sin y + e^x(-\sin y) + 2z = e^x \sin y - e^x \sin y + 2z = 2z.
$$

**(b)** Curl components:

- $R_y - Q_z = 0 - 0 = 0$.
- $P_z - R_x = 0 - 0 = 0$.
- $Q_x - P_y = e^x \cos y - e^x \cos y = 0$.

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}.
$$

**(c)** Since $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ (simply connected), $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.

Finding $f$: $f_x = e^x \sin y \Rightarrow f = e^x \sin y + g(y, z)$. Then $f_y = e^x \cos y + g_y = e^x \cos y \Rightarrow g_y = 0 \Rightarrow g = h(z)$. Then $f_z = h'(z) = z^2 \Rightarrow h(z) = z^3/3 + C$.

$$
f(x,y,z) = e^x \sin y + \frac{z^3}{3} + C.
$$

</details>

### Problem 1.6.P7 — Line Integral Over a Helix

Compute $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ where $\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x, z \rangle$ and $C$ is the helix $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Compute $\mathbf{r}'(t)$.** $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1 \rangle$.

**Step 2: Evaluate $\mathbf{F}$ on the helix.** $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$, $z = t$:

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, t \rangle.
$$

**Step 3: Form the dot product.**

$$
\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = (-\sin t)(-\sin t) + (\cos t)(\cos t) + t \cdot 1 = \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t + t = 1 + t.
$$

**Step 4: Integrate.**

$$
\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} (1 + t)\,dt = \left[t + \frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^{2\pi} = 2\pi + \frac{4\pi^2}{2} = 2\pi + 2\pi^2.
$$

$$
\boxed{\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 2\pi + 2\pi^2 = 2\pi(1 + \pi).}
$$

</details>

### Problem 1.6.P8 — Flux Integral Through a Rectangle

Compute the outward flux of $\mathbf{F} = \langle x^2, y \rangle$ through the rectangle $[0,2] \times [0,3]$ (traversed counterclockwise) using the direct flux integral formula. Verify using Green's Theorem (divergence form).

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Direct computation:** The rectangle $C$ has four sides. Outward flux = $\oint_C P\,dy - Q\,dx$.

- **Bottom** ($y=0$, $x: 0\to 2$): $dy=0$, $P=x^2$, $Q=0$. Contribution: $\int_0^2 x^2(0) - 0\,(-dx) = 0$.

Wait — on the bottom edge traversed left to right, $d\mathbf{r} = \langle dx, 0\rangle$, and the outward normal points downward ($-\hat{j}$), so the outward flux on this edge = $\int P(-dy) - Q(-dx)$... actually let's use the clean formula directly.

Outward flux formula: $\oint_C P\,dy - Q\,dx$:

- **Bottom** ($y=0$, $x: 0\to 2$): $dx = dx$, $dy = 0$. Contribution: $\int_0^2 [x^2 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot dx] = 0$.
- **Right** ($x=2$, $y: 0\to 3$): $dx = 0$, $dy = dy$. $P = 4$. Contribution: $\int_0^3 4\,dy = 12$.
- **Top** ($y=3$, $x: 2\to 0$): $dy = 0$, $Q = 3$, $dx \lt  0$. Contribution: $\int_2^0 [0 - 3\,dx] = -3\int_2^0 dx = -3(0-2) = 6$.
- **Left** ($x=0$, $y: 3\to 0$): $dx = 0$, $P = 0$. Contribution: $\int_3^0 0\,dy = 0$.

Total outward flux = $0 + 12 + 6 + 0 = 18$.

**Verification via Green's Theorem:** $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds = \iint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\,dA$.

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial(x^2)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(y)}{\partial y} = 2x + 1.
$$

$$
\iint_D (2x+1)\,dA = \int_0^3 \int_0^2 (2x+1)\,dx\,dy = \int_0^3 \left[x^2 + x\right]_0^2 dy = \int_0^3 (4+2)\,dy = 6 \cdot 3 = 18. \quad \checkmark
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Outward flux} = 18.}
$$

## ⚠️ 8. Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1 — Confusing Divergence (Scalar) With Curl (Vector)

**The mistake.** Writing $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \text{some number}$ in 3D, or trying to take the curl of a scalar function.

**The fix.** Divergence $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ maps a vector field to a **scalar field**. Curl $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ maps a vector field to another **vector field** (in 3D). The "2D curl" $Q_x - P_y$ is a notational convenience — the actual curl vector points in the $z$-direction: $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (Q_x - P_y)\hat{k}$.

**Memory hook.** Dot product $\to$ scalar. Cross product $\to$ vector. $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ = dot = scalar. $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ = cross = vector.

### Pitfall 2 — "Curl = 0 Implies Conservative" on Non-Simply-Connected Domains

**The mistake.** Computing $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ and immediately concluding $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ without checking domain topology.

**The canonical counterexample.** The vortex field (Problem 1.6.P3):

$$
\mathbf{F} = \left\langle \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right\rangle \quad \text{on } \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}.
$$

This has $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 0$ everywhere it is defined, yet $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 2\pi \neq 0$ around the origin — so it is decidedly not conservative. The domain has a hole at the origin (it is NOT simply connected), and that hole is exactly what allows the non-trivial circulation to exist. Physically, this is the magnetic field of an infinite straight wire.

**The fix.** Before applying Theorem 1.6.4, always verify that the domain is simply connected (no holes, tunnels, or excluded points that loops can encircle).

### Pitfall 3 — Forgetting to Parameterize (and Check Orientation)

**The mistake.** Trying to compute $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ without writing down a concrete parameterization $\mathbf{r}(t)$. Related: parameterizing in the wrong direction and getting a sign error.

**The fix.** Always write $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $t \in [a,b]$ explicitly. Check that $\mathbf{r}(a)$ is the *start* of the curve and $\mathbf{r}(b)$ is the *end*, with the orientation matching the problem statement. Remember: $\int_{-C} = -\int_C$.

**Example of sign error.** For $C$ being the unit circle traversed *clockwise*, parameterize as $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos(-t), \sin(-t) \rangle = \langle \cos t, -\sin t \rangle$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$ — not the standard counterclockwise parameterization. Using the wrong sign gives $-2\pi$ instead of $+2\pi$ for the vortex field circulation.

### Pitfall 4 — Circulation ($\oint \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$) vs. Flux ($\oint \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds$)

**The mistake.** Confusing the circulation integral $\oint \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ (field component *along* the curve) with the flux integral $\oint \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds$ (field component *perpendicular* to the curve through it).

**The fix.** 
- **Circulation** uses $d\mathbf{r} = \langle dx, dy \rangle$ (tangent direction). Equals $\int P\,dx + Q\,dy$. Related to Stokes' theorem / curl.
- **Flux** uses $\hat{n}\,ds = \langle dy, -dx \rangle$ (normal direction, rotated 90° from tangent). Equals $\int P\,dy - Q\,dx$. Related to Green's theorem / divergence.

The formulas differ by a sign swap and $dx \leftrightarrow dy$ exchange — easy to mix up under exam pressure. Keep a reference card until it's reflexive.

### Pitfall 5 — Skipping the Curl Test Before Claiming Conservative

**The mistake.** "The field looks simple, surely it's conservative." Computing the potential for a non-conservative field leads to an inconsistency that reveals itself only at the $g'(y) = ?$ step, wasting effort.

**The fix.** *Always* do the curl test first: $Q_x - P_y = 0$? (2D), or all three components of $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$ are zero? (3D). This takes ten seconds and prevents a potential-function hunt on a non-conservative field. Only after confirming zero curl (and simply-connected domain) should you proceed to find $f$.

### Pitfall 6 — Dropping the Function of Integration

**The mistake.** When integrating $f_x = P$ with respect to $x$, writing $f = \int P\,dx$ and treating the "constant" as a pure number $C$ instead of $g(y, z)$.

**The fix.** When integrating a partial derivative equation, the "constant" can depend on all variables you are *not* integrating over. Integrating $f_x = P(x,y,z)$ with respect to $x$ gives $f = \int P\,dx + g(y,z)$, where $g$ is an unknown function of $y$ and $z$. The subsequent $f_y$ and $f_z$ steps pin down $g$.

## 🔗 9. Cross-References

### Looking Back
- **[[1.4 - Multivariable Differentiation & Gradients]]**: The gradient $\nabla f$ is the prototype vector field — every conservative field is a gradient. Partial derivatives $\partial P/\partial y$, $\partial Q/\partial x$ are the raw ingredients of both curl and the conservative criterion.
- **[[1.5 - Multiple Integrals & Jacobians]]**: The double/triple integrals of §1.5 are what appear on the right-hand side of Green's and Stokes' theorems in Chapter 1.7 — the payoff for learning divergence and curl here.

### Looking Forward
- **[[1.7 - Green's Stokes' and Divergence Theorems]]**: The direct payoff of this chapter. Green's Theorem connects the circulation $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ to the double integral of curl over the enclosed region; Stokes' generalizes this to surfaces; the Divergence Theorem connects flux to the triple integral of divergence. These are among the most powerful theorems in all of mathematics.
- **[[1.8 - Differential Forms & Exterior Calculus]]**: From the coordinate-heavy perspective of div and curl to the coordinate-free language of differential forms: the exterior derivative $d$ unifies gradient, curl, and divergence into a single operator, and Stokes' theorem becomes $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$, valid in all dimensions.

### Physics Connections
- **[[07 - Math and Physics/07 - Electrodynamics & Classical Field Theory]]** (7.x): Maxwell's equations *are* vector field equations written entirely in $\nabla \cdot$ and $\nabla \times$. Every result from this chapter applies directly: Gauss's law is $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0$; Faraday's law is $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\partial \mathbf{B}/\partial t$.
- **[[07 - Math and Physics/04 - Classical Mechanics & Dynamical Systems]]** (4.x): Work equals the line integral of force, $W = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$. Conservative forces (gravity, electrostatics) have zero curl, and their work is path-independent — the foundation of potential energy.
- **[[07 - Math and Physics/06 - Fluid Dynamics & Continuum Mechanics]]**: The fluid velocity field $\mathbf{v}$ has $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ (incompressible) and $\nabla \times \mathbf{v}$ = vorticity. The Navier-Stokes equations are written using these operators.

## 🧠 11. Study Tactics

### The Three-Layer Drill
1. **Mechanical layer.** Use the practice script to generate 8+ divergence and curl computations. Do them by hand, then run SymPy to verify. Build the reflex of "component by component, no shortcuts."
2. **Conceptual layer.** For each result, ask: is this field a source, a sink, or divergence-free? Is it rotating, counter-rotating, or irrotational? Can I visualize the tiny paddle-wheel or the expanding balloon?
3. **Connection layer.** Every time you see $\nabla \cdot$ or $\nabla \times$, ask: which Maxwell equation is this? Which fluid behavior? Which theorem in Chapter 1.7 will connect this to an integral?

### The Potential Function Drill
Pick a random conservative 2D field (e.g., $\mathbf{F} = \langle 3x^2 y, x^3 + 2y \rangle$). Verify curl = 0. Find $f$. Verify $\nabla f = \mathbf{F}$. Evaluate $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$ — both via the potential and via a direct parameterization. They must agree. This drill cements the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals in muscle memory.

### Spaced Repetition Cards
The most important cards to create:
- "What is the formula for curl in 3D?" — write the full determinant.
- "What does $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 0$ tell you, and under what condition?" — emphasize simply connected.
- "What is the counterexample to curl=0 ⟹ conservative?" — the vortex field.
