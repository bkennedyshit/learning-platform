# 5.2 — Entropy & Heat Engines

> *"The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum."* — Rudolf Clausius (1865)

Entropy is the central quantity of thermodynamics — the state function that quantifies irreversibility, determines the direction of spontaneous processes, and sets fundamental limits on the efficiency of heat engines. This chapter develops entropy from the Clausius inequality, constructs the Carnot cycle in complete detail, derives efficiency bounds for all heat engines, and introduces the $T$-$S$ diagram as the natural representation of thermal processes.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Derive entropy as a state function from the Clausius inequality.
2. Compute $\Delta S$ for ideal gas processes (isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric, isochoric, free expansion).
3. Construct the Carnot cycle on both $P$-$V$ and $T$-$S$ diagrams and derive its efficiency.
4. Prove that no engine exceeds Carnot efficiency (Carnot's theorem).
5. Analyze the Otto, Diesel, and Stirling cycles and compute their efficiencies.
6. Apply the entropy-increase principle to determine process spontaneity.
7. Connect macroscopic entropy to the statistical definition $S = k_B \ln \Omega$ (bridge to [[5.5 - Classical Statistical Mechanics - Microstates & Ensembles]]).

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 5.2.1 — Entropy (Thermodynamic)

The **entropy** $S$ is a state function defined by:

$$
dS = \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}
$$

for any reversible infinitesimal process. For a finite reversible process from state $A$ to state $B$:

$$
S(B) - S(A) = \int_A^B \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}
$$

### Definition 5.2.2 — Entropy of an Ideal Gas

For $n$ moles of an ideal gas with constant heat capacities:

$$
S(T, V) = nC_V \ln T + nR \ln V + S_0
$$

$$
S(T, P) = nC_P \ln T - nR \ln P + S_0'
$$

where $S_0$, $S_0'$ are reference constants.

### Definition 5.2.3 — Heat Engine

A **heat engine** is a cyclic device that:
1. Absorbs heat $Q_H > 0$ from a hot reservoir at temperature $T_H$.
2. Rejects heat $Q_C > 0$ to a cold reservoir at temperature $T_C < T_H$.
3. Produces net work $W = Q_H - Q_C > 0$.

The **thermal efficiency** is:

$$
\eta = \frac{W}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{Q_C}{Q_H}
$$

### Definition 5.2.4 — Carnot Cycle

The **Carnot cycle** consists of four reversible steps:
1. **Isothermal expansion** at $T_H$: system absorbs $Q_H$, expands from $V_1$ to $V_2$.
2. **Adiabatic expansion**: system cools from $T_H$ to $T_C$, expands from $V_2$ to $V_3$.
3. **Isothermal compression** at $T_C$: system rejects $Q_C$, compresses from $V_3$ to $V_4$.
4. **Adiabatic compression**: system heats from $T_C$ to $T_H$, compresses from $V_4$ to $V_1$.

### Definition 5.2.5 — Coefficient of Performance (Refrigerator)

For a refrigerator (reversed heat engine):

$$
\text{COP}_{\text{ref}} = \frac{Q_C}{W} = \frac{Q_C}{Q_H - Q_C} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C} \quad (\text{Carnot})
$$

### Definition 5.2.6 — T-S Diagram

In a **temperature-entropy** ($T$-$S$) diagram, the heat exchanged in a reversible process equals the area under the curve:

$$
Q_{\text{rev}} = \int T\,dS
$$

The Carnot cycle appears as a rectangle in the $T$-$S$ plane (two isotherms + two adiabats/isentropes).

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Postulate 5.2.P1 — Entropy Increase Principle

For an isolated system, the entropy never decreases:

$$
\Delta S_{\text{isolated}} \geq 0
$$

Equality holds only for reversible processes. This is the mathematical statement of the Second Law in entropy form.

### Postulate 5.2.P2 — Additivity of Entropy

For a composite system consisting of subsystems $A$ and $B$:

$$
S_{\text{total}} = S_A + S_B
$$

Entropy is an extensive quantity: it scales linearly with system size.

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 5.2.1 — Entropy Change of an Ideal Gas (General Process)

For $n$ moles of an ideal gas going from $(T_1, V_1)$ to $(T_2, V_2)$:

$$
\Delta S = nC_V \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}
$$

**Derivation.** Start from the First Law for a reversible process:

$$
\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = dU + P\,dV = nC_V\,dT + \frac{nRT}{V}\,dV
$$

Divide by $T$:

$$
dS = \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T} = nC_V\frac{dT}{T} + nR\frac{dV}{V}
$$

Integrate from state 1 to state 2:

$$
\Delta S = nC_V \int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{dT}{T} + nR\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{dV}{V} = nC_V\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Lemma 5.2.2 — Entropy Change for Isothermal Process

At constant $T$: $\Delta S = nR\ln(V_2/V_1) = Q/T$.

### Lemma 5.2.3 — Entropy Change for Isobaric Process

At constant $P$: $\Delta S = nC_P\ln(T_2/T_1)$.

**Derivation.** At constant $P$: $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = nC_P\,dT$. Therefore:

$$
\Delta S = \int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{nC_P\,dT}{T} = nC_P\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Lemma 5.2.4 — Entropy of Mixing (Ideal Gases)

When $n_A$ moles of gas $A$ at $(T, P)$ and $n_B$ moles of gas $B$ at $(T, P)$ are mixed at constant $T$ and total $P$:

$$
\Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -nR\sum_i x_i \ln x_i
$$

where $x_i = n_i/(n_A + n_B)$ is the mole fraction and $n = n_A + n_B$.

**Derivation.** Each gas expands from its initial volume to the total volume. Gas $A$ goes from $V_A$ to $V = V_A + V_B$. Since $V_A/V = P_A/P = x_A$ (Dalton's law):

$$
\Delta S_A = n_A R\ln\frac{V}{V_A} = n_A R\ln\frac{1}{x_A} = -n_A R\ln x_A
$$

Similarly for $B$. Total:

$$
\Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -n_A R\ln x_A - n_B R\ln x_B = -nR(x_A\ln x_A + x_B\ln x_B) \quad \blacksquare
$$

Since $0 < x_i < 1$, each $\ln x_i < 0$, so $\Delta S_{\text{mix}} > 0$ always. Mixing is irreversible.

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 5.2.1 — Carnot Efficiency

The efficiency of a Carnot engine operating between reservoirs at $T_H$ and $T_C$ is:

$$
\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}
$$

### Theorem 5.2.2 — Carnot's Theorem (Restated with Entropy)

All reversible engines operating between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency $\eta_C = 1 - T_C/T_H$. All irreversible engines have $\eta < \eta_C$.

### Theorem 5.2.3 — Clausius Inequality (Restated)

For any cyclic process:

$$
\oint \frac{\delta Q}{T} \leq 0
$$

### Theorem 5.2.4 — Entropy Generation

For any process in an isolated system:

$$
\Delta S = \Delta S_{\text{system}} + \Delta S_{\text{surroundings}} \geq 0
$$

The quantity $S_{\text{gen}} = \Delta S_{\text{universe}} \geq 0$ is the **entropy generated** (zero for reversible, positive for irreversible).

### Theorem 5.2.5 — Otto Cycle Efficiency

The air-standard Otto cycle (two adiabats + two isochores) has efficiency:

$$
\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}
$$

where $r = V_{\max}/V_{\min}$ is the compression ratio and $\gamma = C_P/C_V$.

### Theorem 5.2.6 — Diesel Cycle Efficiency

$$
\eta_{\text{Diesel}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}\cdot\frac{r_c^\gamma - 1}{\gamma(r_c - 1)}
$$

where $r$ is the compression ratio and $r_c = V_3/V_2$ is the cutoff ratio.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Complete Derivation of Carnot Cycle Efficiency

Consider $n$ moles of an ideal gas executing the four-step Carnot cycle.

**Step 1→2: Isothermal expansion at $T_H$.**

The gas expands from $V_1$ to $V_2$ while in thermal contact with the hot reservoir. Since $T$ is constant and $U$ depends only on $T$ for an ideal gas:

$$
\Delta U_{1\to2} = 0
$$

$$
Q_H = W_{1\to2} = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{nRT_H}{V}\,dV = nRT_H\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}
$$

**Step 2→3: Adiabatic expansion from $T_H$ to $T_C$.**

No heat exchange: $Q_{2\to3} = 0$. From the adiabatic relation $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}$:

$$
T_H V_2^{\gamma-1} = T_C V_3^{\gamma-1} \tag{I}
$$

Work done:

$$
W_{2\to3} = \frac{nR(T_H - T_C)}{\gamma - 1} = nC_V(T_H - T_C)
$$

**Step 3→4: Isothermal compression at $T_C$.**

$$
\Delta U_{3\to4} = 0
$$

$$
Q_C = |W_{3\to4}| = nRT_C\ln\frac{V_3}{V_4}
$$

(Heat is rejected: the system does negative work, or equivalently, work is done on the system.)

**Step 4→1: Adiabatic compression from $T_C$ to $T_H$.**

$$
Q_{4\to1} = 0, \quad T_C V_4^{\gamma-1} = T_H V_1^{\gamma-1} \tag{II}
$$

**Relating the volumes:** Divide equation (I) by equation (II):

$$
\frac{T_H V_2^{\gamma-1}}{T_C V_4^{\gamma-1}} = \frac{T_C V_3^{\gamma-1}}{T_H V_1^{\gamma-1}}
$$

This simplifies (after canceling $T_H/T_C$ factors) to:

$$
\frac{V_2^{\gamma-1}}{V_1^{\gamma-1}} = \frac{V_3^{\gamma-1}}{V_4^{\gamma-1}}
$$

$$
\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{V_3}{V_4} \tag{III}
$$

**Computing efficiency:**

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{nRT_C\ln(V_3/V_4)}{nRT_H\ln(V_2/V_1)}
$$

By relation (III), $\ln(V_3/V_4) = \ln(V_2/V_1)$. These cancel:

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.2 Derivation of Entropy Change for Irreversible Heat Transfer

Two bodies at temperatures $T_1 > T_2$ are brought into thermal contact. Heat $Q$ flows from body 1 to body 2 (irreversible process). Compute $\Delta S_{\text{universe}}$.

Body 1 loses heat $Q$ at temperature $T_1$: $\Delta S_1 = -Q/T_1$.

Body 2 gains heat $Q$ at temperature $T_2$: $\Delta S_2 = +Q/T_2$.

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = \Delta S_1 + \Delta S_2 = -\frac{Q}{T_1} + \frac{Q}{T_2} = Q\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)
$$

Since $T_1 > T_2 > 0$: $\frac{1}{T_2} > \frac{1}{T_1}$, so $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} > 0$. $\blacksquare$

The entropy generated quantifies the "lost work" — the work that could have been extracted by a Carnot engine operating between $T_1$ and $T_2$:

$$
W_{\text{lost}} = T_{\text{sink}} \cdot S_{\text{gen}} = T_2 \cdot Q\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right) = Q\left(1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)
$$

which is precisely $Q \cdot \eta_{\text{Carnot}}$.

### 5.3 Derivation of Otto Cycle Efficiency

The Otto cycle consists of:
- 1→2: Adiabatic compression ($V_1 \to V_2$, $T_1 \to T_2$)
- 2→3: Isochoric heat addition ($Q_{\text{in}}$ at $V_2$, $T_2 \to T_3$)
- 3→4: Adiabatic expansion ($V_2 \to V_1$, $T_3 \to T_4$)
- 4→1: Isochoric heat rejection ($Q_{\text{out}}$ at $V_1$, $T_4 \to T_1$)

Heat added: $Q_{\text{in}} = nC_V(T_3 - T_2)$.

Heat rejected: $Q_{\text{out}} = nC_V(T_4 - T_1)$.

Efficiency:

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{Q_{\text{out}}}{Q_{\text{in}}} = 1 - \frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2}
$$

From the adiabatic relations:

$$
T_1 V_1^{\gamma-1} = T_2 V_2^{\gamma-1} \implies \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma-1} = r^{\gamma-1}
$$

$$
T_3 V_2^{\gamma-1} = T_4 V_1^{\gamma-1} \implies \frac{T_3}{T_4} = \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma-1} = r^{\gamma-1}
$$

Therefore $T_2/T_1 = T_3/T_4$, which gives $T_4/T_1 = T_3/T_2$. Let $k = r^{\gamma-1}$:

$$
T_2 = kT_1, \quad T_3 = kT_4
$$

$$
\frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2} = \frac{T_4 - T_1}{kT_4 - kT_1} = \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}
$$

$$
\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}} \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.4 The T-S Diagram and Area Interpretation

For any reversible process: $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$. Therefore:

$$
Q_{\text{rev}} = \int T\,dS = \text{area under curve in } T\text{-}S \text{ plane}
$$

For a complete cycle, the net work equals the enclosed area in the $T$-$S$ diagram:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \oint T\,dS = Q_H - Q_C = \text{enclosed area}
$$

The Carnot cycle in $T$-$S$ space is a rectangle with vertices at $(S_1, T_C)$, $(S_2, T_C)$, $(S_2, T_H)$, $(S_1, T_H)$:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = (T_H - T_C)(S_2 - S_1)
$$

$$
Q_H = T_H(S_2 - S_1)
$$

$$
\eta = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_H} = \frac{T_H - T_C}{T_H} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} \quad \checkmark
$$

### 5.5 Entropy Production in a Throttling Process (Joule-Thomson)

In a throttling process (isenthalpic, $\Delta H = 0$), gas passes through a porous plug from pressure $P_1$ to $P_2 < P_1$. The process is irreversible and adiabatic ($Q = 0$).

For an ideal gas: $H = H(T)$ only, so $\Delta T = 0$. The entropy change:

$$
\Delta S = nC_P\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - nR\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = 0 - nR\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = nR\ln\frac{P_1}{P_2} > 0
$$

since $P_1 > P_2$. Entropy is generated even though no heat is exchanged — the irreversibility comes from the pressure drop across the plug. $\blacksquare$

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 5.2.E1 — Carnot Engine Between Two Reservoirs

A Carnot engine operates between $T_H = 600\,\text{K}$ and $T_C = 300\,\text{K}$, absorbing $Q_H = 1000\,\text{J}$ per cycle. Find the work output, heat rejected, and entropy changes.

**Solution:**

Efficiency: $\eta = 1 - 300/600 = 0.5$.

Work output: $W = \eta Q_H = 0.5 \times 1000 = 500\,\text{J}$.

Heat rejected: $Q_C = Q_H - W = 1000 - 500 = 500\,\text{J}$.

Entropy changes per cycle:

$$
\Delta S_H = -\frac{Q_H}{T_H} = -\frac{1000}{600} = -\frac{5}{3}\,\text{J/K}
$$

$$
\Delta S_C = +\frac{Q_C}{T_C} = +\frac{500}{300} = +\frac{5}{3}\,\text{J/K}
$$

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = -\frac{5}{3} + \frac{5}{3} = 0 \quad \checkmark \text{ (reversible)}
$$

---

### Example 5.2.E2 — Entropy Change: Heating Water

Compute $\Delta S$ when 1 kg of water is heated from $20°\text{C}$ to $100°\text{C}$ at constant pressure ($C_P = 4186\,\text{J/(kg·K)}$).

**Solution:**

$$
\Delta S = \int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{mC_P\,dT}{T} = mC_P\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}
$$

Convert to Kelvin: $T_1 = 293\,\text{K}$, $T_2 = 373\,\text{K}$.

$$
\Delta S = (1)(4186)\ln\frac{373}{293} = 4186\ln(1.273) = 4186 \times 0.2412 = 1010\,\text{J/K}
$$

If the heat source is a reservoir at $T_R = 373\,\text{K}$:

$$
Q = mC_P(T_2 - T_1) = 4186 \times 80 = 334{,}880\,\text{J}
$$

$$
\Delta S_{\text{reservoir}} = -\frac{Q}{T_R} = -\frac{334880}{373} = -898\,\text{J/K}
$$

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = 1010 - 898 = 112\,\text{J/K} > 0 \quad (\text{irreversible})
$$

---

### Example 5.2.E3 — Otto Cycle Analysis

An Otto cycle engine has compression ratio $r = 8$ and uses air ($\gamma = 1.4$) as the working fluid. The initial conditions are $T_1 = 300\,\text{K}$, $P_1 = 100\,\text{kPa}$. Heat input per cycle: $Q_{\text{in}} = 800\,\text{kJ/kg}$.

**Solution:**

Efficiency:

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}} = 1 - \frac{1}{8^{0.4}} = 1 - \frac{1}{8^{0.4}}
$$

Compute $8^{0.4} = e^{0.4\ln 8} = e^{0.4 \times 2.079} = e^{0.8318} = 2.298$.

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{1}{2.298} = 1 - 0.435 = 0.565 = 56.5\%
$$

Temperature after adiabatic compression:

$$
T_2 = T_1 \cdot r^{\gamma-1} = 300 \times 2.298 = 689\,\text{K}
$$

Temperature after heat addition (isochoric, $Q_{\text{in}} = C_V(T_3 - T_2)$, with $C_V = 718\,\text{J/(kg·K)}$):

$$
T_3 = T_2 + \frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{C_V} = 689 + \frac{800000}{718} = 689 + 1114 = 1803\,\text{K}
$$

Temperature after adiabatic expansion:

$$
T_4 = \frac{T_3}{r^{\gamma-1}} = \frac{1803}{2.298} = 784\,\text{K}
$$

Net work: $W = \eta \cdot Q_{\text{in}} = 0.565 \times 800 = 452\,\text{kJ/kg}$.

### Example 5.2.E4 — Entropy of Mixing

Two moles of $\text{N}_2$ and three moles of $\text{O}_2$, both at $T = 300\,\text{K}$ and $P = 1\,\text{atm}$, are mixed. Find $\Delta S_{\text{mix}}$.

**Solution:**

Total moles: $n = 2 + 3 = 5$. Mole fractions: $x_{\text{N}_2} = 2/5 = 0.4$, $x_{\text{O}_2} = 3/5 = 0.6$.

$$
\Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -nR\sum_i x_i\ln x_i = -5(8.314)[0.4\ln 0.4 + 0.6\ln 0.6]
$$

$$
= -41.57[0.4(-0.9163) + 0.6(-0.5108)] = -41.57[-0.3665 - 0.3065]
$$

$$
= -41.57 \times (-0.6730) = 27.97\,\text{J/K}
$$

### Example 5.2.E5 — Maximum Work from Two Finite Reservoirs

Two identical bodies (heat capacity $C$, initial temperatures $T_H$ and $T_C$) are used as reservoirs for a heat engine. Find the maximum work extractable and the final equilibrium temperature.

**Solution:**

Let the final temperature of both bodies be $T_f$. For maximum work, the process must be reversible: $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = 0$.

$$
\Delta S = C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_H} + C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_C} = C\ln\frac{T_f^2}{T_H T_C} = 0
$$

$$
T_f^2 = T_H T_C \implies T_f = \sqrt{T_H T_C}
$$

The final temperature is the geometric mean. Total heat released by hot body: $C(T_H - T_f)$. Total heat absorbed by cold body: $C(T_f - T_C)$.

Maximum work:

$$
W_{\max} = C(T_H - T_f) - C(T_f - T_C) = C(T_H + T_C - 2T_f) = C(T_H + T_C - 2\sqrt{T_H T_C})
$$

$$
W_{\max} = C(\sqrt{T_H} - \sqrt{T_C})^2
$$

This elegant result shows that $W_{\max} = 0$ when $T_H = T_C$ (no temperature difference, no work possible). $\blacksquare$

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Efficiency of the Otto Cycle in Terms of Compression Ratio

**Problem.** Derive the thermal efficiency of the ideal Otto cycle (gasoline engine) as a function of the compression ratio $r = V_1/V_2$ and the heat capacity ratio $\gamma = C_P/C_V$. Then compute the efficiency for $r = 10$ with air ($\gamma = 1.4$).

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### The Four Processes of the Otto Cycle

The ideal Otto cycle consists of:
1. **1→2:** Adiabatic compression ($V_1 \to V_2$, $T_1 \to T_2$)
2. **2→3:** Isochoric (constant volume) heat addition ($Q_H$ in, $T_2 \to T_3$)
3. **3→4:** Adiabatic expansion ($V_2 \to V_1$, $T_3 \to T_4$)
4. **4→1:** Isochoric heat rejection ($Q_C$ out, $T_4 \to T_1$)

#### Step 1: Adiabatic Relations

For an adiabatic process on an ideal gas with constant $\gamma$:

$$
TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}
$$

Process 1→2 (compression):

$$
T_1 V_1^{\gamma-1} = T_2 V_2^{\gamma-1} \implies T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma-1} = T_1 r^{\gamma-1}
$$

Process 3→4 (expansion):

$$
T_3 V_2^{\gamma-1} = T_4 V_1^{\gamma-1} \implies T_4 = T_3\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^{\gamma-1} = \frac{T_3}{r^{\gamma-1}}
$$

#### Step 2: Heat Exchanges

Both heat exchanges occur at constant volume, so no work is done during these steps:

$$
Q_H = nC_V(T_3 - T_2) \quad \text{(heat absorbed, process 2→3)}
$$

$$
|Q_C| = nC_V(T_4 - T_1) \quad \text{(heat rejected, process 4→1)}
$$

#### Step 3: Thermal Efficiency

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{|Q_C|}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2}
$$

Now use the adiabatic relations. From Step 1:

$$
T_2 = T_1 r^{\gamma-1}, \qquad T_4 = T_3 r^{-(\gamma-1)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2} = \frac{T_3 r^{-(\gamma-1)} - T_1}{T_3 - T_1 r^{\gamma-1}}
$$

Factor $r^{-(\gamma-1)}$ from the numerator:

$$
= \frac{r^{-(\gamma-1)}(T_3 - T_1 r^{\gamma-1})}{T_3 - T_1 r^{\gamma-1}} = r^{-(\gamma-1)} = \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}}
$$

#### Step 4: Numerical Evaluation

For $r = 10$, $\gamma = 1.4$:

$$
r^{\gamma-1} = 10^{0.4} = 10^{2/5} = (10^2)^{1/5} = 100^{1/5} = 2.512
$$

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{1}{2.512} = 1 - 0.398 = 0.602 = 60.2\%
$$

**Physical interpretation:** Higher compression ratios give higher efficiency because the gas reaches higher temperatures during compression, increasing the temperature difference that drives the cycle. Real gasoline engines achieve $r \approx 8$–$12$ (limited by knock/pre-ignition), giving theoretical efficiencies of 56–63%. Actual efficiencies are 25–35% due to friction, incomplete combustion, and heat losses.

**Comparison with Carnot:** The Otto cycle efficiency depends only on $r$ and $\gamma$, not on the peak temperature $T_3$. A Carnot engine between $T_1$ and $T_3$ would have $\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - T_1/T_3 \gt  \eta_{\text{Otto}}$ always (since the Otto cycle is not reversible — the heat exchange occurs over a range of temperatures, not at fixed $T$).

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Complete Stirling Cycle Calculation

**Problem.** An ideal Stirling engine uses 0.5 mol of helium ($\gamma = 5/3$, monatomic) operating between $T_H = 800\,\text{K}$ and $T_C = 300\,\text{K}$. The volume ratio is $V_{\max}/V_{\min} = 4$. Assuming a perfect regenerator, calculate: (a) heat absorbed from the hot reservoir, (b) net work per cycle, (c) thermal efficiency.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### The Four Processes of the Stirling Cycle

1. **1→2:** Isothermal expansion at $T_H$ ($V_{\min} \to V_{\max}$)
2. **2→3:** Isochoric cooling at $V_{\max}$ ($T_H \to T_C$) — heat stored in regenerator
3. **3→4:** Isothermal compression at $T_C$ ($V_{\max} \to V_{\min}$)
4. **4→1:** Isochoric heating at $V_{\min}$ ($T_C \to T_H$) — heat recovered from regenerator

#### Step 1: Isothermal Expansion (1→2)

At constant temperature $T_H$, for an ideal gas:

$$
Q_{12} = W_{12} = nRT_H\ln\frac{V_{\max}}{V_{\min}} = (0.5)(8.314)(800)\ln 4
$$

$$
Q_{12} = 3325.6 \times 1.3863 = 4611\,\text{J}
$$

#### Step 2: Isochoric Cooling (2→3)

At constant volume, $W = 0$. Heat rejected:

$$
Q_{23} = nC_V(T_C - T_H) = (0.5)\left(\frac{3}{2}\times 8.314\right)(300 - 800) = (0.5)(12.471)(-500) = -3118\,\text{J}
$$

This heat is stored in the regenerator (not rejected to the cold reservoir).

#### Step 3: Isothermal Compression (3→4)

At constant temperature $T_C$:

$$
Q_{34} = W_{34} = nRT_C\ln\frac{V_{\min}}{V_{\max}} = (0.5)(8.314)(300)\ln\frac{1}{4} = 1247.1 \times (-1.3863) = -1729\,\text{J}
$$

This heat is rejected to the cold reservoir.

#### Step 4: Isochoric Heating (4→1)

At constant volume, $W = 0$. Heat absorbed:

$$
Q_{41} = nC_V(T_H - T_C) = (0.5)(12.471)(500) = +3118\,\text{J}
$$

This heat is recovered from the regenerator. With a perfect regenerator: $Q_{41} = -Q_{23}$, so the regenerator is energy-neutral.

#### Part (a): Heat Absorbed from Hot Reservoir

With a perfect regenerator, the only heat absorbed from the external hot reservoir is during the isothermal expansion:

$$
Q_H = Q_{12} = 4611\,\text{J}
$$

#### Part (b): Net Work

$$
W_{\text{net}} = W_{12} + W_{34} = 4611 + (-1729) = 2882\,\text{J}
$$

Alternatively: $W_{\text{net}} = nR(T_H - T_C)\ln(V_{\max}/V_{\min}) = (0.5)(8.314)(500)\ln 4 = 2078.5 \times 1.3863 = 2881\,\text{J}$. ✓

#### Part (c): Thermal Efficiency

$$
\eta = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_H} = \frac{2882}{4611} = 0.625 = 62.5\%
$$

**Check against Carnot:** $\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - 300/800 = 0.625 = 62.5\%$.

The Stirling cycle with a perfect regenerator achieves Carnot efficiency! This is because:
- All heat input from external sources occurs at $T_H$ (isothermal expansion).
- All heat rejection to external sinks occurs at $T_C$ (isothermal compression).
- The isochoric processes exchange heat only with the internal regenerator.

This makes the Stirling engine thermodynamically equivalent to the Carnot engine in the ideal limit. $\blacksquare$

</details>

### Example 8.3 — Entropy Change for Ice Melting at 0°C

**Problem.** Calculate the total entropy change of the universe when 100 g of ice at $0°\text{C}$ melts in a room at $25°\text{C}$. The latent heat of fusion is $L_f = 334\,\text{J/g}$. After melting, the water remains at $0°\text{C}$ (assume the room is large enough to stay at $25°\text{C}$).

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Identify the Heat Transfer

The ice absorbs heat $Q$ from the room to melt:

$$
Q = mL_f = (100\,\text{g})(334\,\text{J/g}) = 33{,}400\,\text{J}
$$

#### Step 2: Entropy Change of the Ice (System)

The ice melts at constant temperature $T_{\text{ice}} = 273.15\,\text{K}$. Since the phase transition occurs at constant $T$ and $P$:

$$
\Delta S_{\text{ice}} = \frac{Q}{T_{\text{ice}}} = \frac{33400}{273.15} = 122.3\,\text{J/K}
$$

#### Step 3: Entropy Change of the Room (Surroundings)

The room loses heat $Q$ at constant temperature $T_{\text{room}} = 298.15\,\text{K}$:

$$
\Delta S_{\text{room}} = -\frac{Q}{T_{\text{room}}} = -\frac{33400}{298.15} = -112.0\,\text{J/K}
$$

#### Step 4: Total Entropy Change of the Universe

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = \Delta S_{\text{ice}} + \Delta S_{\text{room}} = 122.3 + (-112.0) = +10.3\,\text{J/K}
$$

#### Step 5: Interpretation

$\Delta S_{\text{universe}} \gt  0$, confirming the process is irreversible and spontaneous. The irreversibility arises from heat transfer across a finite temperature difference ($\Delta T = 25\,\text{K}$).

**Limiting case:** If the room were at $0°\text{C}$ (same temperature as the ice), then $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = Q/273.15 - Q/273.15 = 0$, and the process would be reversible — but infinitely slow (no driving force for heat transfer).

**Connection to free energy:** At constant $T$ and $P$, the Gibbs free energy change for the ice is:

$$
\Delta G_{\text{ice}} = \Delta H - T_{\text{room}}\Delta S_{\text{ice}} = 33400 - 298.15 \times 122.3 = 33400 - 36460 = -3060\,\text{J}
$$

Wait — this isn't quite right because the ice is at a different temperature than the room. The correct statement is: at $T = 298\,\text{K}$ and $P = 1\,\text{atm}$, the Gibbs free energy of liquid water is lower than that of ice, so melting is spontaneous. The entropy production $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = 10.3\,\text{J/K}$ quantifies the "driving force" for this irreversible process.

</details>

### Example 8.4 — Entropy Change in Irreversible Heat Transfer Between Two Blocks

**Problem.** Two identical copper blocks ($m = 1\,\text{kg}$, $C = 385\,\text{J/(kg·K)}$) are initially at $T_1 = 600\,\text{K}$ and $T_2 = 200\,\text{K}$. They are brought into thermal contact and reach equilibrium. (a) Find the final temperature. (b) Compute $\Delta S_{\text{universe}}$. (c) Compare with the maximum work that could have been extracted.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Part (a): Final Temperature

Energy conservation (no work, no heat loss to surroundings):

$$
mC(T_f - T_1) + mC(T_f - T_2) = 0
$$

$$
2mCT_f = mC(T_1 + T_2) \implies T_f = \frac{T_1 + T_2}{2} = \frac{600 + 200}{2} = 400\,\text{K}
$$

#### Part (b): Entropy Change

$$
\Delta S_1 = mC\ln\frac{T_f}{T_1} = (1)(385)\ln\frac{400}{600} = 385\ln\frac{2}{3} = 385 \times (-0.4055) = -156.1\,\text{J/K}
$$

$$
\Delta S_2 = mC\ln\frac{T_f}{T_2} = (1)(385)\ln\frac{400}{200} = 385\ln 2 = 385 \times 0.6931 = 266.9\,\text{J/K}
$$

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = \Delta S_1 + \Delta S_2 = -156.1 + 266.9 = +110.8\,\text{J/K}
$$

Verify: $\Delta S = mC\ln\frac{T_f^2}{T_1 T_2} = 385\ln\frac{400^2}{600\times200} = 385\ln\frac{160000}{120000} = 385\ln\frac{4}{3} = 385 \times 0.2877 = 110.8\,\text{J/K}$. ✓

#### Part (c): Maximum Extractable Work

If instead of direct contact, we used a reversible heat engine between the blocks (as in Example 5.2.E5), the final temperature would be:

$$
T_f^{\text{rev}} = \sqrt{T_1 T_2} = \sqrt{600 \times 200} = \sqrt{120000} = 346.4\,\text{K}
$$

Maximum work:

$$
W_{\max} = mC(T_1 + T_2 - 2\sqrt{T_1 T_2}) = 385(600 + 200 - 2\times346.4) = 385(800 - 692.8) = 385 \times 107.2 = 41{,}270\,\text{J}
$$

**Lost work due to irreversibility:**

$$
W_{\text{lost}} = T_{\text{ref}}\Delta S_{\text{universe}}
$$

If we take $T_{\text{ref}} = T_f = 400\,\text{K}$ (the final equilibrium temperature as the reference):

$$
W_{\text{lost}} = 400 \times 110.8 = 44{,}320\,\text{J}
$$

This is close to (but not exactly equal to) $W_{\max}$ because the "reference temperature" for lost work depends on the specific process. The exact relation is: the irreversible process wastes the opportunity to extract $W_{\max} = 41.3\,\text{kJ}$ of useful work.

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### Appendix 9.1 — Full Derivation of Carnot Efficiency from the T-S Diagram

The Carnot cycle is the simplest reversible cycle and achieves the maximum possible efficiency between two thermal reservoirs. Here we derive its efficiency purely from the $T$-$S$ diagram, making the geometric interpretation transparent.

**The Carnot cycle in $T$-$S$ space.** The four processes are:

1. **Isothermal expansion (A→B):** The system absorbs heat $Q_H$ from the hot reservoir at constant temperature $T_H$. Since $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$:

$$
Q_H = \int_A^B T\,dS = T_H(S_B - S_A) = T_H\Delta S
$$

where $\Delta S = S_B - S_A > 0$.

2. **Adiabatic expansion (B→C):** No heat exchange ($\delta Q = 0$), so $dS = 0$. The entropy remains constant at $S_B$ while the temperature drops from $T_H$ to $T_C$. This is a vertical line downward in the $T$-$S$ diagram.

3. **Isothermal compression (C→D):** The system rejects heat $Q_C$ to the cold reservoir at constant temperature $T_C$:

$$
Q_C = \int_C^D T\,dS = T_C(S_D - S_C) = T_C(S_A - S_B) = -T_C\Delta S
$$

The heat rejected (positive quantity flowing out) is $|Q_C| = T_C\Delta S$.

4. **Adiabatic compression (D→A):** No heat exchange, entropy constant at $S_A$, temperature rises from $T_C$ to $T_H$. Vertical line upward.

**Geometric interpretation.** The cycle forms a rectangle in $T$-$S$ space with:
- Width: $\Delta S = S_B - S_A$
- Height: $\Delta T = T_H - T_C$

The area enclosed by the cycle equals the net work:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \oint T\,dS = (T_H - T_C)\Delta S
$$

This follows because the area under the top isotherm (A→B) is $T_H\Delta S = Q_H$, and the area under the bottom isotherm (C→D, traversed right to left) is $T_C\Delta S = |Q_C|$. The enclosed area is the difference:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = Q_H - |Q_C| = T_H\Delta S - T_C\Delta S = (T_H - T_C)\Delta S
$$

**Efficiency:**

$$
\eta = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_H} = \frac{(T_H - T_C)\Delta S}{T_H\Delta S} = \frac{T_H - T_C}{T_H} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}
$$

$$
\boxed{\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}}
$$

**Why no real engine can exceed this.** Any irreversible process generates entropy: $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} > 0$. For a cycle (system returns to initial state, $\Delta S_{\text{system}} = 0$):

$$
\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = -\frac{Q_H}{T_H} + \frac{|Q_C|}{T_C} \geq 0
$$

$$
\frac{|Q_C|}{T_C} \geq \frac{Q_H}{T_H} \implies |Q_C| \geq Q_H\frac{T_C}{T_H}
$$

$$
\eta = 1 - \frac{|Q_C|}{Q_H} \leq 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} = \eta_{\text{Carnot}}
$$

Equality holds only for reversible cycles. $\blacksquare$

**Reference:** Kittel & Kroemer, *Thermal Physics*, Ch. 2; Fermi, *Thermodynamics*, Ch. 4; David Tong, *Statistical Mechanics* Notes §1.

### Appendix 9.2 — Clausius Inequality and the Direction of Spontaneous Processes

The Clausius inequality is the mathematical statement of the Second Law for arbitrary cyclic processes:

$$
\oint \frac{\delta Q}{T_{\text{boundary}}} \leq 0
$$

where $T_{\text{boundary}}$ is the temperature of the boundary (reservoir) at which heat $\delta Q$ enters the system. Equality holds for reversible cycles.

**Derivation.** Consider any cyclic process. Replace each infinitesimal heat exchange $\delta Q$ at boundary temperature $T_b$ with a Carnot engine operating between a master reservoir at $T_0$ and the system boundary at $T_b$. The Carnot engine absorbs $\delta Q_0$ from the master reservoir and delivers $\delta Q$ to the system:

$$
\frac{\delta Q_0}{T_0} = \frac{\delta Q}{T_b} \implies \delta Q_0 = T_0\frac{\delta Q}{T_b}
$$

Over the complete cycle, the total heat drawn from the master reservoir is:

$$
Q_0 = T_0\oint\frac{\delta Q}{T_b}
$$

By the Kelvin-Planck statement: no cyclic device can convert heat entirely to work. The composite system (original system + all Carnot engines) is cyclic, so it cannot have drawn net heat from the single master reservoir and converted it all to work. Therefore $Q_0 \leq 0$:

$$
T_0\oint\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} \leq 0 \implies \oint\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} \leq 0 \quad \blacksquare
$$

**Consequence for entropy.** For any process from state 1 to state 2:

$$
S_2 - S_1 \geq \int_1^2\frac{\delta Q}{T_b}
$$

with equality for reversible processes. For an isolated system ($\delta Q = 0$):

$$
\Delta S \geq 0
$$

This is the entropy increase principle: the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. $\blacksquare$

**Reference:** Callen, *Thermodynamics*, Ch. 4; MIT OCW 8.333 Lecture 2.

### Appendix 9.3 — Entropy as a Measure of Irreversibility: The Gouy-Stodola Theorem

The Gouy-Stodola theorem quantifies the "lost work" due to irreversibility:

$$
W_{\text{lost}} = T_0 \cdot S_{\text{gen}}
$$

where $T_0$ is the temperature of the environment (dead state) and $S_{\text{gen}}$ is the total entropy generated by the irreversible process.

**Derivation.** For a system undergoing a process while exchanging heat with a single reservoir at $T_0$:

- Maximum (reversible) work: $W_{\text{rev}} = -(F_2 - F_1)$ where $F = U - T_0 S$ is the availability (Helmholtz free energy relative to the environment).
- Actual work: $W_{\text{actual}} = W_{\text{rev}} - W_{\text{lost}}$.

The entropy balance: $S_{\text{gen}} = \Delta S_{\text{system}} + \Delta S_{\text{surroundings}} = (S_2 - S_1) - Q/T_0$.

From the First Law: $Q = \Delta U + W_{\text{actual}}$, so:

$$
S_{\text{gen}} = (S_2 - S_1) - \frac{\Delta U + W_{\text{actual}}}{T_0}
$$

$$
T_0 S_{\text{gen}} = T_0(S_2 - S_1) - \Delta U - W_{\text{actual}} = -(F_2 - F_1) - W_{\text{actual}} = W_{\text{rev}} - W_{\text{actual}} = W_{\text{lost}}
$$

$$
\boxed{W_{\text{lost}} = T_0 S_{\text{gen}}}
$$

**Physical meaning:** Every bit of entropy generated in an irreversible process represents lost opportunity to do useful work, valued at $T_0$ per unit of entropy. This is the thermodynamic basis for exergy analysis in engineering.

**Reference:** Bejan, *Advanced Engineering Thermodynamics*, Ch. 2; Kittel & Kroemer, *Thermal Physics*, Ch. 8.
