# 5.6 — The Partition Function & Free Energy

> *"The partition function is the rosetta stone of statistical mechanics — from it, all thermodynamic quantities can be decoded."* — after Kittel & Kroemer

The partition function $Z$ is the central object of the canonical ensemble. Once computed, every thermodynamic observable — energy, entropy, free energy, heat capacity, pressure — follows by differentiation. This chapter derives the connection $F = -k_BT\ln Z$, computes $Z$ for fundamental systems (two-state, harmonic oscillator, ideal gas), and proves the equipartition theorem.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Compute the canonical partition function $Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ for discrete and continuous systems.
2. Derive all thermodynamic quantities from $Z$: $\langle E\rangle$, $S$, $F$, $C_V$, $P$.
3. Prove $F = -k_BT\ln Z$ (bridge between statistical and thermodynamic free energy).
4. Compute $Z$ for: two-state system, quantum harmonic oscillator, ideal gas, rigid rotor.
5. Derive and apply the equipartition theorem.
6. Distinguish single-particle from $N$-particle partition functions: $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$.
7. Compute fluctuations $\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle = k_BT^2 C_V$.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 5.6.1 — Canonical Partition Function

$$
Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i} = \sum_i e^{-E_i/(k_BT)}
$$

where the sum runs over all microstates $i$ of the system, and $\beta = 1/(k_BT)$.

For a continuous system: $Z = \frac{1}{N!h^{3N}}\int e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})}\,d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p$.

### Definition 5.6.2 — Helmholtz Free Energy from $Z$

$$
F = -k_BT\ln Z
$$

### Definition 5.6.3 — Thermodynamic Quantities from $Z$

$$
\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta} = k_BT^2\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial T}
$$

$$
S = k_B\ln Z + k_B T\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial T} = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\bigg|_V
$$

$$
P = k_BT\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial V}\bigg|_T = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\bigg|_T
$$

$$
C_V = \frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T}\bigg|_V = k_B\beta^2\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2}
$$

### Definition 5.6.4 — Energy Fluctuations

$$
\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle = \langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 = \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = k_BT^2 C_V
$$

---

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Postulate 5.6.P1 — Factorization for Independent Subsystems

If a system consists of $N$ independent (non-interacting) distinguishable subsystems with individual partition functions $z_i$:

$$
Z = \prod_{i=1}^N z_i
$$

For $N$ identical, indistinguishable particles: $Z_N = Z_1^N / N!$.

---

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 5.6.1 — Two-State System Partition Function

Single particle with states $\epsilon_0 = 0$ and $\epsilon_1 = \epsilon$:

$$
z = 1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon}
$$

$$
\langle\epsilon\rangle = \frac{\epsilon e^{-\beta\epsilon}}{1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon}} = \frac{\epsilon}{e^{\beta\epsilon} + 1}
$$

### Lemma 5.6.2 — Quantum Harmonic Oscillator Partition Function

Energy levels: $E_n = (n + 1/2)\hbar\omega$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$

$$
z = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta(n+1/2)\hbar\omega} = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty (e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})^n = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}
$$

### Lemma 5.6.3 — Ideal Gas Partition Function

For $N$ identical non-interacting monatomic particles in volume $V$:

$$
Z_1 = \frac{V}{\lambda^3}, \quad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}} \quad \text{(thermal de Broglie wavelength)}
$$

$$
Z_N = \frac{Z_1^N}{N!} = \frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{V}{\lambda^3}\right)^N
$$

---

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 5.6.1 — $F = -k_BT\ln Z$

The Helmholtz free energy equals $-k_BT$ times the logarithm of the partition function.

### Theorem 5.6.2 — Equipartition Theorem

For a classical system in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, each quadratic degree of freedom in the Hamiltonian contributes $\frac{1}{2}k_BT$ to the average energy.

If $H = \sum_{i=1}^f a_i x_i^2 + \text{(non-quadratic terms)}$, then $\langle a_i x_i^2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}k_BT$ for each quadratic term.

### Theorem 5.6.3 — Einstein Model of Solid

$3N$ independent quantum harmonic oscillators at frequency $\omega$:

$$
\langle E\rangle = 3N\hbar\omega\left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}\right)
$$

$$
C_V = 3Nk_B\left(\frac{\Theta_E}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{\Theta_E/T}}{(e^{\Theta_E/T}-1)^2}
$$

where $\Theta_E = \hbar\omega/k_B$ is the Einstein temperature.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Proof: $F = -k_BT\ln Z$

From the canonical distribution $P_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$, the Gibbs entropy is:

$$
S = -k_B\sum_i P_i\ln P_i = -k_B\sum_i P_i(-\beta E_i - \ln Z)
$$

$$
= k_B\beta\sum_i P_i E_i + k_B\ln Z\sum_i P_i = k_B\beta\langle E\rangle + k_B\ln Z
$$

$$
= \frac{\langle E\rangle}{T} + k_B\ln Z
$$

Rearrange: $\langle E\rangle - TS = -k_BT\ln Z$. Since $F = U - TS = \langle E\rangle - TS$:

$$
F = -k_BT\ln Z \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.2 Proof of the Equipartition Theorem

Consider a single quadratic term $H_i = a_i x_i^2$ in the Hamiltonian. The average:

$$
\langle a_i x_i^2\rangle = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} a_i x_i^2 e^{-\beta a_i x_i^2}\,dx_i}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\beta a_i x_i^2}\,dx_i}
$$

The denominator is a Gaussian integral: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-\beta a_i x_i^2}\,dx_i = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\beta a_i}}$.

The numerator: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty a_i x_i^2 e^{-\beta a_i x_i^2}\,dx_i = a_i \cdot \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{(\beta a_i)^3}} = \frac{1}{2\beta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\beta a_i}}$.

(Using $\int_{-\infty}^\infty x^2 e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi/\alpha^3}$.)

Dividing:

$$
\langle a_i x_i^2\rangle = \frac{\frac{1}{2\beta}\sqrt{\pi/(\beta a_i)}}{\sqrt{\pi/(\beta a_i)}} = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{1}{2}k_BT \quad \blacksquare
$$

### 5.3 Derivation of Ideal Gas Thermodynamics from $Z$

Single-particle partition function (translational):

$$
Z_1 = \frac{1}{h^3}\int e^{-\beta p^2/(2m)}\,d^3p \int d^3q = \frac{V}{h^3}\left(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-\beta p_x^2/(2m)}\,dp_x\right)^3
$$

Each Gaussian integral: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-\beta p^2/(2m)}\,dp = \sqrt{2\pi m/\beta} = \sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$.

$$
Z_1 = \frac{V}{h^3}(2\pi mk_BT)^{3/2} = \frac{V}{\lambda^3}
$$

where $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength.

For $N$ indistinguishable particles: $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$.

$$
\ln Z_N = N\ln Z_1 - \ln N! \approx N\ln\frac{V}{\lambda^3} - N\ln N + N = N\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + N
$$

**Free energy:**

$$
F = -k_BT\ln Z_N = -Nk_BT\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + 1\right]
$$

**Pressure:**

$$
P = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\bigg|_T = \frac{Nk_BT}{V} \implies PV = Nk_BT \quad \checkmark
$$

**Energy:**

$$
\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z_N}{\partial\beta} = N \cdot \frac{3}{2\beta} = \frac{3}{2}Nk_BT \quad \checkmark
$$

**Heat capacity:** $C_V = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B$. $\blacksquare$

### 5.4 Derivation of Energy Fluctuations

$$
\langle E^2\rangle = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_i E_i^2 e^{-\beta E_i} = \frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial^2 Z}{\partial\beta^2}
$$

$$
\langle E\rangle^2 = \left(\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\right)^2 \cdot (-1)^2 = \left(\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}\right)^2
$$

Wait — more carefully: $\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}$, so $\langle E\rangle = -\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}$.

$$
\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\right) \cdot (-1)^{...}
$$

Let us use the direct approach:

$$
\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial^2 Z}{\partial\beta^2} - \left(\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\right)^2 = \langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 = \langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle
$$

Now relate to $C_V$: $\langle E\rangle = -\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$, so:

$$
C_V = \frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial\beta}\frac{d\beta}{dT} = -\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{k_BT^2}\right) = \frac{\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle}{k_BT^2}
$$

$$
\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle = k_BT^2 C_V \quad \blacksquare
$$

For an ideal gas: $\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle = k_BT^2 \cdot \frac{3}{2}Nk_B = \frac{3}{2}Nk_B^2T^2$. Relative fluctuation: $\sqrt{\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle}/\langle E\rangle = \sqrt{2/(3N)} \to 0$ as $N\to\infty$.

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 5.6.E1 — Two-State System: Complete Thermodynamics

For $N$ particles, each with states $0$ and $\epsilon$:

$Z_N = z^N = (1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon})^N$.

$$
F = -Nk_BT\ln(1 + e^{-\beta\epsilon})
$$

$$
\langle E\rangle = \frac{N\epsilon}{e^{\beta\epsilon} + 1}
$$

$$
C_V = Nk_B\left(\frac{\beta\epsilon}{2}\right)^2 \text{sech}^2\left(\frac{\beta\epsilon}{2}\right) = Nk_B\frac{(\beta\epsilon)^2 e^{\beta\epsilon}}{(e^{\beta\epsilon}+1)^2}
$$

This is the **Schottky anomaly** — $C_V$ has a maximum at $k_BT \approx 0.42\epsilon$ and vanishes at both $T\to 0$ and $T\to\infty$.

### Example 5.6.E2 — Harmonic Oscillator: Average Energy

$$
\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln z}{\partial\beta} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1}
$$

At high $T$ ($\beta\hbar\omega \ll 1$): $e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1 \approx \beta\hbar\omega$, so $\langle E\rangle \approx \hbar\omega/2 + k_BT \approx k_BT$ (equipartition: $\frac{1}{2}k_BT$ kinetic + $\frac{1}{2}k_BT$ potential).

At low $T$ ($\beta\hbar\omega \gg 1$): $\langle E\rangle \approx \hbar\omega/2$ (zero-point energy only).

### Example 5.6.E3 — Ideal Gas Entropy (Sackur-Tetrode from $Z$)

$$
S = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\bigg|_V = Nk_B\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + \frac{5}{2}\right]
$$

Using $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$, so $\lambda^3 \propto T^{-3/2}$:

$$
S = Nk_B\left[\ln V - \ln N + \frac{3}{2}\ln T + \frac{3}{2}\ln\frac{2\pi mk_B}{h^2} + \frac{5}{2}\right]
$$

This matches the Sackur-Tetrode equation derived microcanonically in [[5.5 - Classical Statistical Mechanics - Microstates & Ensembles]]. $\checkmark$

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Partition Function for a Two-State Paramagnet

**Problem.** A system of $N$ non-interacting spin-1/2 particles in a magnetic field $B$ has two energy levels per particle: $\epsilon_\uparrow = -\mu B$ (aligned) and $\epsilon_\downarrow = +\mu B$ (anti-aligned), where $\mu$ is the magnetic moment. (a) Compute the single-particle partition function $z$. (b) Compute $Z_N$, $\langle E\rangle$, $C_V$, and the magnetization $M$. (c) Analyze the high-$T$ and low-$T$ limits.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Part (a): Single-Particle Partition Function

$$
z = \sum_{\text{states}} e^{-\beta\epsilon_s} = e^{-\beta(-\mu B)} + e^{-\beta(+\mu B)} = e^{\beta\mu B} + e^{-\beta\mu B}
$$

$$
\boxed{z = 2\cosh(\beta\mu B)}
$$

where $\beta = 1/(k_BT)$.

#### Part (b): N-Particle Thermodynamics

Since the particles are non-interacting and distinguishable (localized on lattice sites):

$$
Z_N = z^N = [2\cosh(\beta\mu B)]^N
$$

**Free energy:**

$$
F = -k_BT\ln Z_N = -Nk_BT\ln[2\cosh(\beta\mu B)]
$$

**Average energy:**

$$
\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z_N}{\partial\beta} = -N\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln[2\cosh(\beta\mu B)]
$$

$$
= -N\frac{\mu B\sinh(\beta\mu B)}{\cosh(\beta\mu B)} = -N\mu B\tanh(\beta\mu B)
$$

$$
\boxed{\langle E\rangle = -N\mu B\tanh\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)}
$$

**Heat capacity:**

$$
C_V = \frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T} = -N\mu B\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\tanh\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)
$$

Let $x = \mu B/(k_BT) = \beta\mu B$. Then $\partial x/\partial T = -\mu B/(k_BT^2) = -x/T$.

$$
C_V = -N\mu B \cdot \text{sech}^2(x) \cdot \left(-\frac{\mu B}{k_BT^2}\right) = \frac{N(\mu B)^2}{k_BT^2}\text{sech}^2\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)
$$

$$
\boxed{C_V = Nk_B\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)^2\text{sech}^2\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right) = Nk_Bx^2\text{sech}^2(x)}
$$

This is the **Schottky anomaly** — a peaked heat capacity characteristic of two-level systems.

**Magnetization:**

$$
M = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial B}\right)_T = N\mu\tanh\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)
$$

$$
\boxed{M = N\mu\tanh\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)}
$$

#### Part (c): Limiting Cases

**High temperature** ($k_BT \gg \mu B$, i.e., $x \ll 1$): $\tanh x \approx x$:

$$
M \approx \frac{N\mu^2 B}{k_BT} \quad \text{(Curie's law: } M \propto B/T\text{)}
$$

$$
C_V \approx Nk_B x^2(1 - x^2) \approx Nk_B\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right)^2 \to 0
$$

The spins are randomly oriented; the system is paramagnetic with susceptibility $\chi = N\mu^2/(k_BT)$.

**Low temperature** ($k_BT \ll \mu B$, i.e., $x \gg 1$): $\tanh x \approx 1 - 2e^{-2x}$:

$$
M \approx N\mu(1 - 2e^{-2\mu B/(k_BT)}) \to N\mu
$$

$$
C_V \approx Nk_B x^2 \cdot 4e^{-2x} \to 0 \quad \text{(exponentially)}
$$

All spins are aligned; the system is fully magnetized. The heat capacity vanishes exponentially (frozen out).

**Peak of $C_V$:** The Schottky peak occurs at $k_BT \approx 0.42\,\mu B$ (found by setting $dC_V/dT = 0$).

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Quantum Harmonic Oscillator Partition Function

**Problem.** For a single quantum harmonic oscillator with energy levels $\epsilon_n = (n + 1/2)\hbar\omega$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$: (a) Compute the partition function $z$. (b) Find $\langle E\rangle$ and $C_V$. (c) Show the classical limit recovers equipartition.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Part (a): Partition Function

$$
z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta\epsilon_n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta(n+1/2)\hbar\omega} = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-n\beta\hbar\omega}
$$

The sum is a geometric series with ratio $r = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega} \lt  1$:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} r^n = \frac{1}{1-r} = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}
$$

$$
\boxed{z = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} = \frac{1}{2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}}
$$

#### Part (b): Average Energy and Heat Capacity

$$
\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln z}{\partial\beta}
$$

From $\ln z = -\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2} - \ln(1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})$:

$$
\frac{\partial\ln z}{\partial\beta} = -\frac{\hbar\omega}{2} - \frac{\hbar\omega e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} = -\frac{\hbar\omega}{2} - \frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}
$$

$$
\boxed{\langle E\rangle = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \hbar\omega\,\bar{n}}
$$

where $\bar{n} = 1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$ is the Planck (Bose-Einstein) occupation number.

**Heat capacity:** Let $\Theta = \hbar\omega/k_B$ (the "Einstein temperature"):

$$
C_V = \frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T} = k_B\left(\frac{\Theta}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{\Theta/T}}{(e^{\Theta/T} - 1)^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{C_V = k_B\frac{(\beta\hbar\omega)^2 e^{\beta\hbar\omega}}{(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)^2}}
$$

#### Part (c): Classical Limit ($k_BT \gg \hbar\omega$)

When $\beta\hbar\omega \ll 1$: $e^{\beta\hbar\omega} \approx 1 + \beta\hbar\omega + (\beta\hbar\omega)^2/2$:

$$
\langle E\rangle \approx \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega}{\beta\hbar\omega} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + k_BT \approx k_BT
$$

(The zero-point energy $\hbar\omega/2$ is negligible compared to $k_BT$.)

$$
C_V \approx k_B
$$

This is the equipartition result: each quadratic degree of freedom (one kinetic + one potential for the oscillator) contributes $\frac{1}{2}k_BT$ to the energy, giving $\langle E\rangle = k_BT$ and $C_V = k_B$ per oscillator.

**Low-$T$ limit** ($\beta\hbar\omega \gg 1$): $C_V \approx k_B(\beta\hbar\omega)^2 e^{-\beta\hbar\omega} \to 0$ exponentially. The oscillator "freezes out" — quantum mechanics prevents excitation when $k_BT \ll \hbar\omega$.

</details>

### Example 8.3 — Einstein Solid: Heat Capacity of a Crystal

**Problem.** In the Einstein model, a solid of $N$ atoms is treated as $3N$ independent quantum harmonic oscillators, all with the same frequency $\omega_E$. (a) Write the partition function. (b) Derive the heat capacity. (c) Compare with the Debye model at low $T$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Part (a): Partition Function

Since the $3N$ oscillators are independent and distinguishable (each is localized at a lattice site):

$$
Z = z^{3N} = \left[\frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E}}\right]^{3N}
$$

$$
\ln Z = 3N\left[-\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_E}{2} - \ln(1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E})\right]
$$

#### Part (b): Heat Capacity

The total energy is $3N$ times the single-oscillator energy:

$$
\langle E\rangle = 3N\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_E}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega_E}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_E} - 1}\right]
$$

Define the Einstein temperature $\Theta_E = \hbar\omega_E/k_B$:

$$
C_V = 3Nk_B\left(\frac{\Theta_E}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{\Theta_E/T}}{(e^{\Theta_E/T} - 1)^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{C_V = 3Nk_B\,f_E(T/\Theta_E)}
$$

where $f_E(x) = (1/x)^2 e^{1/x}/(e^{1/x} - 1)^2$ is the Einstein function.

**Limits:**
- $T \gg \Theta_E$: $C_V \to 3Nk_B = 3nR$ (Dulong-Petit law). ✓
- $T \ll \Theta_E$: $C_V \approx 3Nk_B(\Theta_E/T)^2 e^{-\Theta_E/T} \to 0$ exponentially.

#### Part (c): Comparison with Debye Model

**Einstein model failure at low $T$:** Experiments show $C_V \propto T^3$ at low temperatures, but the Einstein model gives exponential decay. The problem: all oscillators have the same frequency, so they all freeze out simultaneously.

**Debye model improvement:** Debye treats the solid as a continuum with a spectrum of frequencies up to a cutoff $\omega_D$ (Debye frequency). The density of states is $g(\omega) \propto \omega^2$ (from the 3D density of states for phonons).

The Debye heat capacity:

$$
C_V = 9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2}\,dx
$$

At low $T$ ($T \ll \Theta_D$), the upper limit $\to \infty$ and the integral becomes a constant ($4\pi^4/15$):

$$
C_V = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3 \propto T^3 \quad \text{(Debye } T^3 \text{ law)}
$$

This matches experiment beautifully. The $T^3$ behavior arises because at low $T$, only long-wavelength (low-frequency) phonons are excited, and their density of states $\propto \omega^2$ gives the cubic temperature dependence.

**Typical values:** $\Theta_E \approx \Theta_D$ for most solids. For diamond: $\Theta_D = 2230\,\text{K}$ (very stiff bonds), so quantum effects persist to high temperatures. For lead: $\Theta_D = 105\,\text{K}$ (soft), so it reaches the classical limit near room temperature.

</details>

### Example 8.4 — Free Energy and Equation of State from $Z$

**Problem.** For an ideal gas of $N$ indistinguishable particles, the canonical partition function is $Z_N = z^N/N!$ where $z = V/\lambda^3$ and $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$. Derive the Helmholtz free energy, pressure, entropy, and chemical potential directly from $Z_N$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Free Energy

$$
F = -k_BT\ln Z_N = -k_BT\ln\frac{z^N}{N!} = -k_BT[N\ln z - \ln N!]
$$

Using Stirling ($\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$):

$$
F = -k_BT[N\ln z - N\ln N + N] = -Nk_BT[\ln z - \ln N + 1]
$$

$$
= -Nk_BT\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + 1\right]
$$

$$
\boxed{F = -Nk_BT\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + 1\right]}
$$

#### Step 2: Pressure

$$
P = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N} = Nk_BT \cdot \frac{1}{V}
$$

$$
\boxed{PV = Nk_BT} \quad \text{(ideal gas law)}
$$

#### Step 3: Entropy

$$
S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}
$$

Since $\lambda \propto T^{-1/2}$, we have $\lambda^3 \propto T^{-3/2}$, so $\ln(V/(N\lambda^3)) = \ln(V/N) + \frac{3}{2}\ln T + \text{const}$.

$$
S = Nk_B\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + 1\right] + Nk_BT \cdot \frac{3}{2T} = Nk_B\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + \frac{5}{2}\right]
$$

$$
\boxed{S = Nk_B\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + \frac{5}{2}\right]} \quad \text{(Sackur-Tetrode)}
$$

#### Step 4: Chemical Potential

$$
\mu = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V} = -k_BT\frac{\partial}{\partial N}[N\ln(V/(N\lambda^3)) + N]
$$

$$
= -k_BT\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + N\cdot\frac{-1}{N} + 1\right] = -k_BT\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mu = -k_BT\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)}
$$

where $n = N/V$ is the number density. Note $\mu \lt  0$ for a classical ideal gas (since $n\lambda^3 \ll 1$ in the classical regime). The chemical potential becomes zero when $n\lambda^3 = 1$ — this is the quantum degeneracy condition, signaling the breakdown of classical statistics.

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### Appendix 9.1 — Derivation of $\langle E \rangle = -\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$

This is the most important computational identity in statistical mechanics. We derive it from first principles and show its generalizations.

**Setup.** The canonical partition function is:

$$
Z = \sum_s e^{-\beta E_s}
$$

where the sum runs over all microstates $s$ with energy $E_s$, and $\beta = 1/(k_BT)$.

**Step 1: Differentiate $Z$ with respect to $\beta$.**

$$
\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\sum_s e^{-\beta E_s} = \sum_s (-E_s)e^{-\beta E_s} = -\sum_s E_s e^{-\beta E_s}
$$

**Step 2: Recognize the ensemble average.**

The probability of microstate $s$ is $P_s = e^{-\beta E_s}/Z$. Therefore:

$$
\langle E\rangle = \sum_s E_s P_s = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_s E_s e^{-\beta E_s} = -\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}
$$

**Step 3: Use the logarithmic derivative identity.**

$$
\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta} = \frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}}
$$

**Generalization: Higher moments and fluctuations.**

**Second moment:**

$$
\frac{\partial^2 Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \sum_s E_s^2 e^{-\beta E_s} = Z\langle E^2\rangle
$$

**Variance (energy fluctuations):**

$$
\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\right) = \frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial^2 Z}{\partial\beta^2} - \left(\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\right)^2
$$

$$
= \langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 = \langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle
$$

$$
\boxed{\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle = \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = -\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial\beta} = k_BT^2 C_V}
$$

This connects energy fluctuations to the heat capacity — a deep result known as the **fluctuation-dissipation theorem** (in its simplest form).

**The generating function perspective.** Define $Z(\beta)$ as the moment-generating function for the energy distribution. Then:

$$
\langle E^n\rangle = \frac{(-1)^n}{Z}\frac{\partial^n Z}{\partial\beta^n}
$$

The cumulant-generating function is $\ln Z(\beta)$:
- First cumulant: $\kappa_1 = -\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta = \langle E\rangle$
- Second cumulant: $\kappa_2 = \partial^2\ln Z/\partial\beta^2 = \langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle$
- Third cumulant: $\kappa_3 = -\partial^3\ln Z/\partial\beta^3 = \langle(\Delta E)^3\rangle$ (skewness)

For large $N$, the central limit theorem ensures the energy distribution approaches a Gaussian, so all cumulants beyond the second become negligible relative to $\langle E\rangle$.

**Reference:** Kittel & Kroemer, *Thermal Physics*, Ch. 3; Pathria & Beale, *Statistical Mechanics*, Ch. 3; David Tong, *Statistical Mechanics* Notes §4.

### Appendix 9.2 — The Free Energy as a Variational Principle

The Helmholtz free energy $F = -k_BT\ln Z$ has a beautiful variational interpretation that connects statistical mechanics to information theory.

**The Gibbs variational principle.** Among all probability distributions $\{P_s\}$ over microstates (normalized: $\sum_s P_s = 1$), the canonical distribution $P_s = e^{-\beta E_s}/Z$ uniquely minimizes the **free energy functional**:

$$
\mathcal{F}[P] = \langle E\rangle_P - TS[P] = \sum_s P_s E_s + k_BT\sum_s P_s\ln P_s
$$

**Proof.** Let $P_s^{\text{eq}} = e^{-\beta E_s}/Z$ be the equilibrium (canonical) distribution. For any other distribution $P_s$:

$$
\mathcal{F}[P] - \mathcal{F}[P^{\text{eq}}] = \sum_s P_s E_s + k_BT\sum_s P_s\ln P_s - F
$$

$$
= \sum_s P_s(E_s + k_BT\ln P_s) - (-k_BT\ln Z)
$$

$$
= k_BT\sum_s P_s(\beta E_s + \ln P_s + \ln Z)
$$

$$
= k_BT\sum_s P_s\ln\frac{P_s}{e^{-\beta E_s}/Z} = k_BT\sum_s P_s\ln\frac{P_s}{P_s^{\text{eq}}}
$$

$$
= k_BT\,D_{\text{KL}}(P \| P^{\text{eq}}) \geq 0
$$

where $D_{\text{KL}}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which is always non-negative (Gibbs' inequality). Equality holds if and only if $P_s = P_s^{\text{eq}}$ for all $s$.

**Physical meaning:** The equilibrium state minimizes the free energy — this is the statistical mechanical derivation of the thermodynamic minimum principle for $F$ at constant $T, V$.

**Connection to information theory:** The entropy $S = -k_B\sum P_s\ln P_s$ is (up to a constant) the Shannon information entropy. The canonical distribution maximizes entropy subject to the constraint $\langle E\rangle = U$ (fixed average energy). This is Jaynes' maximum entropy principle — statistical mechanics as inference.

**Reference:** Jaynes, *Information Theory and Statistical Mechanics* (1957); Susskind, *Statistical Mechanics* Lecture 4; Pathria & Beale, Ch. 3.
