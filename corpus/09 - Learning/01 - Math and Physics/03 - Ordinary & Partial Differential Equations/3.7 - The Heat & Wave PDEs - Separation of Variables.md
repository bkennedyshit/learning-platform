# 3.7 — The Heat & Wave PDEs: Separation of Variables

> *"The profound study of nature is the most fertile source of mathematical discoveries."* — Joseph Fourier, *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* (1822)

Partial differential equations govern phenomena where the unknown depends on multiple independent variables — typically space and time. The **heat equation** $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$ models diffusion and thermal conduction; the **wave equation** $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$ models vibrating strings, sound, and electromagnetic waves. The method of **separation of variables** reduces these PDEs to ODEs, whose solutions combine via Fourier series ([[3.5 - Fourier Series & Boundary Value Problems]]) to satisfy boundary and initial conditions.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

1. Classify PDEs as parabolic (heat), hyperbolic (wave), or elliptic (Laplace).
2. Apply separation of variables: assume $u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)$ and derive two ODEs.
3. Solve the 1D heat equation with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions.
4. Solve the 1D wave equation (d'Alembert's formula and Fourier method).
5. Determine Fourier coefficients from initial conditions.
6. Interpret eigenvalues as spatial frequencies and temporal decay/oscillation rates.
7. Understand the physical meaning of the diffusivity $\alpha^2$ and wave speed $c$.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 3.7.1 — The Heat Equation (Diffusion Equation)

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}, \quad 0 < x < L,\; t > 0,
$$

where $\alpha^2 > 0$ is the **thermal diffusivity** (units: length²/time).

### Definition 3.7.2 — The Wave Equation

$$
\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}, \quad 0 < x < L,\; t > 0,
$$

where $c > 0$ is the **wave propagation speed**.

### Definition 3.7.3 — Boundary Conditions

- **Dirichlet:** $u(0,t) = 0$, $u(L,t) = 0$ (fixed endpoints).
- **Neumann:** $u_x(0,t) = 0$, $u_x(L,t) = 0$ (insulated endpoints).
- **Mixed/Robin:** $\alpha u + \beta u_x = 0$ at boundary.

### Definition 3.7.4 — PDE Classification (2nd Order)

For $Au_{xx} + 2Bu_{xy} + Cu_{yy} + \text{lower} = 0$, the discriminant $B^2 - AC$ determines type:
- **Parabolic** ($B^2 - AC = 0$): heat equation.
- **Hyperbolic** ($B^2 - AC > 0$): wave equation.
- **Elliptic** ($B^2 - AC < 0$): Laplace/Poisson equation.

---

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

**Postulate 3.7.P1 (Fourier's Law):** Heat flux is proportional to the negative temperature gradient: $q = -k\,\partial u/\partial x$. Combined with conservation of energy, this yields the heat equation.

**Postulate 3.7.P2 (Superposition for Linear PDEs):** If $u_1$ and $u_2$ solve a linear homogeneous PDE with the same boundary conditions, so does $c_1 u_1 + c_2 u_2$ (and infinite sums, under convergence conditions).

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 3.7.1 — Separation of Variables Ansatz

Assuming $u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)$ in $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$ yields:

$$
\frac{T'(t)}{\alpha^2 T(t)} = \frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} = -\lambda \quad (\text{separation constant}).
$$

This splits into two ODEs: $X'' + \lambda X = 0$ and $T' + \alpha^2\lambda T = 0$.

**Proof.** Substitute $u = XT$ into $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$: $XT' = \alpha^2 X''T$. Divide by $\alpha^2 XT$: $T'/(\alpha^2 T) = X''/X$. The left side depends only on $t$, the right only on $x$; both must equal a constant $-\lambda$. $\blacksquare$

---

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 3.7.1 — Solution of the 1D Heat Equation (Dirichlet BCs)

For $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$ on $[0,L]$ with $u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0$ and $u(x,0) = f(x)$:

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\,e^{-\alpha^2 n^2\pi^2 t/L^2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},
$$

where $B_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx$ (Fourier sine coefficients of $f$).

### Theorem 3.7.2 — Solution of the 1D Wave Equation (Dirichlet BCs)

For $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$ on $[0,L]$ with $u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0$, $u(x,0) = f(x)$, $u_t(x,0) = g(x)$:

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[A_n\cos\frac{n\pi ct}{L} + B_n\sin\frac{n\pi ct}{L}\right]\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},
$$

where $A_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx$ and $B_n = \frac{2}{n\pi c}\int_0^L g(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx$.

### Theorem 3.7.3 — d'Alembert's Solution (Infinite String)

For $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$ on $-\infty < x < \infty$ with $u(x,0) = f(x)$, $u_t(x,0) = g(x)$:

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}[f(x+ct) + f(x-ct)] + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds.
$$

---

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Full Derivation: Heat Equation Solution (Theorem 3.7.1)

**Step 1.** Assume $u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)$. Substitute into $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$:

$$
X(x)T'(t) = \alpha^2 X''(x)T(t) \implies \frac{T'}{\alpha^2 T} = \frac{X''}{X} = -\lambda.
$$

**Step 2.** Spatial ODE: $X'' + \lambda X = 0$ with $X(0) = 0$, $X(L) = 0$.

**Step 3.** For $\lambda > 0$ (write $\lambda = \mu^2$): $X = A\cos\mu x + B\sin\mu x$.
- $X(0) = 0 \Rightarrow A = 0$.
- $X(L) = B\sin\mu L = 0$. For $B \neq 0$: $\mu L = n\pi$, so $\mu_n = n\pi/L$, $\lambda_n = n^2\pi^2/L^2$.

**Step 4.** Eigenfunctions: $X_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L)$.

**Step 5.** Temporal ODE: $T' + \alpha^2\lambda_n T = 0 \Rightarrow T_n(t) = e^{-\alpha^2 n^2\pi^2 t/L^2}$.

**Step 6.** General solution by superposition:

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n\,e^{-\alpha^2 n^2\pi^2 t/L^2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}.
$$

**Step 7.** Apply initial condition $u(x,0) = f(x)$:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}.
$$

By Fourier sine series ([[3.5 - Fourier Series & Boundary Value Problems]]):

$$
B_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx. \quad \blacksquare
$$

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 3.7.E1 — Heat Equation with Simple Initial Condition

**Solve:** $u_t = u_{xx}$, $u(0,t) = u(\pi,t) = 0$, $u(x,0) = 3\sin 2x - 5\sin 4x$.

**Step 1.** Here $\alpha^2 = 1$, $L = \pi$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_n = n^2$.

**Step 2.** The initial condition is already in Fourier sine form: $B_2 = 3$, $B_4 = -5$, all others zero.

**Step 3.** Solution:

$$
u(x,t) = 3e^{-4t}\sin 2x - 5e^{-16t}\sin 4x.
$$

The $n=4$ mode decays 4 times faster than the $n=2$ mode (higher frequencies diffuse faster).

---

### Example 3.7.E2 — Wave Equation

**Solve:** $u_{tt} = 4u_{xx}$, $u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0$, $u(x,0) = \sin(\pi x)$, $u_t(x,0) = 0$.

**Step 1.** $c = 2$, $L = 1$. Since $u_t(x,0) = 0$, all $B_n = 0$.

**Step 2.** $u(x,0) = \sin(\pi x)$ matches $n=1$: $A_1 = 1$, all others zero.

**Step 3.** Solution:

$$
u(x,t) = \cos(2\pi t)\sin(\pi x).
$$

This is a standing wave oscillating with frequency $\omega_1 = 2\pi$.

---

### Example 3.7.E3 — d'Alembert for Infinite String

**Solve:** $u_{tt} = 9u_{xx}$, $u(x,0) = e^{-x^2}$, $u_t(x,0) = 0$, $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Step 1.** $c = 3$. Since $g(x) = 0$, d'Alembert gives:

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}[e^{-(x+3t)^2} + e^{-(x-3t)^2}].
$$

Two Gaussian pulses propagating in opposite directions at speed 3.

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Heat Equation with Mixed Boundary Conditions

A rod of length $L = \pi$ has thermal diffusivity $\alpha^2 = 1$. The left end is held at $0°$C (Dirichlet), the right end is insulated (Neumann): $u(0,t) = 0$, $u_x(\pi, t) = 0$. Initial temperature: $u(x, 0) = x$.

Solve the heat equation $u_t = u_{xx}$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Separation of variables

Let $u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)$. Then $XT' = X''T$, so:

$$
\frac{T'}{T} = \frac{X''}{X} = -\lambda.
$$

#### Step 2: Solve the spatial eigenvalue problem

$$
X'' + \lambda X = 0, \quad X(0) = 0, \quad X'(\pi) = 0.
$$

From the Sturm–Liouville analysis (see §3.5, Example 8.3 with $L = \pi$):

$$
\lambda_n = \left(\frac{2n-1}{2}\right)^2, \quad X_n(x) = \sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$

#### Step 3: Solve the temporal equation

$$
T_n' = -\lambda_n T_n \implies T_n(t) = e^{-\lambda_n t} = e^{-(2n-1)^2 t/4}.
$$

#### Step 4: Form the general solution

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n \sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,e^{-(2n-1)^2 t/4}.
$$

#### Step 5: Determine coefficients from the initial condition

$$
u(x,0) = x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n \sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}.
$$

Using orthogonality on $[0, \pi]$ with weight 1:

$$
B_n = \frac{\int_0^{\pi} x\sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,dx}{\int_0^{\pi}\sin^2\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,dx}.
$$

The denominator: $\int_0^{\pi}\sin^2\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

The numerator: integrate by parts with $u = x$, $dv = \sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,dx$:

$$
\int_0^{\pi} x\sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{2x\cos\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}}{2n-1}\right]_0^{\pi} + \frac{2}{2n-1}\int_0^{\pi}\cos\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,dx.
$$

At $x = \pi$: $\cos\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2} = 0$ (since $(2n-1)\pi/2$ is an odd multiple of $\pi/2$). So the boundary term is 0.

The remaining integral: $\frac{2}{2n-1}\left[\frac{2\sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}}{2n-1}\right]_0^{\pi} = \frac{4}{(2n-1)^2}\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2} = \frac{4(-1)^{n+1}}{(2n-1)^2}$.

Therefore: $B_n = \frac{4(-1)^{n+1}}{(2n-1)^2} \cdot \frac{2}{\pi} = \frac{8(-1)^{n+1}}{\pi(2n-1)^2}$.

**Final Answer:**

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{8}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{(2n-1)^2}\sin\frac{(2n-1)x}{2}\,e^{-(2n-1)^2 t/4}.
$$

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Wave Equation: Plucked String (Triangular Initial Displacement)

A string of length $L = 1$ is fixed at both ends. It is plucked at the midpoint to height $h = 0.1$:

$$
u(x, 0) = \begin{cases} 2hx & 0 \leq x \leq 1/2, \\ 2h(1-x) & 1/2 \leq x \leq 1. \end{cases}
$$

No initial velocity: $u_t(x,0) = 0$. Wave speed $c = 1$. Solve $u_{tt} = u_{xx}$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Separation gives the standard eigenfunctions

With $u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0$:

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(A_n\cos(n\pi t) + B_n\sin(n\pi t)\right)\sin(n\pi x).
$$

#### Step 2: Apply $u_t(x,0) = 0$

$$
u_t(x,0) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n\pi B_n\sin(n\pi x) = 0 \implies B_n = 0 \text{ for all } n.
$$

#### Step 3: Determine $A_n$ from the initial shape

$$
A_n = 2\int_0^1 f(x)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx = 2\left[\int_0^{1/2}2hx\sin(n\pi x)\,dx + \int_{1/2}^1 2h(1-x)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx\right].
$$

By symmetry of the triangular function about $x = 1/2$, we can use the known result for the Fourier sine series of a triangle wave:

$$
A_n = \frac{8h}{\pi^2 n^2}\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}.
$$

This vanishes for even $n$ and alternates $\pm$ for odd $n$:

- $n = 1$: $A_1 = 8h/\pi^2$
- $n = 2$: $A_2 = 0$
- $n = 3$: $A_3 = -8h/(9\pi^2)$
- $n = 5$: $A_5 = 8h/(25\pi^2)$

#### Step 4: Write the solution

**Final Answer:**

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{8h}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)^2}\sin((2k+1)\pi x)\cos((2k+1)\pi t).
$$

With $h = 0.1$: $u(x,t) = \frac{0.8}{\pi^2}\left[\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi t) - \frac{1}{9}\sin(3\pi x)\cos(3\pi t) + \cdots\right]$.

Only odd harmonics are excited (the even harmonics have a node at the pluck point $x = 1/2$). The $1/n^2$ decay means the series converges rapidly — the string sounds "mellow" compared to a sawtooth initial shape ($1/n$ decay).

</details>

### Example 8.3 — 2D Heat Equation on a Rectangle (Steady-State)

Find the steady-state temperature $u(x,y)$ in a $2 \times 1$ rectangle with:
- $u(x,0) = 0$, $u(x,1) = 0$ (top and bottom at 0°C),
- $u(0,y) = 0$ (left at 0°C),
- $u(2,y) = \sin(\pi y)$ (right side heated).

The steady-state satisfies Laplace's equation: $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Separate variables $u(x,y) = X(x)Y(y)$

$$
\frac{X''}{X} = -\frac{Y''}{Y} = \lambda.
$$

#### Step 2: Solve the $Y$ equation (homogeneous BCs)

$Y'' + \lambda Y = 0$, $Y(0) = 0$, $Y(1) = 0$.

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_n = n^2\pi^2$, eigenfunctions: $Y_n = \sin(n\pi y)$.

#### Step 3: Solve the $X$ equation

$X'' - n^2\pi^2 X = 0 \implies X(x) = C_1\sinh(n\pi x) + C_2\cosh(n\pi x)$.

BC $u(0,y) = 0 \implies X(0) = 0 \implies C_2 = 0$.

So $X_n(x) = \sinh(n\pi x)$.

#### Step 4: General solution

$$
u(x,y) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\sinh(n\pi x)\sin(n\pi y).
$$

#### Step 5: Apply the right BC $u(2,y) = \sin(\pi y)$

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\sinh(2n\pi)\sin(n\pi y) = \sin(\pi y).
$$

By orthogonality, only $n = 1$ contributes:

$$
B_1\sinh(2\pi) = 1 \implies B_1 = \frac{1}{\sinh(2\pi)}.
$$

All other $B_n = 0$.

**Final Answer:**

$$
u(x,y) = \frac{\sinh(\pi x)}{\sinh(2\pi)}\sin(\pi y).
$$

**Verification:** $u_{xx} = \frac{\pi^2\sinh(\pi x)}{\sinh(2\pi)}\sin(\pi y)$, $u_{yy} = \frac{-\pi^2\sinh(\pi x)}{\sinh(2\pi)}\sin(\pi y)$. Sum = 0. ✓

</details>

### Example 8.4 — Wave Equation with Initial Velocity (Struck String)

A string of length $\pi$ is at rest ($u(x,0) = 0$) but given an initial velocity $u_t(x,0) = \sin(3x)$. Wave speed $c = 2$. Find $u(x,t)$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: General solution with Dirichlet BCs

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(A_n\cos(2nt) + B_n\sin(2nt))\sin(nx).
$$

#### Step 2: Apply $u(x,0) = 0$

$\sum A_n\sin(nx) = 0 \implies A_n = 0$ for all $n$.

#### Step 3: Apply $u_t(x,0) = \sin(3x)$

$$
u_t(x,0) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2n\,B_n\sin(nx) = \sin(3x).
$$

By orthogonality: $2n\,B_n = 0$ for $n \neq 3$, and $2(3)B_3 = 1 \implies B_3 = 1/6$.

**Final Answer:**

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{6}\sin(6t)\sin(3x).
$$

The string vibrates in its third harmonic mode at frequency $\omega_3 = 6$ rad/s.

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 D'Alembert's Formula for the Infinite String

For the wave equation on the entire real line $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$, $-\infty < x < \infty$, with initial conditions $u(x,0) = f(x)$ and $u_t(x,0) = g(x)$, the solution is given by **d'Alembert's formula**:

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\left[f(x+ct) + f(x-ct)\right] + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct} g(s)\,ds.
$$

**Derivation.** Introduce characteristic coordinates $\xi = x + ct$, $\eta = x - ct$. Then:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\xi} + \frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial t} = c\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi} - c\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}.
$$

Computing second derivatives:

$$
u_{tt} = c^2(u_{\xi\xi} - 2u_{\xi\eta} + u_{\eta\eta}), \qquad u_{xx} = u_{\xi\xi} + 2u_{\xi\eta} + u_{\eta\eta}.
$$

The wave equation $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$ becomes:

$$
c^2(u_{\xi\xi} - 2u_{\xi\eta} + u_{\eta\eta}) = c^2(u_{\xi\xi} + 2u_{\xi\eta} + u_{\eta\eta}).
$$

Simplifying: $-4c^2 u_{\xi\eta} = 0$, i.e., $u_{\xi\eta} = 0$.

**Integrate:** $u_{\xi} = F'(\xi)$ for some function $F$, then $u = F(\xi) + G(\eta)$ for arbitrary $C^2$ functions $F, G$. In original coordinates:

$$
u(x,t) = F(x+ct) + G(x-ct).
$$

This is the **general solution**: a superposition of a right-traveling wave $G(x-ct)$ and a left-traveling wave $F(x+ct)$.

**Apply initial conditions:**

$u(x,0) = F(x) + G(x) = f(x)$. ... (i)

$u_t(x,0) = cF'(x) - cG'(x) = g(x)$. ... (ii)

From (ii): $F'(x) - G'(x) = g(x)/c$. Integrate: $F(x) - G(x) = \frac{1}{c}\int_0^x g(s)\,ds + K$.

Adding and subtracting with (i):

$$
F(x) = \frac{1}{2}f(x) + \frac{1}{2c}\int_0^x g(s)\,ds + \frac{K}{2},
$$

$$
G(x) = \frac{1}{2}f(x) - \frac{1}{2c}\int_0^x g(s)\,ds - \frac{K}{2}.
$$

Substituting back:

$$
u(x,t) = F(x+ct) + G(x-ct) = \frac{1}{2}[f(x+ct) + f(x-ct)] + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}g(s)\,ds.
$$

The constant $K$ cancels. This is d'Alembert's formula. $\blacksquare$

**Physical interpretation:** The initial displacement $f$ splits into two half-amplitude copies traveling in opposite directions at speed $c$. The initial velocity $g$ creates a spreading disturbance whose amplitude at point $x$ at time $t$ depends on the integral of $g$ over the "domain of dependence" $[x-ct, x+ct]$.

*Reference: Walter Strauss, Partial Differential Equations: An Introduction (Wiley, 2008), §2.1; MIT OCW 18.303, Lecture 5.*

### 9.2 Maximum Principle for the Heat Equation

The heat equation has a remarkable property: the maximum (and minimum) of the temperature occurs on the boundary of the space-time domain, never in the interior (unless the solution is constant).

**Theorem (Weak Maximum Principle).** Let $u(x,t)$ satisfy $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$ in the rectangle $R = \{(x,t): 0 \leq x \leq L,\, 0 \leq t \leq T\}$. Let $\Gamma$ denote the "parabolic boundary" (bottom + two sides): $\Gamma = \{t=0\} \cup \{x=0\} \cup \{x=L\}$ (i.e., all of $\partial R$ except the top edge $t = T$). Then:

$$
\max_{\bar{R}} u = \max_{\Gamma} u.
$$

**Physical meaning:** Heat cannot spontaneously concentrate — it always diffuses. If the initial and boundary temperatures are all $\leq M$, then $u(x,t) \leq M$ for all later times. This is the mathematical expression of the second law of thermodynamics for heat conduction.

**Proof sketch (for smooth solutions).** Suppose for contradiction that $u$ attains its maximum at an interior point $(x_0, t_0)$ with $0 < x_0 < L$ and $0 < t_0 \leq T$. At a maximum:
- $u_t(x_0, t_0) \geq 0$ (since $u$ is at its max at time $t_0$, it cannot be decreasing in time at that point — or if $t_0 < T$, then $u_t = 0$).
- $u_{xx}(x_0, t_0) \leq 0$ (second derivative test: at a spatial maximum, the function is concave).

But the heat equation requires $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$, so $u_t \leq 0$ at the maximum. Combined with $u_t \geq 0$, we get $u_t = 0$ and $u_{xx} = 0$. A more careful argument (perturbing $u$ by $\epsilon x^2$) shows this forces $u$ to be constant. (The rigorous proof uses the strong maximum principle of Hopf.)

**Consequences:**
1. **Uniqueness.** If $u_1, u_2$ both solve the heat equation with the same boundary and initial data, then $w = u_1 - u_2$ satisfies the heat equation with zero data. By the maximum principle, $\max w \leq 0$ and $\min w \geq 0$, so $w \equiv 0$.
2. **Stability.** Small changes in boundary/initial data produce small changes in the solution (continuous dependence on data).
3. **Smoothing.** The heat equation instantly smooths discontinuous initial data — for $t > 0$, $u$ is $C^{\infty}$ even if $u(x,0)$ has jumps.

*Reference: Evans, Partial Differential Equations (AMS, 2010), §2.3; Strauss, PDEs, §2.4.*

### 9.3 Energy Methods for the Wave Equation — Uniqueness and Conservation

Define the **total energy** of a vibrating string:

$$
E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L\left[u_t^2 + c^2 u_x^2\right]dx.
$$

The first term is kinetic energy (proportional to velocity squared); the second is potential energy (proportional to strain squared).

**Theorem (Energy Conservation).** If $u$ satisfies $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$ with homogeneous boundary conditions ($u(0,t) = u(L,t) = 0$ or $u_x(0,t) = u_x(L,t) = 0$), then $E(t) = E(0)$ for all $t$.

**Proof.** Differentiate $E(t)$:

$$
\frac{dE}{dt} = \int_0^L\left[u_t u_{tt} + c^2 u_x u_{xt}\right]dx.
$$

Substitute $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$:

$$
= \int_0^L\left[c^2 u_t u_{xx} + c^2 u_x u_{xt}\right]dx = c^2\int_0^L\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u_t u_x)\,dx = c^2\left[u_t u_x\right]_0^L.
$$

(The second equality uses the product rule: $u_t u_{xx} + u_x u_{xt} = \partial_x(u_t u_x)$.)

With Dirichlet BCs: $u(0,t) = 0 \implies u_t(0,t) = 0$, and similarly at $x = L$. So the boundary term vanishes:

$$
\frac{dE}{dt} = 0. \quad \blacksquare
$$

**Uniqueness from energy.** If $u_1, u_2$ are two solutions with the same initial and boundary data, let $w = u_1 - u_2$. Then $w$ satisfies the wave equation with zero initial data ($w(x,0) = 0$, $w_t(x,0) = 0$) and zero BCs. Its energy is $E(0) = 0$. By conservation, $E(t) = 0$ for all $t$, which forces $w_t = 0$ and $w_x = 0$ everywhere, hence $w \equiv 0$ (using $w(x,0) = 0$).

**Contrast with the heat equation:** The heat equation dissipates energy ($dE/dt \leq 0$), while the wave equation conserves it. This reflects the fundamental physical difference: heat conduction is irreversible (entropy increases), while wave propagation is reversible (time-symmetric).

*Reference: Strauss, Partial Differential Equations, §2.2; Evans, PDEs, §2.4.*

### 9.4 Derivation of the Heat Kernel (Fundamental Solution)

The **fundamental solution** (or Green's function) of the heat equation on the infinite line is the response to a point source of heat at the origin at $t = 0$:

$$
u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}, \quad u(x, 0) = \delta(x).
$$

**Derivation via Fourier transform.** Take the Fourier transform in $x$ (let $\hat{u}(k,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(x,t)e^{-ikx}\,dx$):

$$
\hat{u}_t = -\alpha^2 k^2 \hat{u}, \quad \hat{u}(k,0) = 1 \text{ (since } \mathcal{F}\{\delta\} = 1\text{)}.
$$

This is a first-order ODE in $t$:

$$
\hat{u}(k,t) = e^{-\alpha^2 k^2 t}.
$$

**Invert the Fourier transform.** We need $\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{e^{-\alpha^2 k^2 t}\}$. This is a Gaussian in $k$-space, and the inverse Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian:

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha^2 k^2 t}e^{ikx}\,dk.
$$

Complete the square in the exponent: $-\alpha^2 k^2 t + ikx = -\alpha^2 t(k - ix/(2\alpha^2 t))^2 - x^2/(4\alpha^2 t)$.

The integral becomes:

$$
u(x,t) = \frac{e^{-x^2/(4\alpha^2 t)}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha^2 t\,\xi^2}\,d\xi = \frac{e^{-x^2/(4\alpha^2 t)}}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\alpha\sqrt{t}}.
$$

**The heat kernel:**

$$
\Phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\alpha\sqrt{\pi t}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\alpha^2 t}\right), \quad t > 0.
$$

**Properties:**
1. $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(x,t)\,dx = 1$ for all $t > 0$ (total heat is conserved).
2. $\Phi(x,t) \to \delta(x)$ as $t \to 0^+$ (in the distributional sense).
3. $\Phi > 0$ everywhere for $t > 0$ — heat propagates instantaneously to all points (infinite speed of propagation, a non-physical artifact of the diffusion model).
4. The width of the Gaussian grows as $\sigma = \alpha\sqrt{2t}$ — the characteristic diffusion length.

**Solution for general initial data** (by superposition/convolution):

$$
u(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(x-y, t)\,f(y)\,dy = \frac{1}{2\alpha\sqrt{\pi t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)\,\exp\left(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4\alpha^2 t}\right)dy.
$$

This is the Weierstrass transform of $f$ — it smooths the initial data by convolution with a Gaussian of increasing width.

*Reference: Evans, Partial Differential Equations (AMS, 2010), §2.3; Strauss, PDEs, §2.4.*

### 9.5 Duhamel's Principle for Inhomogeneous PDEs

**Problem.** Solve the forced heat equation $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx} + F(x,t)$ with homogeneous BCs and zero initial data.

**Duhamel's principle** reduces this to a family of homogeneous problems. The idea: treat the forcing $F(x,t)$ as a continuous superposition of impulses, each applied at a different time $\tau$.

Let $w(x, t; \tau)$ solve the homogeneous heat equation for $t > \tau$ with initial condition $w(x, \tau; \tau) = F(x, \tau)$ and the same homogeneous BCs. Then:

$$
u(x,t) = \int_0^t w(x, t; \tau)\,d\tau.
$$

**For the heat equation on $[0, L]$ with Dirichlet BCs:** If the homogeneous solution with initial data $f(x)$ is $u(x,t) = \sum_n b_n e^{-\alpha^2 n^2\pi^2 t/L^2}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ where $b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx$, then Duhamel gives:

$$
u(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\int_0^t F_n(\tau)\,e^{-\alpha^2 n^2\pi^2(t-\tau)/L^2}\,d\tau\right)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},
$$

where $F_n(\tau) = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L F(x,\tau)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx$ are the Fourier sine coefficients of the source term.

This is the PDE analogue of the variation of parameters formula for ODEs — and indeed, each Fourier mode satisfies a first-order ODE $T_n' + \lambda_n T_n = F_n(t)$, solved by the integrating factor method.

*Reference: Haberman, Applied Partial Differential Equations (Pearson, 2013), §8.3; MIT OCW 18.303, Lecture 12.*

### 9.6 Finite Difference Methods — Numerical Verification of PDE Solutions

While analytical solutions via separation of variables are elegant, they apply only to simple geometries and linear equations. **Finite difference methods** provide numerical solutions for verification and for problems beyond analytical reach.

**The explicit scheme for the heat equation** $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$:

Discretize: $x_j = j\Delta x$, $t_n = n\Delta t$, $U_j^n \approx u(x_j, t_n)$.

$$
\frac{U_j^{n+1} - U_j^n}{\Delta t} = \alpha^2\frac{U_{j+1}^n - 2U_j^n + U_{j-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2}.
$$

Solving for $U_j^{n+1}$:

$$
U_j^{n+1} = rU_{j-1}^n + (1-2r)U_j^n + rU_{j+1}^n, \quad r = \frac{\alpha^2\Delta t}{(\Delta x)^2}.
$$

**Stability condition (von Neumann analysis):** The scheme is stable if and only if $r \leq 1/2$, i.e., $\Delta t \leq (\Delta x)^2/(2\alpha^2)$. This is the **CFL condition** for the heat equation — it restricts the time step to be proportional to $(\Delta x)^2$, making explicit schemes expensive for fine spatial grids.

**The implicit (Crank–Nicolson) scheme** averages the spatial operator at times $n$ and $n+1$:

$$
\frac{U_j^{n+1} - U_j^n}{\Delta t} = \frac{\alpha^2}{2}\left[\frac{U_{j+1}^{n+1} - 2U_j^{n+1} + U_{j-1}^{n+1}}{(\Delta x)^2} + \frac{U_{j+1}^n - 2U_j^n + U_{j-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2}\right].
$$

This is unconditionally stable (no restriction on $\Delta t/(\Delta x)^2$) and second-order accurate in both space and time: $O((\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta t)^2)$.

**Verification strategy:** Compute the analytical Fourier series solution (truncated to $N$ terms) and the numerical solution on the same grid. The difference should be $O((\Delta x)^2)$ for the Crank–Nicolson scheme — this confirms both the analytical formula and the numerical implementation.

*Reference: Strang, Computational Science and Engineering (Wellesley-Cambridge, 2007), §5.1; LeVeque, Finite Difference Methods for Ordinary and Partial Differential Equations (SIAM, 2007), Ch. 9.*

### 9.7 Classification of Second-Order Linear PDEs

Every second-order linear PDE in two variables $Au_{xx} + 2Bu_{xy} + Cu_{yy} + \text{lower order} = 0$ is classified by the discriminant $\Delta = B^2 - AC$:

| $\Delta$ | Type | Canonical Form | Physical Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\Delta > 0$ | **Hyperbolic** | $u_{\xi\eta} = \ldots$ (wave-like) | Wave equation $u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}$ |
| $\Delta = 0$ | **Parabolic** | $u_{\eta\eta} = \ldots$ (diffusion-like) | Heat equation $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$ |
| $\Delta < 0$ | **Elliptic** | $u_{\xi\xi} + u_{\eta\eta} = \ldots$ (steady-state) | Laplace equation $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$ |

**Key differences in behavior:**
- **Hyperbolic:** Finite speed of propagation, characteristics carry information, initial data on a non-characteristic curve determines the solution (Cauchy problem well-posed).
- **Parabolic:** Infinite speed of propagation, smoothing of initial data, maximum principle holds.
- **Elliptic:** No time evolution, boundary value problems (not initial value), solutions are as smooth as the data allows (elliptic regularity).

The classification determines which boundary/initial conditions make the problem well-posed and which solution methods apply. Separation of variables works for all three types on simple domains; the method of characteristics is specific to hyperbolic equations.

*Reference: Strauss, Partial Differential Equations (Wiley, 2008), §1.3; Evans, PDEs, §2.1.*
