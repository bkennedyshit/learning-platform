# 12.1 — Statics & Equilibrium

> *"The whole of statics reduces to two vector equations: the sum of forces is zero, and the sum of moments about any point is zero."*
> — **R.C. Hibbeler**, *Engineering Mechanics: Statics*

A structure or machine component at rest (or in uniform motion) satisfies the conditions of **static equilibrium**. This chapter builds the complete machinery for analyzing forces, moments, and support reactions in rigid bodies — the essential prerequisite for all stress and deformation analysis that follows.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. State and apply Newton's Laws in the context of rigid-body statics.
2. Construct complete **free-body diagrams** (FBDs) isolating all external forces and reactions.
3. Classify support types (pin, roller, fixed) and identify their reaction components.
4. Write and solve the **six scalar equilibrium equations** (3D) or **three equations** (2D).
5. Compute **moments** (torques) about arbitrary points using the cross product $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$.
6. Analyze **trusses** (method of joints, method of sections) and **frames/machines**.
7. Determine **statical determinacy** vs. indeterminacy.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 12.1.1 — Rigid Body

A **rigid body** is an idealized solid in which the distance between any two particles remains constant under all loading conditions. No deformation occurs. This is the foundational assumption of statics; deformation analysis (Chapters 12.2–12.7) relaxes this assumption.

### Definition 12.1.2 — Force

A **force** $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector quantity characterized by:
- **Magnitude** $|\mathbf{F}|$ (in Newtons, N, or pounds, lb)
- **Direction** (line of action and sense)
- **Point of application**

In component form for 3D:

$$
\mathbf{F} = F_x\,\hat{\mathbf{i}} + F_y\,\hat{\mathbf{j}} + F_z\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

### Definition 12.1.3 — Moment (Torque) About a Point

The **moment** of force $\mathbf{F}$ about point $O$ is:

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}
$$

where $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector from $O$ to any point on the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$.

**Scalar form (2D):** For a force in the $xy$-plane, the moment about $O$ has only a $z$-component:

$$
M_O = r_x F_y - r_y F_x = |\mathbf{r}||\mathbf{F}|\sin\theta = F \cdot d
$$

where $d$ is the **perpendicular distance** (moment arm) from $O$ to the line of action.

### Definition 12.1.4 — Couple

A **couple** is a pair of forces equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and separated by a perpendicular distance $d$. The couple moment is:

$$
\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = Fd
$$

A couple is a **free vector** — its moment is the same about every point in space.

### Definition 12.1.5 — Resultant Force and Moment

The **resultant** of a system of forces $\{\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{F}_n\}$ is:

$$
\mathbf{F}_R = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{F}_i
$$

The **resultant moment** about point $O$:

$$
\mathbf{M}_R = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{F}_i
$$

### Definition 12.1.6 — Support Reactions

| Support Type | Reactions Provided | DOF Removed |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Roller** | 1 force (⊥ to surface) | 1 |
| **Pin (Hinge)** | 2 forces ($F_x$, $F_y$) | 2 |
| **Fixed (Cantilever)** | 2 forces + 1 moment ($F_x$, $F_y$, $M$) | 3 |
| **3D Ball-and-Socket** | 3 forces ($F_x$, $F_y$, $F_z$) | 3 |
| **3D Fixed** | 3 forces + 3 moments | 6 |

### Definition 12.1.7 — Statical Determinacy

A structure is **statically determinate** if the number of unknown reactions equals the number of independent equilibrium equations. For a 2D rigid body:

$$
\text{Unknowns} = 3 \implies \text{Determinate}
$$

$$
\text{Unknowns} > 3 \implies \text{Statically Indeterminate (degree } = n - 3\text{)}
$$

### Definition 12.1.8 — Distributed Load

A **distributed load** $w(x)$ (force per unit length, N/m) acts over a region. Its resultant:

$$
F_R = \int_a^b w(x)\,dx
$$

acts at the centroid of the loading diagram:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\int_a^b x\,w(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}
$$

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Postulate 12.1.P1 — Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia)

A body at rest remains at rest, and a body in uniform rectilinear motion remains in that state, unless acted upon by an unbalanced force. In statics, all bodies are in equilibrium (zero acceleration):

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}
$$

### Postulate 12.1.P2 — Newton's Third Law (Action-Reaction)

For every force exerted by body $A$ on body $B$, body $B$ exerts an equal and opposite force on body $A$:

$$
\mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA}
$$

This is the basis for isolating free bodies and identifying internal vs. external forces.

### Postulate 12.1.P3 — Principle of Transmissibility

A force may be moved along its line of action without changing its external effect on a rigid body. This does **not** hold for deformable bodies (where the point of application matters for internal stress distribution).

### Postulate 12.1.P4 — Parallelogram Law of Force Addition

Two forces acting at a point can be replaced by a single resultant force obtained by the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by the two force vectors:

$$
\mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2
$$

### Postulate 12.1.P5 — Equilibrium of a Rigid Body (3D)

A rigid body is in static equilibrium if and only if:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \quad \text{(translational equilibrium)}
$$

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0} \quad \text{(rotational equilibrium about any point } O\text{)}
$$

In scalar form (3D), this yields **six independent equations**:

$$
\sum F_x = 0, \quad \sum F_y = 0, \quad \sum F_z = 0
$$

$$
\sum M_x = 0, \quad \sum M_y = 0, \quad \sum M_z = 0
$$

In 2D (coplanar forces), this reduces to **three independent equations**:

$$
\sum F_x = 0, \quad \sum F_y = 0, \quad \sum M_O = 0
$$

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 12.1.1 — Moment of a Force via Cross Product (Component Form)

Given $\mathbf{r} = r_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + r_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + r_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ and $\mathbf{F} = F_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + F_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + F_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}$, the moment is:

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ r_x & r_y & r_z \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix}
$$

Expanding the determinant:

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = (r_y F_z - r_z F_y)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (r_x F_z - r_z F_x)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (r_x F_y - r_y F_x)\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

<details>
<summary>🔍 Proof of Determinant Expansion</summary>

We expand along the first row of the $3 \times 3$ determinant:

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = \hat{\mathbf{i}}\begin{vmatrix} r_y & r_z \\ F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix} - \hat{\mathbf{j}}\begin{vmatrix} r_x & r_z \\ F_x & F_z \end{vmatrix} + \hat{\mathbf{k}}\begin{vmatrix} r_x & r_y \\ F_x & F_y \end{vmatrix}
$$

Each $2\times 2$ determinant evaluates as $ad - bc$:

$$
= \hat{\mathbf{i}}(r_y F_z - r_z F_y) - \hat{\mathbf{j}}(r_x F_z - r_z F_x) + \hat{\mathbf{k}}(r_x F_y - r_y F_x)
$$

This is the standard cross-product formula. $\blacksquare$

</details>

### Lemma 12.1.2 — Varignon's Theorem (Principle of Moments)

The moment of a force about a point equals the sum of the moments of its components about that same point:

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times (F_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + F_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}) = \mathbf{r} \times F_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \mathbf{r} \times F_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}
$$

<details>
<summary>🔍 Proof</summary>

This follows directly from the distributive property of the cross product over vector addition:

$$
\mathbf{r} \times (\mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2) = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}_2
$$

Since $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \cdots + \mathbf{F}_n$ (component decomposition):

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{M}_{O,i}
$$

This is extremely useful in practice: instead of computing the moment arm $d$ for an oblique force, resolve the force into horizontal and vertical components and sum their individual moments. $\blacksquare$

</details>

### Lemma 12.1.3 — Equivalence of Moment Summation Point

If a system of forces is in equilibrium ($\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$), then the moment sum about **any** point is zero. That is, if $\sum \mathbf{M}_A = \mathbf{0}$ and $\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\sum \mathbf{M}_B = \mathbf{0}$ for any other point $B$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Proof</summary>

Let $\mathbf{r}_{AB}$ be the position vector from $A$ to $B$. For any force $\mathbf{F}_i$ applied at point $P_i$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_{B \to P_i} = \mathbf{r}_{A \to P_i} - \mathbf{r}_{A \to B}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_B = \sum \mathbf{r}_{B \to P_i} \times \mathbf{F}_i = \sum (\mathbf{r}_{A \to P_i} - \mathbf{r}_{AB}) \times \mathbf{F}_i
$$

$$
= \sum \mathbf{r}_{A \to P_i} \times \mathbf{F}_i - \mathbf{r}_{AB} \times \sum \mathbf{F}_i
$$

$$
= \sum \mathbf{M}_A - \mathbf{r}_{AB} \times \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} - \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}
$$

$\blacksquare$

</details>

### Lemma 12.1.4 — Two-Force Member

If a structural member is subjected to forces at only **two** points (and no couples), then for equilibrium the two forces must be:
1. Equal in magnitude
2. Opposite in direction
3. Collinear (directed along the line connecting the two points)

<details>
<summary>🔍 Proof</summary>

Let forces $\mathbf{F}_A$ and $\mathbf{F}_B$ act at points $A$ and $B$ respectively.

**Force equilibrium:** $\mathbf{F}_A + \mathbf{F}_B = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{F}_B = -\mathbf{F}_A$ (equal magnitude, opposite direction).

**Moment equilibrium about $A$:** $\mathbf{r}_{AB} \times \mathbf{F}_B = \mathbf{0}$.

Since $\mathbf{F}_B \neq \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{AB} \neq \mathbf{0}$, the cross product vanishes only if $\mathbf{F}_B$ is parallel to $\mathbf{r}_{AB}$. Therefore $\mathbf{F}_B$ acts along line $AB$. $\blacksquare$

</details>

### Lemma 12.1.5 — Three-Force Member

If a rigid body is in equilibrium under exactly **three** non-parallel forces, then their lines of action must be **concurrent** (intersect at a single point).

<details>
<summary>🔍 Proof</summary>

Let forces $\mathbf{F}_1$, $\mathbf{F}_2$, $\mathbf{F}_3$ act on the body. Suppose the lines of action of $\mathbf{F}_1$ and $\mathbf{F}_2$ intersect at point $P$.

Take moments about $P$:

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_P = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{r}_{P \to 3} \times \mathbf{F}_3 = \mathbf{0}
$$

(The first two moments vanish because $P$ lies on their lines of action.)

For $\mathbf{r}_{P \to 3} \times \mathbf{F}_3 = \mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{F}_3 \neq \mathbf{0}$, either $\mathbf{r}_{P \to 3} = \mathbf{0}$ (point of application is $P$) or $\mathbf{F}_3$ is parallel to $\mathbf{r}_{P \to 3}$ (line of action passes through $P$). Either way, the line of action of $\mathbf{F}_3$ passes through $P$. $\blacksquare$

</details>

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 12.1.1 — Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for Rigid-Body Equilibrium

A rigid body is in static equilibrium if and only if:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}
$$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{M}_{O,i} = \mathbf{0} \quad \text{for any point } O
$$

**Corollary (2D):** For coplanar force systems, the equilibrium conditions reduce to three scalar equations. Three alternative formulations exist:

| Form | Equations | Condition |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Standard | $\sum F_x = 0,\; \sum F_y = 0,\; \sum M_A = 0$ | — |
| Two-moment | $\sum F_x = 0,\; \sum M_A = 0,\; \sum M_B = 0$ | Line $AB$ not ⊥ to $x$-axis |
| Three-moment | $\sum M_A = 0,\; \sum M_B = 0,\; \sum M_C = 0$ | $A$, $B$, $C$ not collinear |

### Theorem 12.1.2 — Determinacy and Stability

For a 2D structure with $r$ unknown reactions and $n$ rigid bodies:

$$
\text{Available equations} = 3n
$$

- **Statically determinate:** $r = 3n$ (unique solution exists)
- **Statically indeterminate:** $r > 3n$ (degree of indeterminacy $= r - 3n$)
- **Unstable (mechanism):** $r < 3n$ or reactions are concurrent/parallel

### Theorem 12.1.3 — Method of Joints (Trusses)

At each joint of a truss in equilibrium, the sum of forces (including member forces and external loads) equals zero:

$$
\sum F_x = 0, \quad \sum F_y = 0 \quad \text{at each joint}
$$

For a simple truss with $m$ members, $j$ joints, and $r$ reactions:

$$
m + r = 2j \quad \text{(necessary condition for determinacy)}
$$

### Theorem 12.1.4 — Method of Sections (Trusses)

A truss may be cut by an imaginary section exposing at most **three** unknown member forces. The three equilibrium equations for the isolated portion determine these forces directly without solving joint-by-joint.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Derivation: Reactions of a Simply-Supported Beam Under Point Load

**Problem Setup:** A beam of length $L$ is supported by a pin at $A$ (left end) and a roller at $B$ (right end). A concentrated load $P$ acts downward at distance $a$ from $A$.

**Free-Body Diagram Unknowns:** $A_x$, $A_y$ (pin), $B_y$ (roller). Three unknowns, three equations — statically determinate.

**Step 1: Force equilibrium in $x$-direction.**

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad A_x = 0
$$

(No horizontal loads applied.)

**Step 2: Moment equilibrium about $A$.**

Taking counterclockwise as positive:

$$
\sum M_A = 0: \quad -P \cdot a + B_y \cdot L = 0
$$

Solving for $B_y$:

$$
B_y = \frac{Pa}{L}
$$

**Step 3: Force equilibrium in $y$-direction.**

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad A_y + B_y - P = 0
$$

$$
A_y = P - B_y = P - \frac{Pa}{L} = \frac{P(L-a)}{L} = \frac{Pb}{L}
$$

where $b = L - a$ is the distance from the load to support $B$.

**Verification:** Sum moments about $B$:

$$
\sum M_B = A_y \cdot L - P \cdot (L-a) = \frac{Pb}{L} \cdot L - P \cdot b = Pb - Pb = 0 \quad \checkmark
$$

### 5.2 Derivation: Resultant of a Uniform Distributed Load

A uniform distributed load $w$ (constant, N/m) acts over a beam segment from $x = 0$ to $x = L$.

**Resultant magnitude:**

$$
F_R = \int_0^L w\,dx = w \int_0^L dx = wL
$$

**Location of resultant (centroid):**

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\int_0^L x \cdot w\,dx}{\int_0^L w\,dx} = \frac{w \int_0^L x\,dx}{wL} = \frac{w \cdot \frac{L^2}{2}}{wL} = \frac{L}{2}
$$

The resultant of a uniform load acts at the **midpoint** of the loaded segment.

### 5.3 Derivation: Resultant of a Triangular Distributed Load

A linearly varying load increases from $0$ at $x = 0$ to $w_0$ at $x = L$: $w(x) = w_0 \frac{x}{L}$.

**Resultant magnitude:**

$$
F_R = \int_0^L w_0 \frac{x}{L}\,dx = \frac{w_0}{L} \int_0^L x\,dx = \frac{w_0}{L} \cdot \frac{L^2}{2} = \frac{w_0 L}{2}
$$

**Location of resultant:**

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\int_0^L x \cdot w_0 \frac{x}{L}\,dx}{\frac{w_0 L}{2}} = \frac{\frac{w_0}{L} \int_0^L x^2\,dx}{\frac{w_0 L}{2}} = \frac{\frac{w_0}{L} \cdot \frac{L^3}{3}}{\frac{w_0 L}{2}} = \frac{\frac{w_0 L^2}{3}}{\frac{w_0 L}{2}} = \frac{2L}{3}
$$

The resultant acts at $\frac{2}{3}L$ from the zero-load end (i.e., $\frac{1}{3}L$ from the maximum-load end).

### 5.4 Derivation: Method of Joints — Solving a Simple Truss

**Problem:** A symmetric triangular truss with span $L$, height $h$, loaded at the apex with force $P$ downward. Joints: $A$ (pin, left), $B$ (roller, right), $C$ (apex).

**Step 1: Global equilibrium.**

By symmetry: $A_y = B_y = P/2$, $A_x = 0$.

**Step 2: Joint $C$ (apex).**

Members $AC$ and $BC$ meet at $C$. Let $\alpha = \arctan(h/(L/2)) = \arctan(2h/L)$.

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad F_{AC}\sin\alpha + F_{BC}\sin\alpha - P = 0
$$

By symmetry $F_{AC} = F_{BC} = F$:

$$
2F\sin\alpha = P \implies F = \frac{P}{2\sin\alpha}
$$

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad -F_{AC}\cos\alpha + F_{BC}\cos\alpha = 0 \quad \checkmark \text{ (by symmetry)}
$$

**Step 3: Joint $A$.**

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad A_y + F_{AC}\sin\alpha = 0 \implies F_{AC} = -\frac{A_y}{\sin\alpha} = -\frac{P}{2\sin\alpha}
$$

The negative sign indicates **compression** (force pushes toward the joint).

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad F_{AB} + F_{AC}\cos\alpha = 0 \implies F_{AB} = \frac{P\cos\alpha}{2\sin\alpha} = \frac{P}{2\tan\alpha}
$$

Positive → **tension** in the bottom chord.

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 12.1.1 — Simply-Supported Beam with Two Point Loads

**Given:** A beam $AB$ of length $L = 6$ m is supported by a pin at $A$ and a roller at $B$. Two concentrated loads act: $P_1 = 8$ kN downward at $x = 2$ m from $A$, and $P_2 = 12$ kN downward at $x = 4$ m from $A$.

**Find:** All support reactions.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Free-body diagram.**

Unknowns: $A_x$, $A_y$ (pin at $A$), $B_y$ (roller at $B$).

**Step 2: $\sum F_x = 0$:**

$$
A_x = 0
$$

**Step 3: $\sum M_A = 0$ (counterclockwise positive):**

$$
-P_1(2) - P_2(4) + B_y(6) = 0
$$

$$
-(8)(2) - (12)(4) + 6B_y = 0
$$

$$
-16 - 48 + 6B_y = 0
$$

$$
6B_y = 64
$$

$$
B_y = \frac{64}{6} = 10.67 \text{ kN}
$$

**Step 4: $\sum F_y = 0$:**

$$
A_y + B_y - P_1 - P_2 = 0
$$

$$
A_y = 8 + 12 - 10.67 = 9.33 \text{ kN}
$$

**Verification:** $\sum M_B = A_y(6) - P_1(4) - P_2(2) = 9.33(6) - 8(4) - 12(2) = 56 - 32 - 24 = 0$ ✓

**Results:**

$$
A_x = 0, \quad A_y = 9.33 \text{ kN} \uparrow, \quad B_y = 10.67 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

</details>

### Example 12.1.2 — Cantilever Beam with Distributed Load

**Given:** A cantilever beam (fixed at $A$, free at $B$) of length $L = 4$ m carries a uniform distributed load $w = 5$ kN/m over its entire length.

**Find:** The fixed-end reactions $A_x$, $A_y$, and $M_A$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Replace distributed load with resultant.**

$$
F_R = wL = 5 \times 4 = 20 \text{ kN (downward)}
$$

Acting at $\bar{x} = L/2 = 2$ m from $A$.

**Step 2: $\sum F_x = 0$:**

$$
A_x = 0
$$

**Step 3: $\sum F_y = 0$:**

$$
A_y - F_R = 0 \implies A_y = 20 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

**Step 4: $\sum M_A = 0$ (counterclockwise positive):**

$$
M_A - F_R \cdot \bar{x} = 0
$$

$$
M_A = 20 \times 2 = 40 \text{ kN·m (counterclockwise)}
$$

**Results:**

$$
A_x = 0, \quad A_y = 20 \text{ kN} \uparrow, \quad M_A = 40 \text{ kN·m} \circlearrowleft
$$

</details>

### Example 12.1.3 — Truss Analysis by Method of Joints

**Given:** A Pratt truss with 3 panels, each of width $a = 3$ m and height $h = 4$ m. A vertical load $P = 24$ kN is applied at the middle bottom joint. Pin support at left, roller at right.

**Find:** Forces in all members connected to the loaded joint.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Global equilibrium.**

Total span $= 3a = 9$ m. Load at $x = 3$ m from left support (middle of bottom chord, but actually at the second bottom joint at $x = 3$ m).

$$
\sum M_A = 0: \quad B_y(9) - 24(3) = 0 \implies B_y = 8 \text{ kN}
$$

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad A_y = 24 - 8 = 16 \text{ kN}
$$

**Step 2: Joint at loaded point (call it $D$, bottom, $x = 3$ m).**

Members meeting at $D$: horizontal bottom chord left ($F_{CD}$), horizontal bottom chord right ($F_{DE}$), and diagonal going up-right to top chord joint ($F_{DG}$).

Diagonal angle: $\theta = \arctan(4/3) = 53.13°$, so $\sin\theta = 4/5$, $\cos\theta = 3/5$.

Also a vertical member $F_{DF}$ going straight up to the top chord.

**$\sum F_y = 0$ at joint $D$:**

$$
-24 + F_{DF} + F_{DG}\sin\theta = 0
$$

We need another equation. Let's use method of sections instead for the vertical member.

**Method of Sections — cut through panel 1-2:**

Cut members: top chord ($F_{FG}$, horizontal), diagonal ($F_{DG}$), bottom chord ($F_{DE}$).

Take left portion. $\sum M_F = 0$ (moment about top-left joint of cut panel, eliminates $F_{FG}$):

$$
A_y(3) - 24(0) - F_{DE}(4) = 0
$$

Wait — let me reconsider the geometry. Let me label joints systematically.

Bottom joints (left to right): $A$, $C$, $D$, $B$ at $x = 0, 3, 6, 9$ m.
Top joints: $E$ at $x = 3$ m, $F$ at $x = 6$ m (height $= 4$ m).

Load $P = 24$ kN at joint $C$ ($x = 3$ m, bottom).

**Revised global equilibrium:**

$$
\sum M_A = 0: \quad B_y(9) - 24(3) = 0 \implies B_y = 8 \text{ kN}
$$

$$
A_y = 16 \text{ kN}
$$

**Joint $A$ (pin):**

Members: $AC$ (horizontal, bottom chord) and $AE$ (diagonal to top).

Angle of $AE$: $\theta = \arctan(4/3) = 53.13°$.

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad 16 + F_{AE}\sin 53.13° = 0
$$

$$
F_{AE} = \frac{-16}{0.8} = -20 \text{ kN (compression)}
$$

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad F_{AC} + F_{AE}\cos 53.13° = 0
$$

$$
F_{AC} = -(-20)(0.6) = 12 \text{ kN (tension)}
$$

**Joint $C$ (loaded joint):**

Members: $CA$ (left, bottom), $CD$ (right, bottom), $CE$ (vertical up).

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad -24 + F_{CE} = 0 \implies F_{CE} = 24 \text{ kN (tension)}
$$

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad -F_{AC} + F_{CD} = 0 \implies F_{CD} = 12 \text{ kN (tension)}
$$

**Results at loaded joint $C$:**

$$
F_{CA} = 12 \text{ kN (T)}, \quad F_{CD} = 12 \text{ kN (T)}, \quad F_{CE} = 24 \text{ kN (T)}
$$

</details>

### Example 12.1.4 — 3D Equilibrium: Bracket with Ball-and-Socket Joint

**Given:** A rigid bracket is supported by a ball-and-socket joint at $O$ (origin) and two cables. Cable $AB$ connects point $A = (0.3, 0, 0.4)$ m on the bracket to anchor $B = (0, 0.5, 0)$ m. A force $\mathbf{F} = -500\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ N acts at point $C = (0.6, 0, 0)$ m.

**Find:** The tension in cable $AB$ and the reaction at $O$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Unit vector along cable $AB$.**

$$
\mathbf{r}_{AB} = B - A = (0 - 0.3)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (0.5 - 0)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (0 - 0.4)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = -0.3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.5\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 0.4\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$$
|\mathbf{r}_{AB}| = \sqrt{0.09 + 0.25 + 0.16} = \sqrt{0.50} = 0.707 \text{ m}
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{AB} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_{AB}}{|\mathbf{r}_{AB}|} = \frac{-0.3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.5\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 0.4\hat{\mathbf{k}}}{0.707} = -0.424\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.707\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 0.566\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

Cable force: $\mathbf{T}_{AB} = T_{AB}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{AB}$.

**Step 2: Moment equilibrium about $O$.**

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r}_A \times \mathbf{T}_{AB} + \mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Compute $\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F}$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F} = (0.6\hat{\mathbf{i}}) \times (-500\hat{\mathbf{k}}) = 0.6(-500)(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{k}}) = -300(-\hat{\mathbf{j}}) = 300\hat{\mathbf{j}} \text{ N·m}
$$

Compute $\mathbf{r}_A \times \mathbf{T}_{AB}$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_A \times \mathbf{T}_{AB} = T_{AB} \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ 0.3 & 0 & 0.4 \\ -0.424 & 0.707 & -0.566 \end{vmatrix}
$$

$$
= T_{AB}[(0)(-0.566) - (0.4)(0.707)]\hat{\mathbf{i}} - T_{AB}[(0.3)(-0.566) - (0.4)(-0.424)]\hat{\mathbf{j}} + T_{AB}[(0.3)(0.707) - (0)(-0.424)]\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$$
= T_{AB}[-0.283\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (-0.170 + 0.170)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.212\hat{\mathbf{k}}]
$$

$$
= T_{AB}[-0.283\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.212\hat{\mathbf{k}}]
$$

From $\sum M_{O,y} = 0$:

$$
0 + 300 = 0 \quad ???
$$

This indicates we need a second cable or the problem is under-constrained. For a single-cable system, take $\sum M_{O,j}$ component:

Actually, with a ball-and-socket (3 reaction forces) and one cable (1 unknown), we have 4 unknowns and 6 equations — the system is under-constrained unless additional supports exist. Let's simplify: assume the bracket is a 2D problem in the $xz$-plane with the cable providing the $y$-component constraint.

Taking the $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ component of moment equation:

$$
T_{AB}(0.212) = 0 \implies \text{(not useful)}
$$

Taking the $\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ component:

$$
-0.283 T_{AB} = 0 \implies \text{(not useful either)}
$$

The $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ component gives $300 = 0$ which is inconsistent — confirming we need additional support. This demonstrates that **a single cable plus ball-and-socket cannot equilibrate an arbitrary 3D load**. The problem requires at least 2 cables or additional constraints.

**Key Lesson:** Always verify that the number of unknowns matches the available equilibrium equations before solving.

</details>

### Example 12.1.5 — Overhanging Beam with Mixed Loading

**Given:** Beam $AC$ with pin at $A$, roller at $B$ (at $x = 4$ m), and overhang to $C$ (at $x = 6$ m). A uniform load $w = 3$ kN/m acts from $A$ to $B$, and a concentrated load $P = 10$ kN acts at $C$.

**Find:** Support reactions.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Replace distributed load with resultant.**

$$
F_w = w \cdot L_{AB} = 3 \times 4 = 12 \text{ kN} \downarrow \quad \text{at } \bar{x} = 2 \text{ m from } A
$$

**Step 2: $\sum M_A = 0$:**

$$
-F_w(2) - P(6) + B_y(4) = 0
$$

$$
-12(2) - 10(6) + 4B_y = 0
$$

$$
-24 - 60 + 4B_y = 0
$$

$$
B_y = \frac{84}{4} = 21 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

**Step 3: $\sum F_y = 0$:**

$$
A_y + B_y - F_w - P = 0
$$

$$
A_y = 12 + 10 - 21 = 1 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

**Step 4: $\sum F_x = 0$:**

$$
A_x = 0
$$

**Verification:** $\sum M_B = A_y(4) - F_w(2) + P(2) = 1(4) - 12(2) + 10(2) = 4 - 24 + 20 = 0$ ✓

Wait: $F_w$ acts at 2 m from $A$, which is 2 m to the left of $B$. $P$ acts at 6 m from $A$, which is 2 m to the right of $B$.

$$
\sum M_B = A_y(4) - F_w(4-2) - P(6-4) = 1(4) - 12(2) - 10(2) = 4 - 24 - 20 = -40 \neq 0
$$

Let me recompute. Taking moments about $B$:

$$
\sum M_B = A_y(4) - F_w(4-2) - P(6-4)
$$

Hmm, signs: $A_y$ is upward at $A$, which is 4 m to the left of $B$. Its moment about $B$ is $A_y \times 4$ (counterclockwise). $F_w$ acts downward at 2 m from $A$ = 2 m to the left of $B$. Its moment about $B$ is $-F_w \times 2$ (clockwise). $P$ acts downward at $C$, 2 m to the right of $B$. Its moment about $B$ is $+P \times 2$ (counterclockwise? No — downward force to the right of $B$ creates clockwise moment).

Let me redo with consistent sign convention (CCW positive about $B$):

$$
\sum M_B = +A_y(4) + F_w(-2) + P(-2) = 0
$$

Wait, I need to be more careful. Force $F_w = 12$ kN downward at $x = 2$ m. About $B$ at $x = 4$ m: moment arm = $4 - 2 = 2$ m, force is downward, point is to the left of $B$, so moment is **clockwise** (negative):

$$
M_{F_w/B} = -12 \times 2 = -24 \text{ (CW)}
$$

Wait no. A downward force to the LEFT of the pivot creates a COUNTERCLOCKWISE moment. Let me use the formula directly:

$M_B = r \times F$ where $r$ is measured from $B$. For $F_w$: $r = (2-4) = -2$ m (to the left), $F = -12$ kN (downward). $M = (-2)(-12) = +24$ (CCW).

For $P$: $r = (6-4) = +2$ m (to the right), $F = -10$ kN (downward). $M = (2)(-10) = -20$ (CW).

For $A_y$: $r = (0-4) = -4$ m, $F = +A_y$ (upward). $M = (-4)(A_y)$.

$$
\sum M_B = (-4)(A_y) + 24 - 20 = 0
$$

$$
-4A_y + 4 = 0 \implies A_y = 1 \text{ kN} \quad \checkmark
$$

**Results:**

$$
A_x = 0, \quad A_y = 1 \text{ kN} \uparrow, \quad B_y = 21 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

</details>

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — 3D Rigid-Body Equilibrium (Six Equations)

**Given:** A rectangular plate $ABCD$ (dimensions $2\text{ m} \times 1.5\text{ m}$) lies in the $xz$-plane. It is supported by a ball-and-socket joint at $A$ (origin), a cable from $B = (2, 0, 0)$ to point $E = (2, 1.5, 1.5)$, and a roller at $D = (0, 0, 1.5)$ that provides a reaction in the $+y$-direction only. A force $\mathbf{F} = -10\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ kN acts at $C = (2, 0, 1.5)$.

**Find:** All support reactions and the cable tension.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Identify unknowns.**

- Ball-and-socket at $A$: $A_x, A_y, A_z$ (3 unknowns)
- Cable $BE$: tension $T_{BE}$ (1 unknown, direction known)
- Roller at $D$: $D_y$ (1 unknown)

Total: 5 unknowns, 6 equations → check if system is properly constrained. (With 5 unknowns and 6 equations, one equation must be automatically satisfied by geometry, or the system is over-constrained. Here the plate is in the $xz$-plane and the roller plus cable provide $y$-constraints. Let's proceed.)

**Step 2: Unit vector along cable $BE$.**

$$
\mathbf{r}_{BE} = E - B = (2-2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (1.5-0)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (1.5-0)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = 1.5\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 1.5\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$$
|\mathbf{r}_{BE}| = \sqrt{0 + 1.5^2 + 1.5^2} = \sqrt{4.5} = 1.5\sqrt{2} = 2.121 \text{ m}
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{BE} = \frac{1.5\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 1.5\hat{\mathbf{k}}}{2.121} = 0.7071\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.7071\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

Cable force: $\mathbf{T} = T(0.7071\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.7071\hat{\mathbf{k}})$

**Step 3: Write the six equilibrium equations.**

$\sum F_x = 0$:

$$
A_x = 0
$$

$\sum F_y = 0$:

$$
A_y + D_y + 0.7071T - 10 = 0 \tag{1}
$$

$\sum F_z = 0$:

$$
A_z + 0.7071T = 0 \tag{2}
$$

$\sum M_A = \mathbf{0}$ (take moments about $A$ to eliminate $A_x, A_y, A_z$):

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_A = \mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T} + \mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F} + \mathbf{r}_D \times D_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Compute each cross product:

$\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T}$: with $\mathbf{r}_B = 2\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ and $\mathbf{T} = T(0.7071\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.7071\hat{\mathbf{k}})$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T} = 2\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times T(0.7071\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.7071\hat{\mathbf{k}})
$$

$$
= 2T(0.7071(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}}) + 0.7071(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{k}}))
$$

$$
= 2T(0.7071\hat{\mathbf{k}} + 0.7071(-\hat{\mathbf{j}}))
$$

$$
= T(-1.4142\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 1.4142\hat{\mathbf{k}})
$$

$\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F}$: with $\mathbf{r}_C = 2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 1.5\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ and $\mathbf{F} = -10\hat{\mathbf{j}}$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F} = (2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 1.5\hat{\mathbf{k}}) \times (-10\hat{\mathbf{j}})
$$

$$
= 2(-10)(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}}) + 1.5(-10)(\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}})
$$

$$
= -20\hat{\mathbf{k}} + (-15)(-\hat{\mathbf{i}}) = 15\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 20\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$\mathbf{r}_D \times D_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}$: with $\mathbf{r}_D = 1.5\hat{\mathbf{k}}$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_D \times D_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} = 1.5D_y(\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}}) = 1.5D_y(-\hat{\mathbf{i}}) = -1.5D_y\hat{\mathbf{i}}
$$

**Step 4: Moment equations (component form).**

$\hat{\mathbf{i}}$: $15 - 1.5D_y = 0 \implies D_y = 10$ kN

$\hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $-1.4142T = 0 \implies T = 0$

$\hat{\mathbf{k}}$: $1.4142T - 20 = 0 \implies T = 14.14$ kN

**Contradiction!** The $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ equations give different values of $T$. This means the system as described is **improperly constrained** — the cable direction cannot simultaneously satisfy moment equilibrium in both $j$ and $k$ directions.

**Resolution:** The cable must have an $x$-component. Let us revise: $E = (0, 1.5, 1.5)$ instead (cable goes from $B$ toward the origin side).

$$
\mathbf{r}_{BE} = (-2, 1.5, 1.5), \quad |\mathbf{r}_{BE}| = \sqrt{4 + 2.25 + 2.25} = \sqrt{8.5} = 2.915
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{BE} = (-0.686, 0.514, 0.514)
$$

Now re-solve:

$\sum F_x = 0$: $A_x - 0.686T = 0 \implies A_x = 0.686T$

$\sum F_y = 0$: $A_y + D_y + 0.514T - 10 = 0$

$\sum F_z = 0$: $A_z + 0.514T = 0 \implies A_z = -0.514T$

$\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T} = (2\hat{\mathbf{i}}) \times T(-0.686\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.514\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0.514\hat{\mathbf{k}})$

$$
= 2T(0.514\hat{\mathbf{k}} - 0.514\hat{\mathbf{j}}) = T(-1.028\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 1.028\hat{\mathbf{k}})
$$

Moment equations:

$\hat{\mathbf{i}}$: $15 - 1.5D_y = 0 \implies D_y = 10$ kN

$\hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $-1.028T = 0 \implies T = 0$ ← Still zero!

This tells us the cable from $B$ cannot produce a moment about the $y$-axis through $A$ because $\mathbf{r}_B = 2\hat{\mathbf{i}}$ is purely in $x$, and the $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$-component of $\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T}$ involves only $T_z$... Let me reconsider.

Actually: $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ component of $\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T}$:

$$
(\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T})_j = r_{B,z}T_x - r_{B,x}T_z = 0 \cdot T_x - 2(0.514T) = -1.028T
$$

$\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ component: $(\mathbf{r}_B \times \mathbf{T})_k = r_{B,x}T_y - r_{B,y}T_x = 2(0.514T) - 0 = 1.028T$

Full moment equation:

$\hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $-1.028T - 20 = 0$? No — let me recompute $(\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F})_j$:

$$
(\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F})_j = r_{C,z}F_x - r_{C,x}F_z = 1.5(0) - 2(0) = 0
$$

Hmm. Let me redo $\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F}$ carefully with $\mathbf{r}_C = (2, 0, 1.5)$ and $\mathbf{F} = (0, -10, 0)$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ 2 & 0 & 1.5 \\ 0 & -10 & 0\end{vmatrix}
$$

$$
= \hat{\mathbf{i}}(0 \cdot 0 - 1.5 \cdot (-10)) - \hat{\mathbf{j}}(2 \cdot 0 - 1.5 \cdot 0) + \hat{\mathbf{k}}(2(-10) - 0 \cdot 0)
$$

$$
= 15\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 0\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 20\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

So the $\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ moment equation is:

$$
-1.028T + 0 = 0 \implies T = 0
$$

And $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$: $1.028T - 20 = 0 \implies T = 19.46$ kN

Still contradictory. The fundamental issue: a single cable attached at a point on the $x$-axis cannot provide moment about both $y$ and $z$ axes independently. We need **two cables** or a different support.

**Corrected problem:** Add a second cable from $D = (0,0,1.5)$ to $F = (0, 2, 1.5)$, providing force in $+y$ only: $\mathbf{T}_2 = T_2\hat{\mathbf{j}}$.

Now with $D_y$ replaced by cable $T_2$:

$\hat{\mathbf{i}}$: $15 - 1.5T_2 = 0 \implies T_2 = 10$ kN

$\hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $-1.028T_1 = 0 \implies T_1 = 0$

This geometry still fails. The **key lesson** of this example:

> **In 3D equilibrium, the placement of supports must be such that their lines of action can resist moments about all three axes.** A ball-and-socket at the origin plus supports whose moment arms all pass through the same axis will leave the body free to rotate about that axis.

**Properly constrained version:** Ball-and-socket at $A = (0,0,0)$, cable from $C = (2,0,1.5)$ to $E = (2, 2, 3)$, and cable from $D = (0,0,1.5)$ to $F = (-1, 2, 1.5)$. Load $\mathbf{F} = -10\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ kN at midpoint $M = (1, 0, 0.75)$.

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{CE} = \frac{(0, 2, 1.5)}{2.5} = (0, 0.8, 0.6)
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{DF} = \frac{(-1, 2, 0)}{2.236} = (-0.447, 0.894, 0)
$$

$\sum \mathbf{M}_A = \mathbf{0}$:

$\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{T}_1 + \mathbf{r}_D \times \mathbf{T}_2 + \mathbf{r}_M \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$

$\mathbf{r}_M \times \mathbf{F} = (1, 0, 0.75) \times (0, -10, 0) = (0\cdot0 - 0.75(-10))\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (1\cdot0 - 0.75\cdot0)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (1(-10) - 0\cdot0)\hat{\mathbf{k}}$

$$
= 7.5\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 10\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$\mathbf{r}_C \times \mathbf{T}_1 = (2, 0, 1.5) \times T_1(0, 0.8, 0.6)$

$$
= T_1[(0)(0.6) - (1.5)(0.8)]\hat{\mathbf{i}} - T_1[(2)(0.6) - (1.5)(0)]\hat{\mathbf{j}} + T_1[(2)(0.8) - (0)(0)]\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$$
= T_1(-1.2\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 1.2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 1.6\hat{\mathbf{k}})
$$

$\mathbf{r}_D \times \mathbf{T}_2 = (0, 0, 1.5) \times T_2(-0.447, 0.894, 0)$

$$
= T_2[(0)(0) - (1.5)(0.894)]\hat{\mathbf{i}} - T_2[(0)(0) - (1.5)(-0.447)]\hat{\mathbf{j}} + T_2[(0)(0.894) - (0)(-0.447)]\hat{\mathbf{k}}
$$

$$
= T_2(-1.341\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 0.671\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0\hat{\mathbf{k}})
$$

**Moment equations:**

$\hat{\mathbf{i}}$: $-1.2T_1 - 1.341T_2 + 7.5 = 0 \tag{I}$

$\hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $-1.2T_1 - 0.671T_2 + 0 = 0 \tag{II}$

$\hat{\mathbf{k}}$: $1.6T_1 + 0 - 10 = 0 \tag{III}$

From (III): $T_1 = 6.25$ kN

From (II): $-1.2(6.25) - 0.671T_2 = 0 \implies T_2 = \frac{-7.5}{0.671} = -11.18$ kN

Negative $T_2$ means the cable must push — impossible for a cable! This indicates the geometry needs adjustment, or $D$–$F$ should be a strut (compression member).

**Accepting $T_2$ as a strut force:** $T_2 = 11.18$ kN (compression).

Check (I): $-1.2(6.25) - 1.341(-11.18) + 7.5 = -7.5 + 14.99 + 7.5 = 14.99 \neq 0$.

The system is inconsistent with these particular coordinates. This extended example demonstrates the **critical importance of support geometry in 3D** — a lesson often glossed over in textbooks.

**Final Takeaway:** Before solving any 3D equilibrium problem:
1. Count unknowns vs. equations (need exactly 6 for a single rigid body).
2. Verify that no three force lines are concurrent or coplanar in a way that leaves a rotational DOF unresisted.
3. Check that cable supports can only pull (tension), not push.

*Reference: Hibbeler, Engineering Mechanics: Statics, 14th ed., §5.7.*

</details>


### Example 8.2 — Truss Analysis: Method of Joints + Method of Sections Combined

**Given:** A Warren truss (no verticals) has 5 panels, each of width $a = 2$ m. The top and bottom chords are horizontal; diagonals alternate direction. Total span $= 10$ m. Height $h = 2$ m. Pin at left ($A$), roller at right ($F$). Loads: $P_1 = 20$ kN at second bottom joint ($B$, $x = 2$ m), $P_2 = 30$ kN at third bottom joint ($C$, $x = 4$ m).

**Find:** (a) Support reactions. (b) Force in the diagonal member connecting the second top joint to the third bottom joint (call it member $GC$). (c) Force in the top chord between the second and third top joints (member $GH$).

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Global equilibrium.**

Bottom joints: $A(0,0)$, $B(2,0)$, $C(4,0)$, $D(6,0)$, $E(8,0)$, $F(10,0)$.
Top joints: $G(3,2)$, $H(5,2)$, $I(7,2)$ — for a Warren truss, top joints sit at midpoints between bottom joints offset by one panel.

Actually, for a standard 5-panel Warren truss: bottom joints at $x = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10$ and top joints at $x = 1, 3, 5, 7, 9$ (midpoints), all at height $h = 2$ m.

Let me use a simpler labeling. Bottom: $J_1(0,0), J_2(2,0), J_3(4,0), J_4(6,0), J_5(8,0), J_6(10,0)$. Top: $T_1(1,2), T_2(3,2), T_3(5,2), T_4(7,2), T_5(9,2)$.

Loads: $P_1 = 20$ kN $\downarrow$ at $J_2$, $P_2 = 30$ kN $\downarrow$ at $J_3$.

$\sum M_{J_1} = 0$:

$$
R_{J_6}(10) - 20(2) - 30(4) = 0
$$

$$
R_{J_6} = \frac{40 + 120}{10} = 16 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

$\sum F_y = 0$:

$$
R_{J_1} = 20 + 30 - 16 = 34 \text{ kN} \uparrow
$$

**Step 2: Method of Sections — cut through panel containing $T_2$–$T_3$ top chord and diagonal $T_2$–$J_3$.**

Make a vertical cut between $x = 3$ and $x = 5$, cutting members: top chord $T_2 T_3$, diagonal $T_2 J_3$ (or $T_3 J_3$), and bottom chord $J_2 J_3$ (or $J_3 J_4$).

For a Warren truss, the members crossing a cut at $x = 4^-$ (just left of $J_3$) are:
- Top chord: $T_2 T_3$ (horizontal)
- Diagonal: $T_2 J_3$ (going from $(3,2)$ to $(4,0)$)
- Bottom chord: $J_2 J_3$ (horizontal)

Take the left portion. External forces on left portion: $R_{J_1} = 34$ kN $\uparrow$, $P_1 = 20$ kN $\downarrow$ at $J_2$.

**$\sum M_{J_3} = 0$** (eliminates diagonal and bottom chord forces):

$$
R_{J_1}(4) - P_1(2) + F_{T_2T_3}(2) = 0
$$

Here $F_{T_2T_3}$ acts horizontally; its moment arm about $J_3(4,0)$ is the vertical distance = 2 m. If $F_{T_2T_3}$ is tension (pointing right on the cut face), it creates a clockwise moment about $J_3$:

$$

### Example 8.3 — Statically Indeterminate Propped Cantilever

**Given:** A beam of length $L$ is fixed (built-in) at end $A$ and simply supported (roller) at end $B$. A uniform distributed load $w$ (force/length) acts over the entire span.

**Find:** All reactions using the compatibility (force) method.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1: Degree of static indeterminacy.**

Reactions: $A_x, A_y, M_A$ (fixed end) + $B_y$ (roller) = 4 unknowns.
Equilibrium equations (2D): 3.
Degree of indeterminacy: $4 - 3 = 1$.

Choose $B_y$ as the redundant.

**Step 2: Released structure (remove roller at $B$).**

The released structure is a cantilever fixed at $A$ with uniform load $w$. The deflection at $B$ (free end) due to $w$ alone:

$$
\delta_{B,w} = \frac{wL^4}{8EI} \quad (\text{downward})
$$

**Step 3: Deflection at $B$ due to redundant $B_y$ alone (upward force at tip of cantilever).**

$$
\delta_{B,R} = \frac{B_y L^3}{3EI} \quad (\text{upward})
$$

**Step 4: Compatibility condition — net deflection at $B$ is zero (roller support).**

$$
\delta_{B,w} - \delta_{B,R} = 0
$$

$$
\frac{wL^4}{8EI} = \frac{B_y L^3}{3EI}
$$

$$
B_y = \frac{3wL}{8}
$$

**Step 5: Remaining reactions from equilibrium.**

$\sum F_y = 0$: $A_y + B_y - wL = 0 \implies A_y = wL - \frac{3wL}{8} = \frac{5wL}{8}$

$\sum M_A = 0$ (CCW positive):

$$
M_A + B_y(L) - wL\left(\frac{L}{2}\right) = 0
$$

$$
M_A = \frac{wL^2}{2} - \frac{3wL}{8}(L) = \frac{wL^2}{2} - \frac{3wL^2}{8} = \frac{wL^2}{8}
$$

**Final Results:**

$$
A_y = \frac{5wL}{8}, \quad B_y = \frac{3wL}{8}, \quad M_A = \frac{wL^2}{8} \text{ (CW)}
$$

**Verification:** The maximum bending moment occurs where shear $= 0$:

$$
V(x) = A_y - wx = \frac{5wL}{8} - wx = 0 \implies x = \frac{5L}{8}
$$

$$
M_{\max} = A_y\left(\frac{5L}{8}\right) - M_A - w\frac{(5L/8)^2}{2} = \frac{5wL}{8}\cdot\frac{5L}{8} - \frac{wL^2}{8} - \frac{w}{2}\cdot\frac{25L^2}{64}
$$

$$
= \frac{25wL^2}{64} - \frac{8wL^2}{64} - \frac{25wL^2}{128} = \frac{50wL^2 - 16wL^2 - 25wL^2}{128} = \frac{9wL^2}{128}
$$

### Example 8.4 — Cable Under Distributed Load (Catenary vs. Parabolic)

**Given:** A cable spans $L = 60$ m between two supports at the same elevation. It carries its own weight $w_0 = 50$ N/m (weight per unit length of cable). The sag at midspan is $h = 3$ m.

**Find:** (a) Maximum tension in the cable. (b) Length of the cable. (c) Compare with the parabolic approximation.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Part (a): Catenary analysis.**

The catenary equation (origin at lowest point):

$$
y = a\left(\cosh\frac{x}{a} - 1\right)
$$

where $a = H/w_0$ and $H$ is the horizontal tension component.

Boundary condition: at $x = L/2 = 30$ m, $y = h = 3$ m:

$$
3 = a\left(\cosh\frac{30}{a} - 1\right)
$$

This is transcendental — solve iteratively. Try $a = 150$ m:

$$
\cosh(30/150) = \cosh(0.2) = 1.02007
$$

$$
a(\cosh(30/a) - 1) = 150(0.02007) = 3.01 \approx 3 \quad \checkmark
$$

So $a \approx 150$ m, meaning $H = w_0 a = 50 \times 150 = 7500$ N.

**Maximum tension** (at supports, where slope is maximum):

$$
T_{\max} = w_0 a \cosh\frac{L/2}{a} = 50 \times 150 \times \cosh(0.2) = 7500 \times 1.02007 = 7650 \text{ N}
$$

**Part (b): Cable length.**

$$
s = 2a\sinh\frac{L/2}{a} = 2(150)\sinh(0.2) = 300 \times 0.20134 = 60.40 \text{ m}
$$

**Part (c): Parabolic approximation** (valid when sag $\ll$ span, i.e., $h/L \ll 1$; here $h/L = 0.05$).

For a parabolic cable with uniform load per horizontal length:

$$
H = \frac{w_0 L^2}{8h} = \frac{50 \times 3600}{24} = 7500 \text{ N}
$$

$$
T_{\max} = H\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{4h}{L}\right)^2} = 7500\sqrt{1 + (0.2)^2} = 7500\sqrt{1.04} = 7649 \text{ N}
$$

$$
s \approx L\left(1 + \frac{8h^2}{3L^2}\right) = 60\left(1 + \frac{8(9)}{3(3600)}\right) = 60(1 + 0.00667) = 60.40 \text{ m}
$$

**Comparison:** The parabolic approximation gives $T_{\max} = 7649$ N vs. catenary $7650$ N — error $\lt  0.02\%$. For shallow cables ($h/L \lt  0.1$), the parabolic approximation is excellent.

*Reference: Hibbeler, Statics, 14th ed., §7.4 (Cables).*

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### Appendix 9.1 — Principle of Virtual Work for Rigid Bodies

The **Principle of Virtual Work** states: *A system of rigid bodies is in equilibrium if and only if the total virtual work done by all external forces through any compatible virtual displacement is zero.*

$$
\delta W = \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i \cdot \delta\mathbf{r}_i + \sum_j \mathbf{M}_j \cdot \delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_j = 0
$$

for all kinematically admissible virtual displacements $\delta\mathbf{r}_i$, $\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_j$.

**Why it is powerful:** For a system with $n$ degrees of freedom and many internal constraints (pins, rollers), the virtual work method eliminates all internal constraint forces automatically — only external loads and the unknown of interest appear.

**Derivation from Newton's Laws:**

Consider a rigid body in equilibrium: $\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0}$.

Give the body a virtual displacement consisting of translation $\delta\mathbf{r}_O$ and rotation $\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$ about $O$. The virtual displacement of any point $P$ is:

$$
\delta\mathbf{r}_P = \delta\mathbf{r}_O + \delta\boldsymbol{\theta} \times \mathbf{r}_{OP}
$$

The virtual work of all forces:

$$
\delta W = \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i \cdot \delta\mathbf{r}_{P_i} = \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i \cdot (\delta\mathbf{r}_O + \delta\boldsymbol{\theta} \times \mathbf{r}_{OP_i})
$$

$$
= \delta\mathbf{r}_O \cdot \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i + \delta\boldsymbol{\theta} \cdot \sum_i (\mathbf{r}_{OP_i} \times \mathbf{F}_i)
$$

(using the scalar triple product identity $\mathbf{A} \cdot (\mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{B} \cdot (\mathbf{C} \times \mathbf{A})$)

$$
= \delta\mathbf{r}_O \cdot \left(\sum \mathbf{F}\right) + \delta\boldsymbol{\theta} \cdot \left(\sum \mathbf{M}_O\right) = 0 + 0 = 0
$$

since both $\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0}$ by equilibrium. $\blacksquare$

**Converse:** If $\delta W = 0$ for all $\delta\mathbf{r}_O$ and $\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (which are arbitrary and independent), then $\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0}$.

**Application Example:** A simple lever of length $L$ with fulcrum at distance $a$ from force $F_1$ and distance $b = L - a$ from force $F_2$. Virtual rotation $\delta\theta$:

$$
\delta W = F_1(a\,\delta\theta) - F_2(b\,\delta\theta) = 0 \implies F_1 a = F_2 b
$$

This recovers the lever law without computing the fulcrum reaction.

*Reference: Timoshenko & Young, Engineering Mechanics, §8.2; MIT OCW 2.001.*

### Appendix 9.2 — Proof: Method of Joints Guarantees Global Equilibrium

**Claim:** If every joint in a truss satisfies $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$, then the entire truss automatically satisfies all three global equilibrium equations ($\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$, $\sum M = 0$).

**Proof:**

Consider a truss with $j$ joints. At each joint $k$, the equilibrium equations are:

$$
\sum_{m \in \text{members at } k} F_{m,x} + R_{k,x} + P_{k,x} = 0 \tag{for each joint}
$$

where $F_{m,x}$ are internal member forces, $R_{k,x}$ are reactions, and $P_{k,x}$ are applied loads.

**Sum all joint equations in the $x$-direction:**

Each internal member force $F_m$ appears exactly **twice** (once at each end joint) with **opposite signs** (Newton's Third Law: tension pulls both joints toward the member). Therefore all internal forces cancel in the sum:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{j}\left(\sum_{m} F_{m,x}\right) = 0
$$

What remains:

$$
\sum_k R_{k,x} + \sum_k P_{k,x} = 0 \implies \sum F_{x,\text{external}} = 0 \quad \checkmark
$$

Similarly for $\sum F_y = 0$. $\checkmark$

**For moment equilibrium:** Multiply each joint's $\sum F_y = 0$ equation by the $x$-coordinate of that joint, and subtract each joint's $\sum F_x = 0$ equation multiplied by the $y$-coordinate:

$$
\sum_k x_k \left(\sum F_{y,k}\right) - \sum_k y_k \left(\sum F_{x,k}\right) = 0
$$

For each internal member connecting joints $k$ and $l$: the contribution is $x_k F_{kl,y} + x_l F_{lk,y} - y_k F_{kl,x} - y_l F_{lk,x}$. Since $F_{lk} = -F_{kl}$ (equal and opposite), this becomes:

$$
(x_k - x_l)F_{kl,y} - (y_k - y_l)F_{kl,x}
$$

For a two-force member along direction $(x_l - x_k, y_l - y_k)$, the force components satisfy $F_{kl,y}/F_{kl,x} = (y_l - y_k)/(x_l - x_k)$, so:

$$
(x_k - x_l)F_{kl,x}\frac{(y_l-y_k)}{(x_l-x_k)} - (y_k - y_l)F_{kl,x} = -F_{kl,x}(y_l-y_k) + F_{kl,x}(y_l-y_k) = 0
$$

All internal forces vanish, leaving only external forces and reactions:

$$
\sum_k (x_k R_{k,y} - y_k R_{k,x}) + \sum_k (x_k P_{k,y} - y_k P_{k,x}) = 0
$$

This is precisely $\sum M_O = 0$ for the entire truss. $\blacksquare$

**Practical consequence:** When solving a truss by the method of joints, you can start from a joint with only 2 unknowns and proceed sequentially. The last joint provides a **check** — its equations must be automatically satisfied if all previous joints were solved correctly.

*Reference: Hibbeler, Statics, 14th ed., §6.2; Timoshenko, Engineering Mechanics.*

### Appendix 9.3 — Statical Determinacy Classification

A structure is classified by comparing the number of unknown forces to the number of independent equilibrium equations:

**For a 2D truss:**
- Members: $m$ (each is a two-force member → 1 unknown per member)
- Joints: $j$ (each provides 2 equations)
- External reactions: $r$

$$
\text{Determinacy check: } m + r \stackrel{?}{=} 2j
$$

| Condition | Classification |
|:---|:---|
| $m + r = 2j$ | Statically determinate |
| $m + r > 2j$ | Statically indeterminate (degree $= m + r - 2j$) |
| $m + r < 2j$ | Mechanism (unstable) |

**For a 2D frame/beam:**
- Internal unknowns: 3 per member (axial, shear, moment at each cut)
- Equations: 3 per member (equilibrium of each free body) + 3 global
- Reactions: $r$
- Conditions at internal hinges: $c$ (each hinge provides 1 condition: $M = 0$)

$$
\text{Degree of indeterminacy} = r - 3 - c \quad \text{(for single-span beams)}
$$

**For 3D structures:** Replace 3 equations per joint with 6 (or 3 per joint for trusses with 3 force components).

**Example:** A two-span continuous beam (fixed at left, rollers at middle and right):
- Reactions: $A_x, A_y, M_A, B_y, C_y = 5$
- Equilibrium equations: 3
- Degree of indeterminacy: $5 - 3 = 2$

*Reference: Hibbeler, Structural Analysis, 10th ed., §2.4.*
