# 6.8 — Compressible Flow & Shock Waves

> *"The shock wave is nature's most violent discontinuity — across it, pressure, density, and temperature jump instantaneously, and the elegant smoothness of the Navier-Stokes equations gives way to the brutal algebra of conservation laws."* — paraphrased from H.W. Liepmann & A. Roshko, *Elements of Gasdynamics* (1957).

When flow velocities approach or exceed the speed of sound, density changes become significant and the incompressible assumption fails. Compressible flow theory introduces the Mach number as the governing parameter, reveals the phenomenon of choking in nozzles, and predicts the formation of shock waves — thin regions where flow properties change discontinuously. This chapter develops isentropic flow relations, derives the normal shock jump conditions (Rankine-Hugoniot), and analyzes converging-diverging nozzle flows.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 6.8.1 — Speed of Sound

The **speed of sound** $c$ is the speed at which infinitesimal pressure disturbances propagate through a medium:

$$
c = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho}\right)_s},
$$

where the derivative is taken at constant entropy. For an ideal gas with ratio of specific heats $\gamma$:

$$
c = \sqrt{\gamma R T / M_w} = \sqrt{\gamma p / \rho},
$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant, $T$ is temperature, and $M_w$ is molar mass.

### Definition 6.8.2 — Mach Number

$$
M = \frac{v}{c} = \frac{\text{flow speed}}{\text{speed of sound}}.
$$

- $M < 1$: subsonic
- $M = 1$: sonic (critical)
- $1 < M < 5$: supersonic
- $M > 5$: hypersonic

### Definition 6.8.3 — Stagnation (Total) Conditions

**Stagnation temperature** $T_0$, **stagnation pressure** $p_0$, and **stagnation density** $\rho_0$ are the values a fluid element would attain if brought isentropically to rest:

$$
\frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2.
$$

### Definition 6.8.4 — Critical Conditions (Starred Quantities)

**Critical conditions** ($T^*$, $p^*$, $\rho^*$) are the values at $M = 1$ (sonic point):

$$
\frac{T^*}{T_0} = \frac{2}{\gamma + 1}, \qquad \frac{p^*}{p_0} = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}, \qquad \frac{\rho^*}{\rho_0} = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}.
$$

### Definition 6.8.5 — Normal Shock Wave

A **normal shock** is a discontinuous surface perpendicular to the flow direction across which the flow transitions from supersonic to subsonic. Entropy increases across the shock (irreversible process).

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 6.8.A — Ideal Gas Law

$$
p = \rho R_{\text{specific}} T, \qquad R_{\text{specific}} = R/M_w.
$$

### Axiom 6.8.B — Isentropic Process (Away from Shocks)

For smooth, adiabatic flow without shocks:

$$
\frac{p}{\rho^\gamma} = \text{const}, \qquad \frac{T}{\rho^{\gamma-1}} = \text{const}.
$$

### Axiom 6.8.C — Conservation Laws Across a Shock (Integral Form)

Across a shock, the differential N-S equations break down, but the integral conservation laws (mass, momentum, energy) remain valid. These yield the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions.

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 6.8.1 — Isentropic Flow Relations

For isentropic flow of an ideal gas:

$$
\frac{T_0}{T} = 1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2, \qquad \frac{p_0}{p} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}, \qquad \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}.
$$

**Proof.** From the energy equation for adiabatic flow: $h + v^2/2 = h_0$ (total enthalpy is constant). For an ideal gas, $h = c_p T$:

$$
c_p T + \frac{v^2}{2} = c_p T_0.
$$

Divide by $c_p T$:

$$
1 + \frac{v^2}{2c_p T} = \frac{T_0}{T}.
$$

Since $c_p = \gamma R_s/(\gamma-1)$ and $c^2 = \gamma R_s T$:

$$
\frac{v^2}{2c_p T} = \frac{v^2}{2 \cdot \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1} R_s T} = \frac{(\gamma-1)v^2}{2\gamma R_s T} = \frac{(\gamma-1)v^2}{2c^2} = \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2.
$$

Therefore $T_0/T = 1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2$. The pressure and density relations follow from $p/p_0 = (T/T_0)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$ (isentropic). $\blacksquare$

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 6.8.1 — Area-Mach Number Relation (Quasi-1D Isentropic Flow)

For steady, isentropic flow in a variable-area duct:

$$
\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))}.
$$

This shows that $A = A^*$ only at $M = 1$ (the throat), and that both subsonic and supersonic solutions exist for $A > A^*$.

### Theorem 6.8.2 — Normal Shock Relations (Rankine-Hugoniot)

For a normal shock with upstream Mach number $M_1 > 1$:

$$
M_2^2 = \frac{M_1^2 + \frac{2}{\gamma-1}}{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}M_1^2 - 1},
$$

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1),
$$

$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2 + 2},
$$

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{p_2}{p_1} \cdot \frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}.
$$

### Theorem 6.8.3 — Entropy Increase Across a Shock

The entropy change across a normal shock is:

$$
\Delta s = s_2 - s_1 = c_v \ln\left(\frac{p_2/p_1}{(\rho_2/\rho_1)^\gamma}\right) > 0 \quad \text{for } M_1 > 1.
$$

This proves that only compression shocks ($M_1 > 1 \to M_2 < 1$) are physically realizable. Expansion shocks would violate the second law of thermodynamics.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### Proof 5.1 — Derivation of Normal Shock Relations (Theorem 6.8.2)

**Goal:** From conservation of mass, momentum, and energy across the shock, derive the jump conditions.

**Step 1: Conservation laws across the shock (control volume from just upstream to just downstream).**

Mass: $\rho_1 v_1 = \rho_2 v_2$.

Momentum: $p_1 + \rho_1 v_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 v_2^2$.

Energy: $h_1 + \frac{v_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \frac{v_2^2}{2}$ (adiabatic, no work).

**Step 2: Define the mass flux $\dot{m} = \rho_1 v_1 = \rho_2 v_2$.**

From momentum: $p_1 - p_2 = \rho_2 v_2^2 - \rho_1 v_1^2 = \dot{m}(v_2 - v_1)$.

Also: $p_1 - p_2 = \dot{m} v_2 - \dot{m} v_1$... Let us use the standard approach.

**Step 3: From mass conservation, $v_2 = (\rho_1/\rho_2)v_1$. Substitute into momentum:**

$$
p_1 + \rho_1 v_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 \left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^2 v_1^2 = p_2 + \frac{\rho_1^2 v_1^2}{\rho_2}.
$$

$$
p_1 - p_2 = \rho_1 v_1^2\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} - 1\right) = \rho_1 v_1^2 \frac{\rho_1 - \rho_2}{\rho_2}.
$$

**Step 4: Use the energy equation with ideal gas ($h = c_p T = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p}{\rho}$):**

$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{v_1^2}{2} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2} + \frac{v_2^2}{2}.
$$

**Step 5: Introduce $M_1^2 = v_1^2/c_1^2 = v_1^2 \rho_1/(\gamma p_1)$, so $\rho_1 v_1^2 = \gamma p_1 M_1^2$.**

After algebraic manipulation (substituting mass and momentum into energy), one obtains the **Prandtl relation**:

$$
v_1 v_2 = c^{*2} = \frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1} c_p T_0 = \frac{2\gamma R_s T_0}{\gamma+1} \cdot \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma} = c_1 c_2 \cdot \frac{\text{function of } M_1}{...}
$$

The standard algebraic route yields directly:

$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2 + 2}.
$$

**Step 6: Pressure ratio from momentum:**

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1).
$$

**Step 7: Downstream Mach number from the relation:**

$$
M_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2}{\gamma M_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}} = \frac{M_1^2 + \frac{2}{\gamma-1}}{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}M_1^2 - 1}. \quad \blacksquare
$$

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 6.8.1 — Isentropic Nozzle Flow

**Problem.** Air ($\gamma = 1.4$) enters a converging-diverging nozzle with stagnation conditions $T_0 = 500$ K, $p_0 = 500$ kPa. The throat area is $A^* = 0.01$ m². Find conditions at the throat and at a section where $A/A^* = 2$ (supersonic branch).

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** At the throat ($M = 1$):

$$
T^* = T_0 \cdot \frac{2}{\gamma+1} = 500 \cdot \frac{2}{2.4} = 416.7\;\text{K}.
$$

$$
p^* = p_0 \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} = 500 \times (0.8333)^{3.5} = 500 \times 0.5283 = 264.2\;\text{kPa}.
$$

$$
c^* = \sqrt{\gamma R_s T^*} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 287 \times 416.7} = \sqrt{167{,}533} = 409.3\;\text{m/s}.
$$

**Step 2:** For $A/A^* = 2$, solve the area-Mach relation numerically. For the supersonic branch, $M \approx 2.20$.

**Step 3:** At $M = 2.20$:

$$
\frac{T}{T_0} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.2 \times 2.20^2} = \frac{1}{1.968} = 0.508 \implies T = 254\;\text{K}.
$$

$$
\frac{p}{p_0} = (0.508)^{3.5} = 0.0935 \implies p = 46.8\;\text{kPa}.
$$

---

### Example 6.8.2 — Normal Shock at Mach 2.5

**Problem.** A normal shock occurs in air ($\gamma = 1.4$) at $M_1 = 2.5$. Find $M_2$, $p_2/p_1$, $T_2/T_1$, and the stagnation pressure ratio.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** Downstream Mach number:

$$
M_2^2 = \frac{2.5^2 + 5}{7 \times 2.5^2 - 1} = \frac{6.25 + 5}{43.75 - 1} = \frac{11.25}{42.75} = 0.2632.
$$

$$
M_2 = 0.513.
$$

**Step 2:** Pressure ratio:

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2(1.4)}{2.4}(6.25 - 1) = 1 + \frac{2.8}{2.4}(5.25) = 1 + 6.125 = 7.125.
$$

**Step 3:** Density ratio:

$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{2.4 \times 6.25}{0.4 \times 6.25 + 2} = \frac{15}{4.5} = 3.333.
$$

**Step 4:** Temperature ratio:

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{p_2/p_1}{\rho_2/\rho_1} = \frac{7.125}{3.333} = 2.138.
$$

**Step 5:** Stagnation pressure ratio (entropy loss):

$$
\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}} = \frac{p_{02}/p_2 \cdot p_2/p_1}{p_{01}/p_1} = \frac{(1 + 0.2 M_2^2)^{3.5} \cdot 7.125}{(1 + 0.2 \times 6.25)^{3.5}}.
$$

$$
= \frac{(1.0526)^{3.5} \times 7.125}{(2.25)^{3.5}} = \frac{1.194 \times 7.125}{17.09} = \frac{8.505}{17.09} = 0.499.
$$

About 50% of the stagnation pressure is lost — a dramatic irreversibility.

### Example 6.8.3 — Speed of Sound in Various Media

**Problem.** Compute the speed of sound in: (a) air at 20°C, (b) helium at 20°C, (c) water at 20°C.

**Solution.**

(a) Air: $\gamma = 1.4$, $M_w = 0.029$ kg/mol, $T = 293$ K.

$$
c = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M_w}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.4 \times 8.314 \times 293}{0.029}} = \sqrt{117{,}600} = 343\;\text{m/s}.
$$

(b) Helium: $\gamma = 5/3$, $M_w = 0.004$ kg/mol.

$$
c = \sqrt{\frac{(5/3) \times 8.314 \times 293}{0.004}} = \sqrt{1{,}015{,}000} = 1008\;\text{m/s}.
$$

(c) Water: Use bulk modulus $K = 2.2$ GPa, $\rho = 998$ kg/m³.

$$
c = \sqrt{K/\rho} = \sqrt{2.2 \times 10^9 / 998} = \sqrt{2.204 \times 10^6} = 1485\;\text{m/s}.
$$

### Example 6.8.4 — Converging-Diverging Nozzle Design

**Problem.** Design a nozzle to produce Mach 2.0 flow at the exit. The throat area is $A^* = 0.005$ m². Find the exit area and, if the stagnation conditions are $T_0 = 400$ K, $p_0 = 300$ kPa, find exit temperature, pressure, and velocity.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** Area ratio for $M = 2.0$:

$$
\frac{A_e}{A^*} = \frac{1}{2.0}\left[\frac{2}{2.4}\left(1 + 0.2 \times 4\right)\right]^{3} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{2}{2.4} \times 1.8\right]^3 = \frac{1}{2}(1.5)^3 = \frac{1}{2}(3.375) = 1.6875.
$$

Wait — let us use the correct formula. For $\gamma = 1.4$:

$$
\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1))}.
$$

With $M = 2$, $(\gamma+1)/(2(\gamma-1)) = 2.4/0.8 = 3$:

$$
\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{2}{2.4}(1 + 0.2 \times 4)\right]^3 = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{2 \times 1.8}{2.4}\right]^3 = \frac{1}{2}\left[1.5\right]^3 = \frac{3.375}{2} = 1.6875.
$$

So $A_e = 1.6875 \times 0.005 = 0.00844$ m².

**Step 2:** Exit temperature:

$$
T_e = \frac{T_0}{1 + 0.2M^2} = \frac{400}{1.8} = 222.2\;\text{K}.
$$

**Step 3:** Exit pressure:

$$
p_e = \frac{p_0}{(1.8)^{3.5}} = \frac{300}{(1.8)^{3.5}}.
$$

$(1.8)^{3.5} = (1.8)^3 \times (1.8)^{0.5} = 5.832 \times 1.342 = 7.824$.

$$
p_e = 300/7.824 = 38.4\;\text{kPa}.
$$

**Step 4:** Exit velocity:

$$
c_e = \sqrt{\gamma R T_e} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 287 \times 222.2} = \sqrt{89{,}270} = 298.8\;\text{m/s}.
$$

$$
v_e = M \times c_e = 2.0 \times 298.8 = 597.6\;\text{m/s}.
$$

### Example 6.8.5 — Entropy Increase Across a Shock

**Problem.** Compute the entropy increase $\Delta s / R$ across a normal shock at $M_1 = 3.0$ in air.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** From the shock relations at $M_1 = 3$:

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2(1.4)}{2.4}(9-1) = 1 + \frac{2.8}{2.4}(8) = 1 + 9.333 = 10.333.
$$

$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{2.4 \times 9}{0.4 \times 9 + 2} = \frac{21.6}{5.6} = 3.857.
$$

**Step 2:** Entropy change:

$$
\frac{\Delta s}{R} = \frac{1}{\gamma - 1}\ln\left(\frac{p_2/p_1}{(\rho_2/\rho_1)^\gamma}\right) = \frac{1}{0.4}\ln\left(\frac{10.333}{(3.857)^{1.4}}\right).
$$

**Step 3:** Compute $(3.857)^{1.4}$:

$\ln(3.857) = 1.350$, so $1.4 \times 1.350 = 1.890$, $(3.857)^{1.4} = e^{1.890} = 6.619$.

$$
\frac{\Delta s}{R} = 2.5 \times \ln\left(\frac{10.333}{6.619}\right) = 2.5 \times \ln(1.561) = 2.5 \times 0.445 = 1.11.
$$

The entropy increase is substantial — about $1.11 R$ per unit mass, confirming the strong irreversibility of the shock.

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Normal Shock Jump Conditions (Rankine-Hugoniot)

**Problem.** Air ($\gamma = 1.4$) flows at Mach $M_1 = 2.5$ into a stationary normal shock. Compute all downstream conditions: $M_2$, $p_2/p_1$, $\rho_2/\rho_1$, $T_2/T_1$, and the stagnation pressure ratio $p_{02}/p_{01}$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Downstream Mach Number

The normal shock relation for downstream Mach number:

$$
M_2^2 = \frac{1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_1^2}{\gamma M_1^2 - \frac{\gamma-1}{2}} = \frac{1 + 0.2(6.25)}{1.4(6.25) - 0.2} = \frac{1 + 1.25}{8.75 - 0.2} = \frac{2.25}{8.55} = 0.2632.
$$

$$
M_2 = \sqrt{0.2632} = 0.513.
$$

The flow is subsonic downstream of the shock, as expected.

#### Step 2: Pressure Ratio

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1) = 1 + \frac{2(1.4)}{2.4}(6.25 - 1) = 1 + \frac{2.8}{2.4}(5.25) = 1 + 6.125 = 7.125.
$$

#### Step 3: Density Ratio

$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2 + 2} = \frac{2.4 \times 6.25}{0.4 \times 6.25 + 2} = \frac{15.0}{2.5 + 2} = \frac{15.0}{4.5} = 3.333.
$$

#### Step 4: Temperature Ratio

From the ideal gas law $p = \rho R T$:

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{p_2/p_1}{\rho_2/\rho_1} = \frac{7.125}{3.333} = 2.138.
$$

Alternatively, using the direct formula:

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{[2\gamma M_1^2 - (\gamma-1)][(\gamma-1)M_1^2 + 2]}{(\gamma+1)^2 M_1^2} = \frac{[2(1.4)(6.25) - 0.4][0.4(6.25) + 2]}{(2.4)^2(6.25)}.
$$

$$
= \frac{[17.5 - 0.4][2.5 + 2]}{5.76 \times 6.25} = \frac{17.1 \times 4.5}{36.0} = \frac{76.95}{36.0} = 2.138. \quad \checkmark
$$

#### Step 5: Stagnation Pressure Ratio

The stagnation pressure ratio measures the irreversibility (entropy increase) across the shock:

$$
\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2 + 2}\right]^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} \cdot \left[\frac{2\gamma M_1^2 - (\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}\right]^{-1/(\gamma-1)}.
$$

$$
= \left[\frac{2.4 \times 6.25}{4.5}\right]^{3.5} \cdot \left[\frac{17.5 - 0.4}{2.4}\right]^{-2.5} = (3.333)^{3.5} \cdot (7.125)^{-2.5}.
$$

Compute $(3.333)^{3.5}$: $\ln(3.333) = 1.204$, $3.5 \times 1.204 = 4.213$, $e^{4.213} = 67.56$.

Compute $(7.125)^{2.5}$: $\ln(7.125) = 1.964$, $2.5 \times 1.964 = 4.909$, $e^{4.909} = 135.6$.

$$
\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}} = \frac{67.56}{135.6} = 0.499.
$$

The stagnation pressure drops by about 50% — a massive loss. This is why supersonic inlets must be carefully designed to minimize shock losses.

#### Step 6: Verification — Entropy Increase

$$
\frac{\Delta s}{R} = \ln\frac{p_{01}}{p_{02}} = \ln\frac{1}{0.499} = \ln(2.004) = 0.695.
$$

This is positive, confirming the second law: entropy increases across the shock. $\checkmark$

**Final Answer:**

$$
M_2 = 0.513,\; \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 7.125,\; \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = 3.333,\; \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.138,\; \frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}} = 0.499.
$$

### Example 8.2 — The Sod Shock Tube Problem (1D Riemann Problem)

**Problem.** A shock tube has a diaphragm at $x = 0$ separating high-pressure gas (left: $p_L = 1.0$ atm, $\rho_L = 1.0$ kg/m³) from low-pressure gas (right: $p_R = 0.1$ atm, $\rho_R = 0.125$ kg/m³). Both sides are initially at rest. At $t = 0$ the diaphragm bursts. Describe the wave structure and compute the post-shock state using the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions. Assume $\gamma = 1.4$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Wave Structure of the Riemann Problem

When the diaphragm bursts, three waves emerge:
1. **Expansion fan** (rarefaction) propagating to the left into the high-pressure gas.
2. **Contact discontinuity** propagating to the right (separating gas that was originally on different sides).
3. **Shock wave** propagating to the right into the low-pressure gas.

The solution has four constant states separated by these three waves:
- State L (undisturbed left)
- State 2 (between expansion fan and contact)
- State 3 (between contact and shock) — same $p$ and $u$ as State 2, but different $\rho$ and $T$
- State R (undisturbed right)

#### Step 2: Compute Sound Speeds

$$
c_L = \sqrt{\gamma p_L / \rho_L} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 101325 / 1.0} = \sqrt{141855} = 376.6\;\text{m/s}.
$$

$$
c_R = \sqrt{\gamma p_R / \rho_R} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 10132.5 / 0.125} = \sqrt{113484} = 336.9\;\text{m/s}.
$$

#### Step 3: The Shock Jump (Right-Moving Shock into State R)

Let the shock have speed $V_s$ (positive, moving right). In the shock frame, the upstream (right) gas approaches at $w_1 = V_s$ (since $u_R = 0$). The downstream (state 3) gas moves at $w_2 = V_s - u_3$.

The Rankine-Hugoniot relations give:

$$
\frac{p_3}{p_R} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(\frac{V_s^2}{c_R^2} - 1\right).
$$

$$
u_3 = \frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(V_s - \frac{c_R^2}{V_s}\right).
$$

#### Step 4: The Expansion Fan (Left-Moving Rarefaction)

Across the isentropic expansion fan, the Riemann invariant $J_+ = u + \frac{2c}{\gamma-1}$ is constant:

$$
u_2 + \frac{2c_2}{\gamma-1} = u_L + \frac{2c_L}{\gamma-1} = 0 + \frac{2 \times 376.6}{0.4} = 1883.
$$

The isentropic relation: $c_2 = c_L(p_2/p_L)^{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)}$.

#### Step 5: Matching Conditions at the Contact

Across the contact discontinuity: $p_2 = p_3$ and $u_2 = u_3$. This gives a system of equations that must be solved iteratively (or by Newton's method) for $p^* = p_2 = p_3$ and $u^* = u_2 = u_3$.

For the Sod problem with the given parameters, the exact solution gives:

$$
p^* = 0.3031\;\text{atm}, \qquad u^* = 293.0\;\text{m/s}.
$$

#### Step 6: Shock Speed

From the shock relations:

$$
\frac{p^*}{p_R} = \frac{0.3031}{0.1} = 3.031.
$$

$$
M_s^2 = 1 + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\left(\frac{p^*}{p_R} - 1\right) = 1 + \frac{2.4}{2.8}(2.031) = 1 + 1.741 = 2.741.
$$

$$
M_s = 1.656, \qquad V_s = M_s \cdot c_R = 1.656 \times 336.9 = 557.5\;\text{m/s}.
$$

#### Step 7: Post-Shock Density

$$
\frac{\rho_3}{\rho_R} = \frac{(\gamma+1)(p^*/p_R) + (\gamma-1)}{(\gamma-1)(p^*/p_R) + (\gamma+1)} = \frac{2.4(3.031) + 0.4}{0.4(3.031) + 2.4} = \frac{7.674}{3.612} = 2.124.
$$

$$
\rho_3 = 2.124 \times 0.125 = 0.2655\;\text{kg/m}^3.
$$

#### Step 8: Expansion Fan Details

The head of the expansion fan moves left at speed $-c_L = -376.6$ m/s. The tail moves at speed $u_2 - c_2$. From the isentropic relation:

$$
c_2 = c_L\left(\frac{p^*}{p_L}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)} = 376.6 \times (0.3031)^{1/7} = 376.6 \times 0.8504 = 320.2\;\text{m/s}.
$$

Tail speed: $u_2 - c_2 = 293.0 - 320.2 = -27.2$ m/s (still moving left, but slowly).

**Final Answer:**

$$
p^* = 0.303\;\text{atm}, \quad u^* = 293\;\text{m/s}, \quad V_s = 558\;\text{m/s}, \quad \rho_3 = 0.266\;\text{kg/m}^3.
$$

</details>

### Example 8.3 — Prandtl-Meyer Expansion Fan

**Problem.** A supersonic flow at $M_1 = 2.0$ turns through an expansion corner of angle $\Delta\theta = 15°$. Find the downstream Mach number $M_2$ and the fan angle.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Prandtl-Meyer Function

The Prandtl-Meyer function $\nu(M)$ gives the total turning angle from $M = 1$ to Mach $M$:

$$
\nu(M) = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}(M^2-1)} - \arctan\sqrt{M^2-1}.
$$

For $\gamma = 1.4$: $\sqrt{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} = \sqrt{6} = 2.449$.

#### Step 2: Compute $\nu(M_1)$ at $M_1 = 2.0$

$$
\sqrt{M_1^2 - 1} = \sqrt{3} = 1.732.
$$

$$
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2-1)} = \sqrt{\frac{0.4}{2.4} \times 3} = \sqrt{0.5} = 0.7071.
$$

$$
\nu(2.0) = 2.449 \times \arctan(0.7071) - \arctan(1.732).
$$

$$
= 2.449 \times 35.26° - 60.0° = 86.37° - 60.0° = 26.38°.
$$

#### Step 3: Compute $\nu(M_2)$

For an expansion (convex corner), the flow turns away from itself and accelerates:

$$
\nu(M_2) = \nu(M_1) + \Delta\theta = 26.38° + 15° = 41.38°.
$$

#### Step 4: Invert the Prandtl-Meyer Function

We need $M_2$ such that $\nu(M_2) = 41.38°$. This requires numerical inversion (or tables).

Try $M_2 = 2.6$:

$$
\sqrt{M^2-1} = \sqrt{5.76} = 2.4, \quad \sqrt{0.1667 \times 5.76} = \sqrt{0.96} = 0.9798.
$$

$$
\nu(2.6) = 2.449 \times \arctan(0.9798) - \arctan(2.4) = 2.449 \times 44.42° - 67.38° = 108.8° - 67.38° = 41.4°.
$$

Excellent — $\nu(2.6) = 41.4° \approx 41.38°$. So $M_2 \approx 2.60$.

#### Step 5: Mach Angles

The Mach angle is $\mu = \arcsin(1/M)$:

$$
\mu_1 = \arcsin(1/2.0) = 30.0°.
$$

$$
\mu_2 = \arcsin(1/2.6) = 22.6°.
$$

The expansion fan is bounded by the upstream Mach line (at angle $\mu_1 = 30°$ to the upstream flow) and the downstream Mach line (at angle $\mu_2 = 22.6°$ to the downstream flow). The total fan opening angle is:

$$
\text{Fan angle} = (\mu_1 + \mu_2) - \Delta\theta = 30° + 22.6° - 15° = 37.6°.
$$

Wait — the fan angle is more precisely: the upstream Mach line makes angle $\mu_1$ with the upstream wall, and the downstream Mach line makes angle $\mu_2$ with the downstream wall. The angular spread of the fan is $\mu_1 + \Delta\theta - \mu_2 = 30° + 15° - 22.6° = 22.4°$... 

Actually, the fan spans from the first characteristic (at angle $\mu_1$ to the upstream flow direction) to the last characteristic (at angle $\mu_2$ to the downstream flow direction). Relative to the corner, the fan subtends:

$$
\text{Fan half-angle} = \mu_1 - 0 = 30° \text{ (first wave from upstream direction)},
$$

$$
\text{Last wave at } \Delta\theta + \mu_2 = 15° + 22.6° = 37.6° \text{ from upstream direction}.
$$

So the fan spans from $30°$ to $37.6°$ measured from the upstream flow direction — a narrow fan of $7.6°$ width.

#### Step 6: Pressure and Temperature Ratios (Isentropic)

Since the expansion is isentropic:

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\frac{1 + 0.2 M_1^2}{1 + 0.2 M_2^2}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} = \left(\frac{1.8}{2.352}\right)^{3.5} = (0.7653)^{3.5}.
$$

$\ln(0.7653) = -0.2674$, $3.5 \times (-0.2674) = -0.936$, $e^{-0.936} = 0.392$.

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 0.392.
$$

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{1.8}{2.352} = 0.765.
$$

**Final Answer:**

$$
M_2 = 2.60, \qquad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 0.392, \qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 0.765.
$$

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 Derivation of the Rankine-Hugoniot Relations from Conservation Laws

The Rankine-Hugoniot (R-H) relations are the jump conditions across a normal shock wave, derived from the integral conservation laws applied to a thin control volume straddling the shock.

**Setup.** Consider a stationary normal shock. In the shock-fixed frame, gas enters from the left (state 1: $\rho_1, u_1, p_1, h_1$) and exits to the right (state 2: $\rho_2, u_2, p_2, h_2$). The shock is infinitesimally thin — all changes occur discontinuously.

**Conservation of mass (continuity):**

$$
\rho_1 u_1 = \rho_2 u_2 \equiv \dot{m}. \tag{R-H 1}
$$

**Conservation of momentum:**

Apply Newton's second law to the control volume. The net force equals the momentum flux out minus in:

$$
p_1 + \rho_1 u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 u_2^2. \tag{R-H 2}
$$

(Pressure acts on both faces; momentum flux is $\rho u^2$ per unit area.)

**Conservation of energy:**

The total enthalpy $h_0 = h + u^2/2$ is conserved across the shock (no heat addition, no work):

$$
h_1 + \frac{u_1^2}{2} = h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2}. \tag{R-H 3}
$$

For a calorically perfect gas: $h = c_p T = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p}{\rho}$.

**Derivation of the pressure-density relation (Hugoniot curve).**

**Step 1:** From (R-H 1): $u_2 = (\rho_1/\rho_2)u_1$. Substitute into (R-H 2):

$$
p_1 + \rho_1 u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 \left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^2 u_1^2 = p_2 + \frac{\rho_1^2}{\rho_2}u_1^2.
$$

$$
p_2 - p_1 = \rho_1 u_1^2\left(1 - \frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right) = \rho_1 u_1^2 \cdot \frac{\rho_2 - \rho_1}{\rho_2}.
$$

**Step 2:** Solve for $u_1^2$:

$$
u_1^2 = \frac{(p_2 - p_1)\rho_2}{\rho_1(\rho_2 - \rho_1)}.
$$

Similarly: $u_2^2 = \frac{(p_2 - p_1)\rho_1}{\rho_2(\rho_2 - \rho_1)}$.

**Step 3:** Substitute into the energy equation (R-H 3). Using $h = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p}{\rho}$:

$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{1}{2}u_1^2 = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2} + \frac{1}{2}u_2^2.
$$

$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{p_1}{\rho_1} - \frac{p_2}{\rho_2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(u_2^2 - u_1^2).
$$

Substitute the expressions for $u_1^2$ and $u_2^2$:

$$
\frac{1}{2}(u_2^2 - u_1^2) = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{2(\rho_2 - \rho_1)}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} - \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right) = -\frac{(p_2-p_1)(\rho_2^2 - \rho_1^2)}{2\rho_1\rho_2(\rho_2-\rho_1)}.
$$

$$
= -\frac{(p_2-p_1)(\rho_1+\rho_2)}{2\rho_1\rho_2}.
$$

Setting this equal to the enthalpy difference:

$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{p_1}{\rho_1} - \frac{p_2}{\rho_2}\right) = -\frac{(p_2-p_1)(\rho_1+\rho_2)}{2\rho_1\rho_2}.
$$

$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{p_1\rho_2 - p_2\rho_1}{\rho_1\rho_2} = -\frac{(p_2-p_1)(\rho_1+\rho_2)}{2\rho_1\rho_2}.
$$

Multiply both sides by $\rho_1\rho_2$:

$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(p_1\rho_2 - p_2\rho_1) = -\frac{(p_2-p_1)(\rho_1+\rho_2)}{2}.
$$

**Step 4:** Solve for $\rho_2/\rho_1$ in terms of $p_2/p_1$. Let $r = \rho_2/\rho_1$ and $\pi = p_2/p_1$:

$$
\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}(r - \pi) = -(\pi - 1)(r + 1) \cdot r/r... 
$$

After algebraic manipulation (multiply out and collect terms in $r$):

$$
r = \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{(\gamma+1)\pi + (\gamma-1)}{(\gamma-1)\pi + (\gamma+1)} = \frac{(\gamma+1)p_2/p_1 + (\gamma-1)}{(\gamma-1)p_2/p_1 + (\gamma+1)}.
$$

This is the **Hugoniot relation** — it replaces the isentropic relation $p/\rho^\gamma = \text{const}$ across a shock.

**Step 5:** Express in terms of Mach number. From $u_1^2 = M_1^2 c_1^2 = M_1^2 \gamma p_1/\rho_1$ and the momentum equation:

$$
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_1^2 - 1).
$$

$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)M_1^2 + 2}.
$$

These are the standard normal shock relations used in Example 8.1.

**Key physical insight:** The Hugoniot curve lies *above* the isentrope through state 1 in the $p$-$v$ plane (where $v = 1/\rho$). This means the shock compresses the gas more than an isentropic compression to the same pressure — the excess goes into entropy production (irreversible heating).

**Limiting cases:**
- Weak shock ($M_1 \to 1$): the Hugoniot approaches the isentrope, and the entropy jump $\Delta s \propto (M_1^2 - 1)^3$ (third-order small).
- Strong shock ($M_1 \to \infty$): $\rho_2/\rho_1 \to (\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1) = 6$ for air. There is a maximum compression ratio — no matter how strong the shock, the density ratio cannot exceed 6 (for $\gamma = 1.4$).

### 9.2 The Method of Characteristics for Supersonic Flow

In supersonic flow, disturbances propagate along characteristic lines (Mach lines). The **method of characteristics** exploits this to solve 2D steady supersonic flow problems exactly.

**Mathematical basis.** The 2D steady Euler equations for irrotational, isentropic flow can be written as:

$$
(1 - M_x^2)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} - M_x M_y\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right) + (1 - M_y^2)\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0,
$$

where $M_x = u/c$, $M_y = v/c$. This is a quasi-linear PDE that is:
- **Elliptic** when $M < 1$ (subsonic) — no real characteristics, information propagates everywhere.
- **Hyperbolic** when $M > 1$ (supersonic) — two families of real characteristics exist.

**Characteristic directions.** The characteristics make angles $\pm\mu$ with the local streamline, where $\mu = \arcsin(1/M)$ is the Mach angle. Along these characteristics, the **Riemann invariants** are constant:

- Along $C^+$ characteristics (left-running): $\theta + \nu(M) = \text{const} = J^+$.
- Along $C^-$ characteristics (right-running): $\theta - \nu(M) = \text{const} = J^-$.

Here $\theta$ is the local flow angle and $\nu(M)$ is the Prandtl-Meyer function.

**Algorithm:**
1. Specify initial data along a non-characteristic curve (e.g., a nozzle exit).
2. At each point, draw $C^+$ and $C^-$ characteristics.
3. At intersections, use the constancy of Riemann invariants to solve for $\theta$ and $M$.
4. March downstream, building the solution point by point.

**Applications:**
- Design of supersonic nozzles (minimum-length nozzle for given exit Mach number).
- Computation of Prandtl-Meyer expansion fans (each characteristic carries a constant $J^-$).
- Interaction of expansion fans with shocks.
- Supersonic inlet design.

The method of characteristics was the primary tool for supersonic aerodynamic design before CFD, and it remains important for validation and for understanding wave interactions.

**References:** Anderson, *Modern Compressible Flow*, Ch. 11; Liepmann & Roshko, Ch. 4; NACA Report 1135.
