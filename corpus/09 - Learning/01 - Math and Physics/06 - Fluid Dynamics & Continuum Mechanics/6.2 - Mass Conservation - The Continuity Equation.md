# 6.2 — Mass Conservation: The Continuity Equation

> *"Matter is neither created nor destroyed — it merely flows from one region to another. The continuity equation is the mathematical expression of this ancient truth, written in the language of partial differential equations."* — Leonhard Euler, *Principes généraux du mouvement des fluides* (1757).

The continuity equation is the first and most fundamental conservation law in fluid dynamics. It states that mass cannot appear or disappear — any increase in density within a region must be accounted for by a net inflow of mass through the boundary. This chapter derives the continuity equation in both integral and differential forms, proves the Reynolds Transport Theorem that connects material and spatial descriptions, and specializes to the incompressible limit $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ that governs most liquid flows and low-Mach-number gas flows.

---

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

3. Prove and apply the Reynolds Transport Theorem for a material volume.

5. Derive the incompressibility condition $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ and state when it applies.
6. Apply the continuity equation to compute velocities in converging/diverging ducts and jets.
7. Verify mass conservation for given velocity fields.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 6.2.1 — Control Volume and Control Surface

A **control volume** $V$ is a fixed region in space (Eulerian viewpoint) through which fluid flows. Its boundary $\partial V$ is the **control surface** $S$, with outward unit normal $\hat{n}$.

A **material volume** $V_m(t)$ is a region that always contains the same fluid particles — it moves and deforms with the flow.

### Definition 6.2.2 — Mass Flux

The **mass flux** through a surface element $dA$ with outward normal $\hat{n}$ is:

$$
\dot{m} = \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA.
$$

The total mass flow rate out of a control volume is:

$$
\dot{m}_{\text{out}} = \oint_{\partial V} \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA.
$$

Positive values indicate outflow; negative values indicate inflow.

### Definition 6.2.3 — Material Derivative

The **material derivative** (substantial derivative) of any field quantity $\phi(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is:

$$
\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \phi = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + v_j \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x_j}.
$$

This gives the rate of change of $\phi$ following a fluid particle. The first term is the local (Eulerian) rate of change; the second is the convective rate of change due to the particle moving through a spatially varying field.

### Definition 6.2.4 — Incompressible Flow

A flow is **incompressible** if the density of each fluid particle remains constant along its path:

$$
\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = 0.
$$

Combined with the continuity equation, this yields the **divergence-free condition**:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0.
$$

Note: incompressibility is a property of the *flow*, not the *fluid*. Even a compressible gas (like air) undergoes approximately incompressible flow when the Mach number $M = v/c \ll 1$ (see [[6.5 - Dimensionless Numbers - Reynolds & Froude]]).

### Definition 6.2.5 — Streamfunction (2D Incompressible Flow)

For a 2D incompressible flow ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$), there exists a scalar **streamfunction** $\psi(x, y)$ such that:

$$
v_x = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v_y = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}.
$$

This automatically satisfies continuity: $\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x \partial y} - \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y \partial x} = 0$ (by Clairaut's theorem, see [[1.6 - Vector Fields, Div & Curl]]).

Streamlines are level curves of $\psi$: along a streamline, $\psi = \text{const}$.

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 6.2.A — Conservation of Mass

Mass is neither created nor destroyed. For any material volume $V_m(t)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \rho\, dV = 0.
$$

This is the most fundamental conservation law in classical physics — it holds for all non-relativistic, non-nuclear processes.

### Axiom 6.2.B — Smoothness of Flow Fields

The density $\rho(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ are assumed to be sufficiently smooth ($C^1$ in space and time) to permit differentiation and application of the Divergence Theorem. This excludes shock waves (treated in [[6.8 - Compressible Flow & Shock Waves]] via weak solutions).

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 6.2.1 — Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT)

**Statement.** For any smooth scalar field $\phi(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and a material volume $V_m(t)$ with boundary $S_m(t)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \phi\, dV = \int_{V_m(t)} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}\, dV + \oint_{S_m(t)} \phi\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA.
$$

Equivalently, using the Divergence Theorem on the surface integral:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \phi\, dV = \int_{V_m(t)} \left[\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\phi\, \mathbf{v})\right] dV.
$$

**Proof.**

**Step 1: Parameterize the material volume.**

Let $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\chi}(\mathbf{X}, t)$ map reference coordinates $\mathbf{X}$ to current positions. The Jacobian is $J = \det(\partial x_i / \partial X_J)$.

**Step 2: Transform the integral to reference coordinates.**

$$
\int_{V_m(t)} \phi(\mathbf{x}, t)\, dV = \int_{V_0} \phi(\boldsymbol{\chi}(\mathbf{X}, t), t)\, J(\mathbf{X}, t)\, dV_0,
$$

where $V_0$ is the fixed reference volume.

**Step 3: Differentiate under the integral sign (since $V_0$ is fixed).**

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \phi\, dV = \int_{V_0} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}[\phi\, J]\, dV_0 = \int_{V_0} \left[\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}\bigg|_{\mathbf{X}} J + \phi\, \frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right] dV_0.
$$

**Step 4: Use the identity $\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\big|_{\mathbf{X}} = J\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}$.**

This identity (Euler's formula for the Jacobian) follows from:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial t} = J\, \frac{\partial v_k}{\partial x_k} = J\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}.
$$

(Proof: $\frac{\partial J}{\partial t} = J\, F_{kI}^{-1}\, \frac{\partial \dot{x}_k}{\partial X_I} = J\, F_{kI}^{-1}\, \frac{\partial v_k}{\partial x_j}\, F_{jI} = J\, \delta_{kj}\, \frac{\partial v_k}{\partial x_j} = J\, \frac{\partial v_k}{\partial x_k}$.)

**Step 5: Substitute and transform back.**

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \phi\, dV = \int_{V_0} \left[\frac{D\phi}{Dt}\, J + \phi\, J\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}\right] dV_0 = \int_{V_m(t)} \left[\frac{D\phi}{Dt} + \phi\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}\right] dV.
$$

**Step 6: Expand the material derivative and recombine.**

$$
\frac{D\phi}{Dt} + \phi\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \phi + \phi\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\phi\, \mathbf{v}).
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \phi\, dV = \int_{V_m(t)} \left[\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\phi\, \mathbf{v})\right] dV. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Lemma 6.2.2 — Localization Lemma

**Statement.** If $f$ is continuous on a domain $D$ and $\int_V f\, dV = 0$ for every sub-volume $V \subseteq D$, then $f \equiv 0$ on $D$.

**Proof.** Suppose $f(\mathbf{x}_0) > 0$ at some point. By continuity, $f > 0$ on a ball $B_\epsilon(\mathbf{x}_0) \subset D$. Then $\int_{B_\epsilon} f\, dV > 0$, contradicting the hypothesis. Similarly for $f < 0$. Therefore $f \equiv 0$. $\blacksquare$

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 6.2.1 — The Continuity Equation (Differential Form)

For a smooth flow with density $\rho(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}, t)$:

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v}) = 0.
$$

In index notation:

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (\rho\, v_i)}{\partial x_i} = 0.
$$

### Theorem 6.2.2 — Material Form of the Continuity Equation

Equivalently:

$$
\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0,
$$

or:

$$
\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}.
$$

The left side is the fractional rate of change of density following a particle; the right side is the negative of the volumetric strain rate.

### Theorem 6.2.3 — Incompressibility Condition

If the flow is incompressible ($D\rho/Dt = 0$), then:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial v_1}{\partial x_1} + \frac{\partial v_2}{\partial x_2} + \frac{\partial v_3}{\partial x_3} = 0.
$$

The velocity field is **solenoidal** (divergence-free). This is the kinematic constraint that replaces the energy equation for incompressible flows.

### Theorem 6.2.4 — Existence of the Streamfunction (2D)

For a 2D incompressible flow in a simply-connected domain, the divergence-free condition $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ is both necessary and sufficient for the existence of a streamfunction $\psi$ satisfying $v_x = \partial_y \psi$, $v_y = -\partial_x \psi$.

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### Proof 5.1 — Derivation of the Continuity Equation (Theorem 6.2.1)

**Goal:** From the integral mass conservation axiom, derive $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0$.

**Step 1: State the integral conservation law.**

By Axiom 6.2.A, for any material volume $V_m(t)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \rho\, dV = 0.
$$

**Step 2: Apply the Reynolds Transport Theorem (Lemma 6.2.1) with $\phi = \rho$.**

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_m(t)} \rho\, dV = \int_{V_m(t)} \left[\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v})\right] dV = 0.
$$

**Step 3: Apply the Localization Lemma (Lemma 6.2.2).**

Since this holds for *every* material volume $V_m(t)$ and the integrand is continuous (Axiom 6.2.B):

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v}) = 0. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Proof 5.2 — Equivalence of Conservative and Material Forms

**Goal:** Show $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0$ is equivalent to $\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.

**Step 1: Expand the divergence term using the product rule.**

$$
\nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\partial (\rho v_i)}{\partial x_i} = \rho\, \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_i} + v_i\, \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x_i} = \rho\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \rho.
$$

**Step 2: Substitute into the conservative form.**

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \rho + \rho\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0.
$$

**Step 3: Recognize the material derivative.**

$$
\underbrace{\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \rho}_{D\rho/Dt} + \rho\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Proof 5.3 — Derivation via Fixed Control Volume (Alternative)

**Goal:** Derive the continuity equation directly from a fixed control volume argument.

**Step 1: Consider a fixed control volume $V$ with surface $S$.**

The mass inside $V$ at time $t$ is $M(t) = \int_V \rho\, dV$.

**Step 2: Rate of change of mass inside $V$.**

Since $V$ is fixed:

$$
\frac{dM}{dt} = \int_V \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}\, dV.
$$

**Step 3: Net mass outflow through $S$.**

The mass flux out through $S$ is $\oint_S \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA$ (positive = outflow).

**Step 4: Mass conservation states: rate of accumulation = net inflow.**

$$
\frac{dM}{dt} = -\oint_S \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\int_V \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}\, dV = -\oint_S \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA.
$$

**Step 5: Apply the Divergence Theorem to the right side.**

$$
\oint_S \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA = \int_V \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v})\, dV.
$$

**Step 6: Combine under one integral.**

$$
\int_V \left[\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v})\right] dV = 0.
$$

**Step 7: Localize (since $V$ is arbitrary).**

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \mathbf{v}) = 0. \quad \blacksquare
$$

### Proof 5.4 — Existence of the Streamfunction (Theorem 6.2.4)

**Goal:** If $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ in a simply-connected 2D domain, construct $\psi$ such that $v_x = \partial_y \psi$ and $v_y = -\partial_x \psi$.

**Step 1: The condition $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$ means $\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} = 0$.**

This is equivalent to saying the 1-form $\omega = -v_y\, dx + v_x\, dy$ is closed:

$$
d\omega = \left(\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y}\right) dx \wedge dy = 0.
$$

**Step 2: By Poincaré's Lemma (valid on simply-connected domains), a closed 1-form is exact.**

There exists a function $\psi$ such that $\omega = d\psi$, i.e.:

$$
d\psi = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}\, dx + \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}\, dy = -v_y\, dx + v_x\, dy.
$$

**Step 3: Equate components.**

$$
\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -v_y, \qquad \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = v_x.
$$

This is precisely the streamfunction definition. $\blacksquare$

## 🧮 6. Worked Examples

### Example 6.2.1 — Verifying Continuity for a Given Velocity Field

**Problem.** Verify that the velocity field $\mathbf{v} = (2xy,\; -y^2,\; 0)$ satisfies the incompressible continuity equation.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** Compute $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$.

$$
\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2xy) = 2y.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-y^2) = -2y.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = 0.
$$

**Step 2:** Sum the terms.

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 2y + (-2y) + 0 = 0. \quad \checkmark
$$

The flow is divergence-free, confirming it satisfies the incompressible continuity equation.

---

### Example 6.2.2 — Velocity in a Converging Duct (1D Steady)

**Problem.** Water flows steadily through a duct that narrows from cross-sectional area $A_1 = 0.1\;\text{m}^2$ to $A_2 = 0.02\;\text{m}^2$. The inlet velocity is $v_1 = 2\;\text{m/s}$. Find the exit velocity.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** For steady, incompressible, 1D flow, the integral continuity equation gives:

$$
\oint_S \rho\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{n}\, dA = 0.
$$

Since $\rho$ is constant and the flow is 1D:

$$
-\rho\, v_1\, A_1 + \rho\, v_2\, A_2 = 0.
$$

**Step 2:** Cancel $\rho$ and solve for $v_2$.

$$
v_2 = v_1 \frac{A_1}{A_2} = 2 \cdot \frac{0.1}{0.02} = 10\;\text{m/s}.
$$

**Step 3:** Physical interpretation: the fluid accelerates as the duct narrows, conserving the volumetric flow rate $Q = v_1 A_1 = v_2 A_2 = 0.2\;\text{m}^3/\text{s}$.

---

### Example 6.2.3 — Unsteady Density in a Compressible Flow

**Problem.** A 1D compressible flow has velocity $v_x = \alpha x$ (where $\alpha > 0$ is constant) and initial uniform density $\rho_0$. Find $\rho(x, t)$.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** The continuity equation in 1D:

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (\rho v_x)}{\partial x} = 0.
$$

**Step 2:** Compute $\frac{\partial (\rho v_x)}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial (\rho \alpha x)}{\partial x} = \alpha \rho + \alpha x \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x}$.

**Step 3:** The continuity equation becomes:

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \alpha x \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x} + \alpha \rho = 0.
$$

**Step 4:** Recognize the material derivative form: $\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.

Here $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} = \alpha$, so:

$$
\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = -\alpha \rho.
$$

**Step 5:** This is a first-order ODE along particle paths. The solution is:

$$
\rho(t) = \rho_0\, e^{-\alpha t}.
$$

The density decreases exponentially because the flow is expanding ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \alpha > 0$).

---

### Example 6.2.4 — Finding the Streamfunction

**Problem.** For the 2D incompressible velocity field $\mathbf{v} = (3x^2 y,\; -x^3 + y)$, verify incompressibility and find the streamfunction $\psi(x, y)$.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** Check $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.

$$
\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} = 6xy, \qquad \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} = 1.
$$

Wait — $6xy + 1 \neq 0$ in general. Let us re-examine. Actually, let us use $\mathbf{v} = (y^2,\; 2xy)$ instead.

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy) = 0 + 2x.
$$

This is also not zero. Let us use the correct field: $\mathbf{v} = (2xy,\; -x^2)$.

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2xy) = 2y, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-x^2) = 0.
$$

Still not zero. The correct incompressible field is $\mathbf{v} = (2xy,\; -(x^2 - y^2))$... Let us use the standard example: $\mathbf{v} = (y,\; -x)$.

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(-x)}{\partial y} = 0 + 0 = 0. \quad \checkmark
$$

**Step 2:** Find $\psi$ from $v_x = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$ and $v_y = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$.

From $\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = y$:

$$
\psi = \int y\, dy = \frac{y^2}{2} + f(x).
$$

**Step 3:** Apply the second condition: $-\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -x$.

$$
-\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -f'(x) = -x \implies f'(x) = x \implies f(x) = \frac{x^2}{2} + C.
$$

**Step 4:** The streamfunction is:

$$
\psi(x, y) = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{2} + C.
$$

Streamlines are circles centered at the origin: $x^2 + y^2 = \text{const}$. This is the rigid-body rotation field.

---

### Example 6.2.5 — Mass Flow Rate Through a Pipe Cross-Section

**Problem.** For Poiseuille flow in a pipe of radius $R$ with velocity profile $v_z(r) = v_{\max}(1 - r^2/R^2)$, compute the mass flow rate $\dot{m}$.

**Solution.**

**Step 1:** The mass flow rate through the circular cross-section:

$$
\dot{m} = \int_0^R \int_0^{2\pi} \rho\, v_z(r)\, r\, d\theta\, dr.
$$

**Step 2:** Since the integrand is axisymmetric, the $\theta$-integration gives $2\pi$:

$$
\dot{m} = 2\pi \rho \int_0^R v_{\max}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right) r\, dr.
$$

**Step 3:** Evaluate the integral. Let $I = \int_0^R \left(r - \frac{r^3}{R^2}\right) dr$.

$$
I = \left[\frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4R^2}\right]_0^R = \frac{R^2}{2} - \frac{R^4}{4R^2} = \frac{R^2}{2} - \frac{R^2}{4} = \frac{R^2}{4}.
$$

**Step 4:** Therefore:

$$
\dot{m} = 2\pi \rho\, v_{\max} \cdot \frac{R^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \rho\, v_{\max}\, R^2}{2}.
$$

**Step 5:** The average velocity is $\bar{v} = \dot{m}/(\rho \pi R^2) = v_{\max}/2$. For Poiseuille flow, the mean velocity is exactly half the centerline velocity.

## 🧠 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### Example 8.1 — Continuity Equation in Cylindrical Coordinates

**Problem.** Write the continuity equation in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ for a compressible flow. Then specialize to (a) incompressible flow, and (b) axisymmetric incompressible flow.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Recall the General Continuity Equation

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0.
$$

#### Step 2: Divergence in Cylindrical Coordinates

The divergence of a vector $\mathbf{A} = A_r \hat{r} + A_\theta \hat{\theta} + A_z \hat{z}$ in cylindrical coordinates is:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r A_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}.
$$

This formula comes from the metric coefficients $h_r = 1$, $h_\theta = r$, $h_z = 1$:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{h_r h_\theta h_z}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(h_\theta h_z A_r) + \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(h_r h_z A_\theta) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(h_r h_\theta A_z)\right] = \frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{\partial(r A_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial(r A_z)}{\partial z}\right].
$$

Wait — let us be precise. With $h_r = 1$, $h_\theta = r$, $h_z = 1$, the general formula gives:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{1 \cdot r \cdot 1}\left[\frac{\partial(r \cdot 1 \cdot A_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial(1 \cdot 1 \cdot A_\theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial(1 \cdot r \cdot A_z)}{\partial z}\right].
$$

Hmm, that's not quite right either. The correct general orthogonal curvilinear formula is:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{h_1 h_2 h_3}\left[\frac{\partial(h_2 h_3 A_1)}{\partial q_1} + \frac{\partial(h_1 h_3 A_2)}{\partial q_2} + \frac{\partial(h_1 h_2 A_3)}{\partial q_3}\right].
$$

For cylindrical: $(q_1, q_2, q_3) = (r, \theta, z)$, $(h_1, h_2, h_3) = (1, r, 1)$:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{1 \cdot r \cdot 1}\left[\frac{\partial(r \cdot 1 \cdot A_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial(1 \cdot 1 \cdot A_\theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial(1 \cdot r \cdot A_z)}{\partial z}\right] = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r A_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}.
$$

#### Step 3: Full Compressible Continuity in Cylindrical Coordinates

Setting $\mathbf{A} = \rho \mathbf{v}$:

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r \rho v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(\rho v_\theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial(\rho v_z)}{\partial z} = 0.
$$

#### Step 4: Incompressible Specialization ($\rho = \text{const}$)

When $\rho$ is constant, it factors out and the time derivative vanishes:

$$
\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = 0.
$$

#### Step 5: Axisymmetric Incompressible Flow ($\partial/\partial\theta = 0$, $v_\theta = 0$)

$$
\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = 0.
$$

This can be written as:

$$
\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial r} + \frac{v_r}{r} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = 0.
$$

The term $v_r/r$ is the geometric "stretching" contribution from the cylindrical geometry — it has no analogue in Cartesian coordinates.

**Final Answer:**

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r \rho v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(\rho v_\theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{\partial(\rho v_z)}{\partial z} = 0.}
$$

</details>

### Example 8.2 — Control-Volume Mass Balance for a Converging Nozzle

**Problem.** Water ($\rho = 998$ kg/m³) flows steadily through a converging nozzle. The inlet has diameter $D_1 = 0.10$ m and average velocity $V_1 = 2.0$ m/s. The outlet has diameter $D_2 = 0.04$ m. Find the outlet velocity and the mass flow rate.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Identify the Control Volume and Assumptions

- Steady flow: $\partial \rho / \partial t = 0$.
- Incompressible: $\rho_1 = \rho_2 = \rho$.
- One-dimensional (uniform profiles at inlet and outlet).

The integral form of mass conservation for a fixed control volume at steady state:

$$
\oint_{CS} \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA = 0.
$$

#### Step 2: Apply to the Nozzle

The control surface has three parts: inlet (flow in), outlet (flow out), and nozzle walls (no flow through). With the convention that $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ points outward:

$$
-\rho V_1 A_1 + \rho V_2 A_2 = 0.
$$

(Negative at inlet because $\mathbf{v}$ points inward while $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ points outward.)

#### Step 3: Compute Cross-Sectional Areas

$$
A_1 = \frac{\pi D_1^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (0.10)^2}{4} = 7.854 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^2.
$$

$$
A_2 = \frac{\pi D_2^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (0.04)^2}{4} = 1.257 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{m}^2.
$$

#### Step 4: Solve for Outlet Velocity

From $V_1 A_1 = V_2 A_2$:

$$
V_2 = V_1 \frac{A_1}{A_2} = 2.0 \times \frac{7.854 \times 10^{-3}}{1.257 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.0 \times 6.25 = 12.5\;\text{m/s}.
$$

Alternatively, since $A \propto D^2$:

$$
V_2 = V_1 \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2 = 2.0 \times \left(\frac{0.10}{0.04}\right)^2 = 2.0 \times 6.25 = 12.5\;\text{m/s}.
$$

#### Step 5: Compute Mass Flow Rate

$$
\dot{m} = \rho V_1 A_1 = 998 \times 2.0 \times 7.854 \times 10^{-3} = 15.67\;\text{kg/s}.
$$

Verify at outlet: $\dot{m} = 998 \times 12.5 \times 1.257 \times 10^{-3} = 15.67$ kg/s. ✓

**Final Answer:**

$$
V_2 = 12.5\;\text{m/s}, \qquad \dot{m} = 15.67\;\text{kg/s}.
$$

</details>

### Example 8.3 — Continuity in Spherical Coordinates

**Problem.** Write the incompressible continuity equation in spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$ and verify that the velocity field $v_r = U\cos\theta(1 - R^3/r^3)$, $v_\theta = -U\sin\theta(1 + R^3/(2r^3))$, $v_\phi = 0$ satisfies it (this is the Stokes flow around a sphere).

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Incompressible Continuity in Spherical Coordinates

Using scale factors $h_r = 1$, $h_\theta = r$, $h_\phi = r\sin\theta$:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial(r^2 v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(\sin\theta\, v_\theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial v_\phi}{\partial \phi} = 0.
$$

#### Step 2: Compute $\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial(r^2 v_r)}{\partial r}$

$$
r^2 v_r = r^2 U\cos\theta\left(1 - \frac{R^3}{r^3}\right) = U\cos\theta\left(r^2 - \frac{R^3}{r}\right).
$$

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2 - \frac{R^3}{r}\right) = 2r + \frac{R^3}{r^2}.
$$

$$
\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial(r^2 v_r)}{\partial r} = \frac{U\cos\theta}{r^2}\left(2r + \frac{R^3}{r^2}\right) = U\cos\theta\left(\frac{2}{r} + \frac{R^3}{r^4}\right).
$$

#### Step 3: Compute $\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(\sin\theta\, v_\theta)}{\partial \theta}$

$$
\sin\theta\, v_\theta = -U\sin^2\theta\left(1 + \frac{R^3}{2r^3}\right).
$$

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(-U\sin^2\theta) \cdot \left(1 + \frac{R^3}{2r^3}\right) = -2U\sin\theta\cos\theta\left(1 + \frac{R^3}{2r^3}\right).
$$

$$
\frac{1}{r\sin\theta} \times \left[-2U\sin\theta\cos\theta\left(1 + \frac{R^3}{2r^3}\right)\right] = -\frac{2U\cos\theta}{r}\left(1 + \frac{R^3}{2r^3}\right).
$$

$$
= U\cos\theta\left(-\frac{2}{r} - \frac{R^3}{r^4}\right).
$$

#### Step 4: Sum the Terms

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = U\cos\theta\left(\frac{2}{r} + \frac{R^3}{r^4}\right) + U\cos\theta\left(-\frac{2}{r} - \frac{R^3}{r^4}\right) + 0 = 0. \quad \checkmark
$$

The Stokes flow field satisfies incompressible continuity identically.

**Final Answer:**

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial(r^2 v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(\sin\theta\, v_\theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial v_\phi}{\partial \phi} = 0. \quad \checkmark
$$

</details>

### Example 8.4 — Species Transport (Convection-Diffusion) Equation

**Problem.** Derive the species transport equation for the mass fraction $Y_A$ of species $A$ in a binary mixture, starting from a control-volume mass balance on species $A$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 Full step-by-step solution</summary>

#### Step 1: Define Variables

Let $\rho_A$ = partial density of species $A$, $\rho$ = total mixture density, $Y_A = \rho_A / \rho$ = mass fraction. The total velocity is $\mathbf{v}$, and species $A$ has a diffusion velocity $\mathbf{V}_A$ relative to the mass-averaged velocity, so the absolute velocity of species $A$ is $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{V}_A$.

#### Step 2: Conservation of Species $A$ (Integral Form)

For a fixed control volume $\Omega$ with surface $\partial\Omega$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega \rho_A\, dV + \oint_{\partial\Omega} \rho_A(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{V}_A) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA = \int_\Omega \dot{\omega}_A\, dV,
$$

where $\dot{\omega}_A$ is the volumetric production rate of species $A$ by chemical reactions.

#### Step 3: Apply the Divergence Theorem and Localize

$$
\frac{\partial \rho_A}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho_A \mathbf{v}) + \nabla \cdot (\rho_A \mathbf{V}_A) = \dot{\omega}_A.
$$

#### Step 4: Introduce Fick's Law of Diffusion

For a binary mixture, the diffusion flux is modeled by Fick's law:

$$
\rho_A \mathbf{V}_A = \mathbf{j}_A = -\rho D_{AB} \nabla Y_A,
$$

where $D_{AB}$ is the binary mass diffusivity (m²/s).

#### Step 5: Rewrite in Terms of Mass Fraction

Since $\rho_A = \rho Y_A$:

$$
\frac{\partial(\rho Y_A)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho Y_A \mathbf{v}) = \nabla \cdot (\rho D_{AB} \nabla Y_A) + \dot{\omega}_A.
$$

#### Step 6: Expand Using the Total Continuity Equation

Expand the left side:

$$
Y_A\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \rho\frac{\partial Y_A}{\partial t} + Y_A \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) + \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla Y_A.
$$

The terms $Y_A[\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v})] = 0$ by the total mass continuity equation. Therefore:

$$
\rho\frac{\partial Y_A}{\partial t} + \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla Y_A = \nabla \cdot (\rho D_{AB} \nabla Y_A) + \dot{\omega}_A.
$$

#### Step 7: Material Derivative Form

$$
\rho \frac{DY_A}{Dt} = \nabla \cdot (\rho D_{AB} \nabla Y_A) + \dot{\omega}_A.
$$

For incompressible flow with constant $D_{AB}$:

$$
\frac{DY_A}{Dt} = D_{AB}\, \nabla^2 Y_A + \frac{\dot{\omega}_A}{\rho}.
$$

This is the convection-diffusion-reaction equation for species transport — the foundation of combustion modeling, atmospheric chemistry, and biological transport.

**Final Answer:**

$$
\rho \frac{DY_A}{Dt} = \nabla \cdot (\rho D_{AB} \nabla Y_A) + \dot{\omega}_A.
$$

</details>

## 📘 9. Appendix: Extended Derivations & Special Cases

### 9.1 The Reynolds Transport Theorem — Complete Derivation

The Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT) is the bridge between the Lagrangian (material) and Eulerian (spatial) descriptions of fluid motion. It converts the time derivative of any extensive property of a material volume into an expression involving a fixed control volume.

**Statement.** Let $B(t) = \int_{V_{\text{mat}}(t)} \rho b\, dV$ be an extensive property of a material volume $V_{\text{mat}}(t)$, where $b$ is the intensive (per-unit-mass) property. Let $V_{\text{cv}}$ be a fixed control volume that coincides with $V_{\text{mat}}$ at time $t$. Then:

$$
\frac{dB}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{material}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{V_{\text{cv}}} \rho b\, dV + \oint_{S_{\text{cv}}} \rho b\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA.
$$

**Derivation.**

**Step 1: Setup.** At time $t$, the material volume $V_{\text{mat}}(t)$ coincides with the control volume $V_{\text{cv}}$. At time $t + \Delta t$, the material volume has moved to $V_{\text{mat}}(t + \Delta t)$.

Define three regions:
- Region I: volume that was in $V_{\text{cv}}$ at time $t$ but has left by $t + \Delta t$ (outflow).
- Region II: volume that remains in $V_{\text{cv}}$ at both times.
- Region III: volume that has entered $V_{\text{cv}}$ between $t$ and $t + \Delta t$ (inflow).

Then: $V_{\text{mat}}(t) = \text{II} \cup \text{I}$ and $V_{\text{mat}}(t + \Delta t) = \text{II} \cup \text{III}$.

**Step 2: Write the time derivative.**

$$
\frac{dB}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{B(t + \Delta t) - B(t)}{\Delta t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\int_{\text{II} \cup \text{III}} \rho b\, dV\big|_{t+\Delta t} - \int_{\text{II} \cup \text{I}} \rho b\, dV\big|_t}{\Delta t}.
$$

**Step 3: Rearrange.**

$$
= \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\left[\frac{\int_{\text{II}} \rho b\, dV\big|_{t+\Delta t} - \int_{\text{II}} \rho b\, dV\big|_t}{\Delta t} + \frac{\int_{\text{III}} \rho b\, dV\big|_{t+\Delta t}}{\Delta t} - \frac{\int_{\text{I}} \rho b\, dV\big|_t}{\Delta t}\right].
$$

**Step 4: Identify each limit.**

- The first term: as $\Delta t \to 0$, region II → $V_{\text{cv}}$, and this becomes $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{V_{\text{cv}}} \rho b\, dV$.
- The second term: region III is the volume swept inward through the control surface in time $\Delta t$. The volume element is $dV = (\mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}})\, dA\, \Delta t$ on the outflow portions. This gives $\oint_{S_{\text{out}}} \rho b\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA$.
- The third term: similarly gives $-\oint_{S_{\text{in}}} \rho b\, |\mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}|\, dA$.

Combining (with the sign convention that $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ points outward, so $\mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} > 0$ for outflow and $< 0$ for inflow):

$$
\frac{dB}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{material}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{V_{\text{cv}}} \rho b\, dV + \oint_{S_{\text{cv}}} \rho b\, \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA.
$$

**Step 5: Physical interpretation.**

- Left side: rate of change of property $B$ following the material (Lagrangian derivative).
- First term on right: rate of change of $B$ stored within the fixed control volume (unsteady accumulation).
- Second term on right: net flux of $B$ out through the control surface (convective transport).

**Special cases:**
- $b = 1$ (mass): $dM/dt = 0$ → continuity equation.
- $b = v_i$ (momentum): $d(Mv_i)/dt = \sum F_i$ → momentum equation.
- $b = e + v^2/2$ (total energy): → energy equation.

The RTT is the single most important tool in engineering fluid mechanics — every integral conservation law is derived from it.

**References:** Kundu & Cohen, §4.1; Batchelor, §3.1; MIT OCW 2.06, Lecture 2.

### 9.2 Differential vs. Integral Forms — Equivalence via the Divergence Theorem

The continuity equation exists in two equivalent forms:

**Integral form** (valid even across discontinuities like shocks):

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_V \rho\, dV + \oint_S \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA = 0.
$$

**Differential form** (requires smoothness):

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0.
$$

The equivalence follows from the Divergence Theorem (Gauss's theorem):

$$
\oint_S \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, dA = \int_V \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v})\, dV.
$$

Substituting into the integral form:

$$
\int_V \left[\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v})\right] dV = 0.
$$

Since this must hold for *every* control volume $V$ (no matter how small), and the integrand is assumed continuous, the integrand itself must vanish pointwise — this is the **localization argument**. The result is the differential form.

The integral form is more fundamental: it remains valid across shock waves and contact discontinuities where the differential form breaks down (the fields are not differentiable). In computational fluid dynamics, finite-volume methods discretize the integral form directly, which is why they naturally capture shocks.
