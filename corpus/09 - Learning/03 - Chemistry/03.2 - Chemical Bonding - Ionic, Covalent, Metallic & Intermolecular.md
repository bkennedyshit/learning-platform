# 03.2 — Chemical Bonding: Ionic, Covalent, Metallic & Intermolecular

> *"The nature of the chemical bond is the problem at the heart of all chemistry."* — Linus Pauling, *The Nature of the Chemical Bond* (1939)

Chemical bonding is quantum mechanics applied to multi-atom systems. An ionic bond is electrostatic attraction between ions. A covalent bond is the constructive interference of atomic wavefunctions. A metallic bond is electron delocalization across a lattice. This chapter derives all three from first principles, using the linear algebra you already know.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Derive the molecular orbital (MO) energy levels for H₂⁺ using the LCAO method (a 2×2 eigenvalue problem).
2. Construct MO diagrams for homonuclear and heteronuclear diatomic molecules.
3. Predict bond order, magnetic properties, and relative bond strengths from MO theory.
4. Apply VSEPR theory to predict molecular geometry from electron pair repulsion.
5. Distinguish ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding using electronegativity differences.
6. Explain intermolecular forces (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding) and their consequences.
7. Connect orbital hybridization (sp, sp², sp³) to the geometry of carbon compounds.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 03.2.1 — Chemical Bond

A **chemical bond** is a lasting attraction between atoms that enables the formation of molecules. It occurs when the total energy of the bonded system is lower than the separated atoms.

### Definition 03.2.2 — Bond Order

$$
\text{Bond Order} = \frac{(\text{electrons in bonding MOs}) - (\text{electrons in antibonding MOs})}{2}
$$

### Definition 03.2.3 — LCAO (Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals)

Molecular orbitals are constructed as linear combinations of atomic orbitals:

$$
\psi_{\text{MO}} = c_1\phi_1 + c_2\phi_2 + \cdots + c_N\phi_N
$$

Finding the coefficients $c_i$ is an eigenvalue problem — exactly the matrix diagonalization from [[2.6 - Eigenvalues Eigenvectors & Diagonalization]].

### Definition 03.2.4 — Overlap Integral

$$
S_{ij} = \int \phi_i^*(\mathbf{r})\,\phi_j(\mathbf{r})\,d^3r
$$

Measures how much two atomic orbitals overlap in space. $S = 0$ means orthogonal; $S = 1$ means identical.

### Definition 03.2.5 — Electronegativity Difference and Bond Type

| $\Delta\chi$ | Bond Type |
|---|---|
| $< 0.4$ | Nonpolar covalent |
| $0.4 - 1.7$ | Polar covalent |
| $> 1.7$ | Ionic |

### Definition 03.2.6 — Hybridization

**Hybridization** is the mixing of atomic orbitals on the same atom to form equivalent hybrid orbitals optimized for bonding geometry:
- $sp$: 2 hybrid orbitals, linear (180°)
- $sp^2$: 3 hybrid orbitals, trigonal planar (120°)
- $sp^3$: 4 hybrid orbitals, tetrahedral (109.5°)

## 📐 2. Mathematical Foundations

### 2.1 The LCAO-MO Method: H₂⁺ as a 2×2 Eigenvalue Problem

The simplest molecule: H₂⁺ (one electron, two protons). Trial wavefunction:

$$
\psi = c_1\phi_{1s}^A + c_2\phi_{1s}^B
$$

The secular equation (from the variational principle):

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} H_{AA} - ES_{AA} & H_{AB} - ES_{AB} \\ H_{BA} - ES_{BA} & H_{BB} - ES_{BB} \end{pmatrix} = 0
$$

By symmetry: $H_{AA} = H_{BB} \equiv \alpha$ (Coulomb integral), $H_{AB} = H_{BA} \equiv \beta$ (resonance integral), $S_{AA} = S_{BB} = 1$, $S_{AB} = S_{BA} \equiv S$.

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} \alpha - E & \beta - ES \\ \beta - ES & \alpha - E \end{pmatrix} = 0
$$

$$
(\alpha - E)^2 - (\beta - ES)^2 = 0
$$

$$
\alpha - E = \pm(\beta - ES)
$$

**Solutions:**

$$
E_+ = \frac{\alpha + \beta}{1 + S} \quad \text{(bonding)}, \qquad E_- = \frac{\alpha - \beta}{1 - S} \quad \text{(antibonding)}
$$

Since $\beta < 0$ (attractive interaction) and $S > 0$: $E_+ < \alpha < E_-$.

**Eigenvectors:**
- Bonding: $\psi_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+S)}}(\phi_A + \phi_B)$ — constructive interference
- Antibonding: $\psi_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-S)}}(\phi_A - \phi_B)$ — destructive interference

### 2.2 The Hückel Approximation (Simplified MO Theory)

For larger molecules, simplify: set $S = 0$ (neglect overlap) and only consider nearest-neighbor interactions. The secular equation becomes:

$$
\det(\mathbf{H} - E\mathbf{I}) = 0
$$

This is literally the eigenvalue problem from [[2.6 - Eigenvalues Eigenvectors & Diagonalization]].

**Example: Butadiene** (4 carbon $p_z$ orbitals in conjugation):

$$
\mathbf{H} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta & 0 & 0 \\ \beta & \alpha & \beta & 0 \\ 0 & \beta & \alpha & \beta \\ 0 & 0 & \beta & \alpha \end{pmatrix}
$$

Eigenvalues: $E_k = \alpha + 2\beta\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{5}\right)$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, 4$.

### 2.3 Born-Oppenheimer Approximation

Since nuclei are ~2000× heavier than electrons, we separate nuclear and electronic motion:

$$
\Psi_{\text{total}}(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{r}) \approx \psi_{\text{elec}}(\mathbf{r}; \mathbf{R}) \cdot \chi_{\text{nuc}}(\mathbf{R})
$$

Solve the electronic Schrödinger equation at fixed nuclear positions $\mathbf{R}$ → get the potential energy surface $E(\mathbf{R})$ → nuclei move on this surface.

### 2.4 Lattice Energy (Ionic Bonding)

For an ionic crystal, the lattice energy is:

$$
U_{\text{lattice}} = -\frac{N_A M Z^+ Z^- e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_0}\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)
$$

where $M$ is the Madelung constant (depends on crystal structure: 1.748 for NaCl), $r_0$ is the equilibrium interionic distance, and $n$ is the Born exponent (~8–12).

## 🔬 3. Chemical Mechanisms

### 3.1 Constructing MO Diagrams

**Step 1:** Identify the atomic orbitals that can combine (same symmetry, similar energy).

**Step 2:** Form bonding (lower energy, constructive) and antibonding (higher energy, destructive) combinations.

**Step 3:** Fill electrons from lowest MO upward (Aufbau + Pauli + Hund).

**Step 4:** Calculate bond order.

**For O₂:** Configuration: $(\sigma_{1s})^2(\sigma_{1s}^*)^2(\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2s}^*)^2(\sigma_{2p})^2(\pi_{2p})^4(\pi_{2p}^*)^2$

Bond order = $(8 - 4)/2 = 2$ (double bond). Two unpaired electrons in $\pi^*$ → **paramagnetic**. This is a triumph of MO theory — Lewis structures predict O₂ is diamagnetic (wrong!).

### 3.2 VSEPR Theory (Geometry Prediction)

**Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion:** Electron pairs (bonding and lone) arrange themselves to minimize repulsion.

| Electron Pairs | Geometry | Bond Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃, SO₃ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, NH₄⁺ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°/120° | PCl₅ |
| 6 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |

**Lone pairs compress bond angles** (they occupy more space than bonding pairs):
- NH₃: 4 pairs, 1 lone → trigonal pyramidal, 107°
- H₂O: 4 pairs, 2 lone → bent, 104.5°

### 3.3 Intermolecular Forces (Weakest to Strongest)

1. **London Dispersion Forces** — Instantaneous dipole-induced dipole. Present in ALL molecules. Strength ∝ polarizability ∝ molecular size.

2. **Dipole-Dipole** — Permanent dipole alignment. Present in polar molecules (HCl, acetone).

3. **Hydrogen Bonding** — Special case of dipole-dipole: H bonded to F, O, or N interacts with lone pair on another F, O, or N. Explains water's anomalous properties.

4. **Ion-Dipole** — Ion interacts with polar molecule. Drives dissolution of ionic compounds in water.

## ✍️ 4. Worked Examples

### Example 03.2.1 — MO Diagram for N₂: Why It's Triple-Bonded

**Problem:** Construct the MO diagram for N₂ and determine bond order, magnetic properties, and bond strength relative to O₂.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Nitrogen has 7 electrons; N₂ has 14 total.

**Step 2:** For N₂ (and lighter diatomics B₂, C₂, N₂), the $\sigma_{2p}$ orbital is HIGHER than $\pi_{2p}$ due to s-p mixing. The MO ordering is:

$$
\sigma_{1s} \lt  \sigma_{1s}^* \lt  \sigma_{2s} \lt  \sigma_{2s}^* \lt  \pi_{2p} \lt  \sigma_{2p} \lt  \pi_{2p}^* \lt  \sigma_{2p}^*
$$

**Step 3:** Fill 14 electrons:

$$
(\sigma_{1s})^2(\sigma_{1s}^*)^2(\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2s}^*)^2(\pi_{2p})^4(\sigma_{2p})^2
$$

**Step 4:** Bond order:

$$
\text{BO} = \frac{10 - 4}{2} = 3
$$

Triple bond! Consistent with the Lewis structure :N≡N:

**Step 5:** All electrons are paired → **diamagnetic**. ✓

**Step 6:** Comparison with O₂ (BO = 2): N₂ has a stronger, shorter bond.
- N₂: bond energy = 945 kJ/mol, bond length = 110 pm
- O₂: bond energy = 498 kJ/mol, bond length = 121 pm

The triple bond in N₂ makes it extremely unreactive — this is why nitrogen gas is so stable in the atmosphere.

</details>

### Example 03.2.2 — VSEPR: Predicting the Shape of SF₄

**Problem:** Predict the molecular geometry of SF₄ using VSEPR theory.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Count valence electrons: S has 6, each F has 7. Total = $6 + 4(7) = 34$.

**Step 2:** Draw Lewis structure: S is central, bonded to 4 F atoms. After placing 4 bonds (8 electrons) and lone pairs on F atoms (24 electrons), 2 electrons remain → 1 lone pair on S.

**Step 3:** Electron geometry: 5 electron pairs → trigonal bipyramidal.

**Step 4:** Molecular geometry (considering only atoms, not lone pairs): The lone pair occupies an equatorial position (minimizes 90° repulsions).

Result: **See-saw (or sawhorse) geometry**.

Bond angles: approximately 120° (equatorial) and slightly less than 90° (axial), compressed by the lone pair.

**Step 5:** Verify: SF₄ is polar (asymmetric geometry → net dipole moment). ✓

</details>

### Example 03.2.3 — Hybridization of Carbon in Ethene (C₂H₄)

**Problem:** Determine the hybridization of each carbon in ethene and explain the double bond.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Each carbon in ethene is bonded to 2 H atoms and 1 other C, with a double bond to C. That's 3 σ-bond regions → $sp^2$ hybridization.

**Step 2:** The three $sp^2$ hybrid orbitals are coplanar (120° apart). They form:
- 2 C–H σ bonds
- 1 C–C σ bond

**Step 3:** Each carbon has one unhybridized $p_z$ orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane. These overlap sideways to form the **π bond**.

**Step 4:** The double bond = 1 σ bond + 1 π bond.

**Step 5:** Consequences:
- Ethene is planar (rotation around C=C is restricted because it would break the π bond)
- Bond length: 134 pm (shorter than C–C single bond at 154 pm)
- Bond energy: 614 kJ/mol (stronger than single bond at 348 kJ/mol, but NOT double — the π bond is weaker than the σ bond)

**The QM perspective:** The σ bond is a $\sigma_g$ MO (cylindrically symmetric around the bond axis). The π bond is a $\pi_u$ MO (one nodal plane containing the bond axis). This connects directly to the symmetry labels from your angular momentum studies.

</details>

### Example 03.2.4 — Why Does Ice Float? (Hydrogen Bonding)

**Problem:** Explain why solid water (ice) is less dense than liquid water, using intermolecular force theory.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Water molecules form hydrogen bonds: each O has 2 lone pairs and 2 O–H bonds, allowing up to 4 hydrogen bonds per molecule.

**Step 2:** In liquid water, hydrogen bonds are dynamic — constantly forming and breaking. Molecules are relatively close but disordered.

**Step 3:** In ice, hydrogen bonds lock into a rigid tetrahedral network. Each water molecule is hydrogen-bonded to exactly 4 neighbors in a crystalline lattice.

**Step 4:** The tetrahedral arrangement creates an open structure with significant empty space (like a diamond lattice). This open structure is LESS dense than the disordered liquid.

**Step 5:** Quantitatively:
- Ice density: 0.917 g/cm³
- Liquid water at 4°C: 1.000 g/cm³
- Volume expansion on freezing: ~9%

**Biological significance:** If ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up, killing aquatic life. The ice layer insulates the liquid below.

**The deeper point:** This is an example of how intermolecular forces (quantum mechanical in origin — hydrogen bonding involves partial covalent character from orbital overlap) determine macroscopic properties.

</details>

## ⚠️ 6. Common Misconceptions & Where Most Students Fail

### ❌ Misconception 1: "Electrons are shared equally in all covalent bonds"

**The truth:** Only homonuclear diatomics (H₂, O₂, N₂) have perfectly equal sharing. In heteronuclear bonds (H–Cl, C–O), electrons are pulled toward the more electronegative atom. This creates a **polar covalent bond** — a continuum between pure covalent and pure ionic.

### ❌ Misconception 2: "Antibonding orbitals are never occupied"

**The truth:** Antibonding orbitals ARE occupied in many molecules. O₂ has 2 electrons in $\pi^*$ orbitals. The bond order accounts for this: BO = (bonding - antibonding)/2. A molecule exists as long as BO > 0.

### ❌ Misconception 3: "Hybridization is a real physical process"

**The truth:** Hybridization is a mathematical convenience — a way to construct localized bonding descriptions from delocalized MOs. The atom doesn't "actually" mix its orbitals before bonding. MO theory is more fundamental; hybridization is a useful approximation for predicting geometry.

### ❌ Misconception 4: "Hydrogen bonds are just strong dipole-dipole forces"

**The truth:** Hydrogen bonds have significant covalent character — there's actual orbital overlap between the H and the lone pair. This is why they're directional (unlike pure electrostatic interactions) and why they're so much stronger than typical dipole-dipole forces (~20 kJ/mol vs ~5 kJ/mol).

### ❌ Misconception 5: "Lewis structures tell you everything about bonding"

**The truth:** Lewis structures fail for:
- O₂ (predicts diamagnetic — wrong)
- Benzene (can't represent delocalization)
- Electron-deficient compounds (B₂H₆)
- Hypervalent compounds (SF₆ — Lewis says "expanded octet," MO theory says 3-center-4-electron bonds)

MO theory is always correct; Lewis structures are a useful shorthand that sometimes fails.

### 💪 The Pep Talk

Bonding is where most students first feel lost in chemistry — too many "types" of bonds, too many rules, too many exceptions. But here's the secret: **there's only ONE type of bond** — the quantum mechanical lowering of energy when atomic wavefunctions overlap constructively. Ionic, covalent, metallic, hydrogen bonds — they're all points on a continuum, all explained by the same Schrödinger equation.

You already know how to solve eigenvalue problems. MO theory is just that: diagonalize the Hamiltonian matrix in the basis of atomic orbitals. The "chemistry" is just choosing which matrix to diagonalize.

## 🔬 8. Advanced Derivations — Molecular Orbital Theory & Geometry from First Principles

### 8.1 — VSEPR Full Taxonomy: Predicting Every Molecular Geometry

VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) predicts geometry by minimizing repulsion between electron domains around a central atom. Here is the complete taxonomy with worked predictions.

**The VSEPR Algorithm:**

1. Draw the Lewis structure (count bonds and lone pairs on the central atom).
2. Count **electron domains** (each bond — single, double, or triple — counts as ONE domain; each lone pair counts as ONE domain).
3. Determine the **electron-domain geometry** from the total count.
4. Determine the **molecular geometry** by considering only atom positions (lone pairs are "invisible" to geometry naming).

**Complete VSEPR Table:**

| Domains | Electron Geometry | Lone Pairs | Molecular Geometry | Bond Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 0 | Linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃, SO₃ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 1 | Bent | ~117° | SO₂, O₃ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, SiH₄ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | ~107° | NH₃, PH₃ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 2 | Bent | ~104.5° | H₂O, H₂S |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° | PCl₅ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 1 | Seesaw | ~90°, ~120° | SF₄ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 2 | T-shaped | ~90° | ClF₃ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 3 | Linear | 180° | XeF₂ |
| 6 | Octahedral | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 6 | Octahedral | 1 | Square pyramidal | ~85° | BrF₅ |
| 6 | Octahedral | 2 | Square planar | 90° | XeF₄ |

**Worked Prediction — ICl₄⁻:**

**Step 1:** Central atom = I. Valence electrons: I has 7, plus 4 from Cl bonds, plus 1 for the negative charge = 12 electrons = 6 pairs around I.

**Step 2:** 4 bonding pairs (to 4 Cl atoms) + 2 lone pairs = 6 electron domains.

**Step 3:** Electron geometry = octahedral. With 2 lone pairs in an octahedral arrangement, lone pairs go TRANS to each other (minimizes lp-lp repulsion at 180° rather than 90°).

**Step 4:** Molecular geometry = **square planar**. Bond angles = 90°.

**Why lone pairs compress bond angles:**

Lone pairs occupy more angular space than bonding pairs (they're held closer to the nucleus, spread out more). This is quantifiable: the repulsion hierarchy is:

$$
\text{lp-lp} > \text{lp-bp} > \text{bp-bp}
$$

For H₂O: two lone pairs compress the H-O-H angle from the ideal 109.5° to 104.5°. For NH₃: one lone pair compresses H-N-H from 109.5° to 107.3°.

### 8.2 — Molecular Orbital Theory: LCAO for H₂⁺, H₂, O₂, and CO

#### The H₂⁺ Ion: The Simplest Molecule

One electron, two protons separated by distance $R$. The Hamiltonian:

$$
\hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2 - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_A} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_B} + \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R}
$$

where $r_A$ and $r_B$ are distances from the electron to nuclei A and B.

**LCAO Ansatz:** $\psi = c_A\phi_A + c_B\phi_B$ where $\phi_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}e^{-r_A/a_0}$ (1s orbital on atom A).

**The secular equation** (from the variational principle):

$$
\begin{vmatrix} H_{AA} - ES_{AA} & H_{AB} - ES_{AB} \\ H_{BA} - ES_{BA} & H_{BB} - ES_{BB} \end{vmatrix} = 0
$$

By symmetry: $H_{AA} = H_{BB} \equiv \alpha$ (Coulomb integral), $H_{AB} = H_{BA} \equiv \beta$ (resonance integral), $S_{AA} = S_{BB} = 1$, $S_{AB} = S_{BA} \equiv S$ (overlap integral).

**The Coulomb integral** $\alpha$:

$$
\alpha = \langle\phi_A|\hat{H}|\phi_A\rangle = E_{1s} + \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left\langle\phi_A\left|\frac{1}{r_B}\right|\phi_A\right\rangle
$$

The last term is the attraction of the electron (in orbital A) to nucleus B — it's negative and stabilizing.

**The resonance integral** $\beta$:

$$
\beta = \langle\phi_A|\hat{H}|\phi_B\rangle = E_{1s}S + \frac{e^2 S}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left\langle\phi_A\left|\frac{1}{r_B}\right|\phi_B\right\rangle
$$

**Solving the secular equation:**

$$
(\alpha - E)^2 - (\beta - ES)^2 = 0
$$

$$
E_{\pm} = \frac{\alpha \pm \beta}{1 \pm S}
$$

**The bonding orbital** ($E_+$, lower energy):

$$
\psi_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+S)}}(\phi_A + \phi_B), \quad E_+ = \frac{\alpha + \beta}{1 + S}
$$

**The antibonding orbital** ($E_-$, higher energy):

$$
\psi_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-S)}}(\phi_A - \phi_B), \quad E_- = \frac{\alpha - \beta}{1 - S}
$$

**Key insight:** $\beta < 0$ (the resonance integral is negative for bonding interactions), so $E_+ < \alpha < E_-$. The antibonding orbital is destabilized MORE than the bonding orbital is stabilized (because of the $1/(1 \pm S)$ factors). This asymmetry is why He₂ doesn't exist — filling both MOs gives net destabilization.

#### The O₂ Molecule: Paramagnetism Explained

Oxygen has 16 electrons total, 12 in the valence MOs. The MO diagram for O₂ (and all homonuclear diatomics from O₂ to Ne₂):

**Energy ordering (for O₂, F₂, Ne₂):**

$$
\sigma_{2s} < \sigma_{2s}^* < \sigma_{2p} < \pi_{2p} = \pi_{2p} < \pi_{2p}^* = \pi_{2p}^* < \sigma_{2p}^*
$$

**Note:** For B₂, C₂, N₂, the ordering is different — $\pi_{2p}$ drops below $\sigma_{2p}$ due to $s$-$p$ mixing.

**Filling for O₂ (12 valence electrons):**

$$
(\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2s}^*)^2(\sigma_{2p})^2(\pi_{2p})^2(\pi_{2p})^2(\pi_{2p}^*)^1(\pi_{2p}^*)^1
$$

**Bond order:**

$$
BO = \frac{8 - 4}{2} = 2 \quad \text{(double bond)}
$$

**The triumph of MO theory:** The two electrons in $\pi^*$ orbitals have PARALLEL spins (Hund's rule) → O₂ is **paramagnetic** (attracted to a magnetic field). Lewis structures predict O₂ is diamagnetic (all electrons paired). This was one of the great early successes of MO theory over Lewis/VBT.

#### The CO Molecule: A Heteronuclear Case

Carbon and oxygen have different electronegativities, so the MOs are no longer symmetric combinations. The more electronegative atom (O) contributes more to bonding MOs; the less electronegative atom (C) contributes more to antibonding MOs.

**LCAO for CO:**

$$
\psi_{\text{bonding}} = c_C\phi_C + c_O\phi_O, \quad |c_O| > |c_C|
$$

$$
\psi_{\text{antibonding}} = c_C'\phi_C - c_O'\phi_O, \quad |c_C'| > |c_O'|
$$

**The HOMO of CO** is a $\sigma$ orbital concentrated on CARBON (the less electronegative atom). This is why CO is such a good ligand — it donates electron density from carbon, not oxygen. This explains:
- CO poisoning (binds to Fe in hemoglobin via the carbon end)
- Transition metal carbonyl complexes (Ni(CO)₄, Fe(CO)₅)
- The "anti-intuitive" dipole moment of CO (small, with negative end on C)

**Bond order of CO = 3** (isoelectronic with N₂). Triple bond: one $\sigma$ + two $\pi$.

### 8.3 — Intermolecular Forces: Quantitative Treatment

#### London Dispersion Energy Formula

Even nonpolar molecules attract each other. The London dispersion force arises from correlated fluctuations in electron density — a purely quantum mechanical effect (requires electron correlation beyond Hartree-Fock).

**The London formula for dispersion energy between two atoms/molecules:**

$$
E_{\text{disp}} = -\frac{3}{4}\frac{\alpha_1\alpha_2}{(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2}\frac{I_1 I_2}{I_1 + I_2}\frac{1}{r^6}
$$

where:
- $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ = polarizabilities of the two species
- $I_1, I_2$ = ionization energies
- $r$ = intermolecular distance

**Derivation sketch (second-order perturbation theory):**

The instantaneous dipole on molecule 1 creates an electric field $\sim \mu_1/r^3$ at molecule 2. This induces a dipole $\mu_2^{\text{ind}} = \alpha_2 E \sim \alpha_2\mu_1/r^3$. The interaction energy is:

$$
E \sim -\frac{\mu_1\mu_2^{\text{ind}}}{r^3} \sim -\frac{\alpha_2\mu_1^2}{r^6}
$$

Since $\langle\mu_1^2\rangle \propto \alpha_1 I_1$ (from quantum fluctuations), we get the $r^{-6}$ dependence.

**Worked example — Ar-Ar interaction:**

- $\alpha(\text{Ar}) = 1.64 \times 10^{-30}\,\text{m}^3$
- $I(\text{Ar}) = 15.76\,\text{eV} = 2.52 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{J}$
- At $r = 3.76\,\text{Å}$ (van der Waals minimum):

$$
E_{\text{disp}} = -\frac{3}{4}\frac{(1.64 \times 10^{-30})^2}{(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2}\frac{(2.52 \times 10^{-18})^2}{2 \times 2.52 \times 10^{-18}}\frac{1}{(3.76 \times 10^{-10})^6}
$$

$$
\approx -1.2 \times 10^{-21}\,\text{J} \approx -0.0075\,\text{eV} \approx -0.7\,\text{kJ/mol}
$$

This matches the experimental well depth of Ar₂ (~1.2 kJ/mol) to order of magnitude. The London formula is approximate but captures the essential physics.

#### Dipole-Dipole Interaction (Keesom Force)

For two permanent dipoles $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ at temperature $T$:

$$
E_{\text{Keesom}} = -\frac{2\mu_1^2\mu_2^2}{3(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2 k_B T}\frac{1}{r^6}
$$

This is the Boltzmann-averaged interaction over all orientations. Note: also $r^{-6}$, but temperature-dependent (weakens at high $T$ because thermal motion randomizes orientations).

#### Hydrogen Bonding: Beyond Classical Electrostatics

Hydrogen bonds (O-H···O, N-H···N, etc.) are NOT purely electrostatic. They have:
- ~60% electrostatic character (dipole-dipole)
- ~20% charge transfer (covalent character — electron density flows from lone pair into σ* of O-H)
- ~10% dispersion
- ~10% exchange repulsion reduction

**Evidence for covalent character:**
- H-bond lengths are shorter than van der Waals contact
- NMR chemical shifts change upon H-bonding
- Infrared O-H stretching frequency decreases (bond weakens as electron density shifts)
- Compton scattering experiments directly show electron density in the H-bond region

**Energy scale:** 10–40 kJ/mol (vs. ~1 kJ/mol for dispersion, ~400 kJ/mol for covalent bonds).

### 8.4 — The Hybridization Debate: Is sp³ Real?

> [!warning] Important Conceptual Point
> Hybridization is a **mathematical convenience**, not a physical observable. You cannot measure an sp³ orbital. What you CAN measure are molecular geometries and electron densities — and these are perfectly explained by MO theory without invoking hybridization.

**What hybridization actually is:**

Take the carbon atom. Its ground state is $2s^2 2p^2$. To form 4 equivalent bonds (as in CH₄), we construct 4 equivalent orbitals by mixing:

$$
h_1 = \frac{1}{2}(s + p_x + p_y + p_z)
$$

$$
h_2 = \frac{1}{2}(s + p_x - p_y - p_z)
$$

$$
h_3 = \frac{1}{2}(s - p_x + p_y - p_z)
$$

$$
h_4 = \frac{1}{2}(s - p_x - p_y + p_z)
$$

These point toward the vertices of a tetrahedron. But this is just a **unitary transformation** of the basis — it doesn't change the physics. The total electron density from 4 sp³ orbitals is identical to the density from the original $s + 3p$ orbitals.

**Why hybridization is useful:**
- Provides intuitive geometry predictions
- Connects to VSEPR
- Simplifies bonding descriptions for organic chemistry

**Why hybridization is misleading:**
- Suggests "promotion" of an electron from $2s$ to $2p$ costs energy (it doesn't — the energy is recovered in bond formation)
- Implies discrete hybridization states (sp, sp², sp³) when reality is a continuum
- Fails for molecules like SF₆ (would need "sp³d²" — but $d$ orbitals are too high in energy to participate significantly)
- Cannot explain photoelectron spectra of CH₄ (which shows TWO ionization energies, not one — inconsistent with 4 equivalent sp³ orbitals)

**The MO theory explanation of CH₄:**

MO theory for CH₄ gives:
- One $a_1$ bonding MO (from C $2s$ + H $1s$ combination) — lower energy
- Three degenerate $t_2$ bonding MOs (from C $2p$ + H $1s$ combinations) — higher energy

Photoelectron spectroscopy confirms: CH₄ has ionization peaks at 14.4 eV ($a_1$) and 23.0 eV ($t_2$). If all four bonds were equivalent sp³, there would be only ONE peak.

**Bottom line:** Use hybridization as a quick prediction tool. Use MO theory when you need the real answer.

### 8.5 — MO Theory vs. Valence Bond Theory: Complete Comparison

| Feature | MO Theory | VB Theory |
|---|---|---|
| **Wavefunction** | Electrons delocalized over entire molecule | Electrons localized in bonds between atom pairs |
| **Bond description** | Bonding/antibonding MOs | Resonance structures |
| **Paramagnetism of O₂** | ✅ Correctly predicted | ❌ Requires special treatment |
| **Aromaticity** | Natural (delocalized $\pi$ MOs) | Requires resonance (multiple structures) |
| **Bond dissociation** | ❌ Gives wrong limit (ionic + covalent mix) | ✅ Correct dissociation to neutral atoms |
| **Computational cost** | Lower (single determinant) | Higher (multiple determinants) |
| **Photoelectron spectra** | ✅ Directly interpretable | ❌ Requires Koopmans' theorem adaptation |
| **Intuition for organic** | Less intuitive for large molecules | More intuitive (think in terms of bonds) |
| **Modern use** | Basis for all computational chemistry (DFT, HF, CI) | Basis for chemical intuition and retrosynthesis |

**The reconciliation:** At the highest level of theory (full configuration interaction), MO and VB approaches give identical results. They're different ways of expanding the same exact wavefunction. The debate is about which truncation is more useful for which problem.

> [!tip] Cross-link to Track 09
> The mathematical equivalence of MO and VB at full CI is proven in [[9.4 - Angular Momentum, Spin & Fine Structure]] using the completeness of Slater determinant bases. For practical chemistry, MO theory dominates computational work while VB intuition dominates synthetic planning.

## 📎 9. Appendix — Deep Dives & Mastery Challenges

### Appendix A — Complete LCAO-MO Calculation for H₂ (Two Electrons)

For H₂ (not H₂⁺), we have two electrons. The spatial MOs are the same as H₂⁺:

$$
\sigma_g = \frac{\phi_A + \phi_B}{\sqrt{2(1+S)}}, \quad \sigma_u^* = \frac{\phi_A - \phi_B}{\sqrt{2(1-S)}}
$$

**The ground state:** Both electrons in $\sigma_g$ with opposite spins:

$$
\Psi_{\text{MO}} = \sigma_g(1)\sigma_g(2)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\alpha(1)\beta(2) - \beta(1)\alpha(2)]
$$

Expanding the spatial part:

$$
\sigma_g(1)\sigma_g(2) = \frac{1}{2(1+S)}[\phi_A(1) + \phi_B(1)][\phi_A(2) + \phi_B(2)]
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{2(1+S)}[\phi_A(1)\phi_A(2) + \phi_B(1)\phi_B(2) + \phi_A(1)\phi_B(2) + \phi_B(1)\phi_A(2)]
$$

**The problem:** The first two terms represent both electrons on the same atom — ionic configurations (H⁻H⁺ and H⁺H⁻). MO theory gives them equal weight to the covalent terms. At large $R$, this is wrong — H₂ dissociates to two neutral H atoms, not to H⁺ + H⁻.

**The VB fix:** Valence Bond theory uses only the covalent terms:

$$
\Psi_{\text{VB}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+S^2)}}[\phi_A(1)\phi_B(2) + \phi_B(1)\phi_A(2)]
$$

This correctly dissociates but underestimates bonding at equilibrium (misses ionic contribution).

**The correct answer:** Configuration Interaction (CI) mixes both:

$$
\Psi_{\text{CI}} = c_1\,\sigma_g(1)\sigma_g(2) + c_2\,\sigma_u^*(1)\sigma_u^*(2)
$$

At equilibrium: $c_1 \gg c_2$ (mostly MO-like). At dissociation: $c_1 = c_2$ (purely covalent). This is the simplest example of electron correlation.

### Appendix B — Hückel MO Theory for Conjugated Systems

For planar conjugated $\pi$ systems, Hückel theory provides MO energies using only two parameters: $\alpha$ (Coulomb integral for carbon $2p_z$) and $\beta$ (resonance integral for adjacent carbons).

**The Hückel secular determinant for butadiene (4 carbon chain):**

$$
\begin{vmatrix} \alpha - E & \beta & 0 & 0 \\ \beta & \alpha - E & \beta & 0 \\ 0 & \beta & \alpha - E & \beta \\ 0 & 0 & \beta & \alpha - E \end{vmatrix} = 0
$$

Substituting $x = (\alpha - E)/\beta$:

$$
\begin{vmatrix} x & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & x & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & x & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & x \end{vmatrix} = 0
$$

$$
x^4 - 3x^2 + 1 = 0
$$

**Solutions:** $x = \pm 1.618, \pm 0.618$ (the golden ratio appears!)

**Energies:** $E_k = \alpha + 2\beta\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{5}\right)$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, 4$

- $E_1 = \alpha + 1.618\beta$ (most bonding)
- $E_2 = \alpha + 0.618\beta$ (bonding)
- $E_3 = \alpha - 0.618\beta$ (antibonding)
- $E_4 = \alpha - 1.618\beta$ (most antibonding)

**Delocalization energy:** 4 electrons fill $E_1$ and $E_2$. Total $\pi$ energy = $2(\alpha + 1.618\beta) + 2(\alpha + 0.618\beta) = 4\alpha + 4.472\beta$. For two isolated double bonds: $4\alpha + 4\beta$. Delocalization stabilization = $0.472|\beta| \approx 36\,\text{kJ/mol}$.

```python
import numpy as np

def huckel_chain(n):
    """Hückel MO energies for a linear conjugated chain of n carbons."""
    # Build the Hückel matrix (tridiagonal)
    H = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n-1):
        H[i, i+1] = 1.0  # beta (in units of beta)
        H[i+1, i] = 1.0
    # Eigenvalues give x = (alpha - E)/beta, so E = alpha + eigenvalue*beta
    eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
    return np.sort(eigenvalues)[::-1]  # Sorted from most bonding to most antibonding

def huckel_ring(n):
    """Hückel MO energies for a cyclic conjugated ring of n carbons."""
    H = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        H[i, (i+1) % n] = 1.0
        H[(i+1) % n, i] = 1.0
    eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
    return np.sort(eigenvalues)[::-1]

# Benzene (6-membered ring)
benzene_E = huckel_ring(6)
print("Benzene MO energies (units of beta from alpha):")
for i, e in enumerate(benzene_E):
    print(f"  MO {i+1}: alpha + {e:.3f}*beta")

# Total pi energy (6 electrons)
total_pi = 2*benzene_E[0] + 2*benzene_E[1] + 2*benzene_E[2]
print(f"\nTotal pi energy: 6*alpha + {total_pi:.3f}*beta")
print(f"Three isolated double bonds: 6*alpha + 6*beta")
print(f"Delocalization energy: {total_pi - 6:.3f}*|beta| = ~{abs(total_pi-6)*75:.0f} kJ/mol")
# (using |beta| ~ 75 kJ/mol for carbon-carbon)
```

### Appendix C — The Lennard-Jones Potential: Combining Attraction and Repulsion

The complete intermolecular potential combines London dispersion (attraction, $r^{-6}$) with Pauli repulsion (short-range, modeled as $r^{-12}$):

$$
V(r) = 4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^6\right]
$$

where:
- $\epsilon$ = well depth (minimum energy)
- $\sigma$ = distance at which $V = 0$ (collision diameter)
- Minimum at $r_{\min} = 2^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.122\sigma$

**Parameters for common interactions:**

| Pair | $\epsilon/k_B$ (K) | $\sigma$ (Å) |
|---|---|---|
| He-He | 10.2 | 2.56 |
| Ne-Ne | 35.7 | 2.75 |
| Ar-Ar | 120 | 3.40 |
| Kr-Kr | 164 | 3.63 |
| N₂-N₂ | 95.1 | 3.70 |
| CH₄-CH₄ | 148 | 3.82 |

**Connection to thermodynamics:** The second virial coefficient $B(T)$ (deviation from ideal gas behavior) is computed directly from the pair potential:

$$
B(T) = -2\pi N_A\int_0^\infty\left[e^{-V(r)/(k_BT)} - 1\right]r^2\,dr
$$

This connects molecular-level bonding (this chapter) to macroscopic thermodynamics ([[5.6 - The Partition Function & Free Energy]]).

> [!danger] 🧠 Common Misconceptions — Section 8 & 9 Summary
> 
> **❌ "Double bonds are just two single bonds"**
> **Truth:** A double bond consists of one σ bond (head-on overlap) and one π bond (lateral overlap). The π bond is weaker (~260 kJ/mol vs ~350 kJ/mol for σ) and restricts rotation. They are fundamentally different types of orbital overlap.
> 
> **❌ "Hybridization is a physical process that happens to atoms"**
> **Truth:** Hybridization is a mathematical transformation of basis orbitals. No atom "decides" to hybridize. It's a human convenience for predicting geometry. MO theory gives the same geometries without invoking hybridization.
> 
> **❌ "Electronegativity determines everything about a bond"**
> **Truth:** Electronegativity determines polarity and ionic/covalent character, but bond STRENGTH depends on orbital overlap (size matching, symmetry matching). HF has a stronger bond than HI not just because F is more electronegative, but because the 1s-2p overlap is better than 1s-5p overlap.
> 
> **❌ "London dispersion forces are weak and unimportant"**
> **Truth:** For large molecules (proteins, polymers, geckos climbing walls), the cumulative London dispersion is enormous. Gecko adhesion (~10 N/cm²) is almost entirely dispersion forces from billions of nanoscale contacts. Dispersion also determines boiling points of all nonpolar substances.
> 
> **❌ "Antibonding orbitals are never occupied"**
> **Truth:** Antibonding orbitals ARE occupied in many stable molecules (O₂ has two electrons in π* orbitals). What matters is the NET bond order. Even F₂ has occupied antibonding orbitals — it's stable because bonding electrons outnumber antibonding ones.
> 
> **❌ "VSEPR works because electron pairs repel each other electrostatically"**
> **Truth:** The dominant repulsion is actually Pauli repulsion (the exclusion principle forces same-spin electrons apart), not classical Coulomb repulsion. VSEPR works for the right reason stated wrong. The geometry minimizes the kinetic energy cost of antisymmetrization.
