# 03.7 — Organic Chemistry Foundations

> *"Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Biochemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds that crawl."* — Mike Adams

> *"The art of organic synthesis is the art of controlling electrons."* — R.B. Woodward (Nobel Prize, 1965)

Organic chemistry is not memorization — it's pattern recognition. There are only a handful of fundamental mechanism types, and every reaction in organic chemistry is a variation on one of them. This chapter teaches you to think mechanistically: follow the electrons, predict the products.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Identify and name the major functional groups (alkane, alkene, alkyne, alcohol, aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid, amine, ester, amide).
2. Draw curved arrows showing electron flow in reaction mechanisms.
3. Distinguish and predict products for SN1 vs SN2 substitution reactions.
4. Distinguish and predict products for E1 vs E2 elimination reactions.
5. Predict products of electrophilic addition to alkenes (Markovnikov/anti-Markovnikov).
6. Predict products of electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) on benzene.
7. Apply frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory to explain reactivity.
8. Assign R/S stereochemistry and predict stereochemical outcomes.

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 03.7.1 — Functional Group

A specific arrangement of atoms that determines the chemical reactivity of a molecule. The carbon skeleton is inert; functional groups are where reactions happen.

| Functional Group | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Alkene | C=C | Ethene (CH₂=CH₂) |
| Alcohol | –OH | Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | Acetaldehyde (CH₃CHO) |
| Ketone | –CO– | Acetone (CH₃COCH₃) |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) |
| Amine | –NH₂ | Methylamine (CH₃NH₂) |
| Ester | –COO– | Ethyl acetate (CH₃COOCH₂CH₃) |
| Amide | –CONH₂ | Acetamide (CH₃CONH₂) |

### Definition 03.7.2 — Nucleophile

An electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to form a new bond. "Nucleus-loving." Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O.

### Definition 03.7.3 — Electrophile

An electron-poor species that accepts an electron pair. "Electron-loving." Examples: H⁺, carbocations (R⁺), BF₃.

### Definition 03.7.4 — Leaving Group

A group that departs with the bonding electrons. Good leaving groups are stable anions (weak bases): I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ > OH⁻ (in order of leaving ability).

### Definition 03.7.5 — Carbocation

A carbon with only 6 electrons (positive charge). Stability: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl (hyperconjugation and inductive effects stabilize).

### Definition 03.7.6 — Stereochemistry (R/S)

The 3D arrangement of atoms around a chiral center (carbon with 4 different substituents). Assigned by Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules.

### Definition 03.7.7 — Curved Arrow Notation

Curved arrows show electron movement:
- **Full arrow (⟶):** Movement of an electron PAIR (bond formation/breaking)
- **Half arrow (⇀):** Movement of a SINGLE electron (radical mechanisms)

Arrows always go FROM electron-rich TO electron-poor.

## 📐 2. Mathematical Foundations

### 2.1 Frontier Molecular Orbital (FMO) Theory

Reactions occur when the **HOMO** (Highest Occupied MO) of one reactant interacts with the **LUMO** (Lowest Unoccupied MO) of another:

$$
\text{Rate} \propto \frac{|\langle\text{HOMO}|\hat{H}'|\text{LUMO}\rangle|^2}{E_{\text{LUMO}} - E_{\text{HOMO}}}
$$

- Nucleophile's HOMO → Electrophile's LUMO (nucleophilic attack)
- Small HOMO-LUMO gap → faster reaction

This is perturbation theory from [[9.5 - Time-Independent Perturbation Theory]] applied to molecular orbitals!

### 2.2 Hammond's Postulate

The transition state resembles the species (reactant or product) closest to it in energy:
- Exothermic reactions: early transition state (resembles reactants)
- Endothermic reactions: late transition state (resembles products)

This explains why carbocation stability determines product distribution in SN1/E1 reactions.

### 2.3 Kinetics of Substitution and Elimination

| Mechanism | Rate Law | Kinetic Order |
|---|---|---|
| SN2 | rate = $k[\text{substrate}][\text{nucleophile}]$ | Second order |
| SN1 | rate = $k[\text{substrate}]$ | First order |
| E2 | rate = $k[\text{substrate}][\text{base}]$ | Second order |
| E1 | rate = $k[\text{substrate}]$ | First order |

### 2.4 Thermodynamic vs Kinetic Control

- **Kinetic control** (low T, short time): Product formed fastest (lowest $E_a$)
- **Thermodynamic control** (high T, long time): Most stable product (lowest $\Delta G$)

## 🔬 3. Chemical Mechanisms

### 3.1 SN2 — Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution

**Mechanism:** One concerted step. Nucleophile attacks the back side of the carbon bearing the leaving group (backside attack).

**Key features:**
- Rate = $k[\text{substrate}][\text{Nu}]$ (second order)
- **Inversion** of stereochemistry (Walden inversion)
- Favored by: strong nucleophile, methyl/primary substrate, polar aprotic solvent
- Disfavored by: bulky substrate (steric hindrance), weak nucleophile

**Arrow pushing:**

$$
\text{Nu}^- \xrightarrow{\text{attacks}} \text{C}—\text{LG} \longrightarrow \text{Nu}—\text{C} + \text{LG}^-
$$

The nucleophile's lone pair forms a new bond to carbon; simultaneously, the C–LG bond breaks (LG departs with both electrons).

### 3.2 SN1 — Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution

**Mechanism:** Two steps.
1. Leaving group departs → carbocation intermediate (rate-determining)
2. Nucleophile attacks carbocation

**Key features:**
- Rate = $k[\text{substrate}]$ (first order — only step 1 in rate law)
- **Racemization** (nucleophile attacks from both faces of planar carbocation)
- Favored by: tertiary substrate (stable carbocation), weak nucleophile, polar protic solvent
- Disfavored by: primary substrate (unstable carbocation)

### 3.3 E2 — Bimolecular Elimination

**Mechanism:** One concerted step. Base removes a β-hydrogen while the leaving group departs simultaneously.

**Key features:**
- Rate = $k[\text{substrate}][\text{base}]$
- Requires **anti-periplanar** geometry (H and LG on opposite sides, 180° dihedral)
- Produces alkene (Zaitsev's rule: more substituted alkene preferred)
- Favored by: strong, bulky base; high temperature

### 3.4 E1 — Unimolecular Elimination

**Mechanism:** Two steps.
1. Leaving group departs → carbocation (same as SN1 step 1)
2. Base removes β-hydrogen from carbocation → alkene

**Key features:**
- Rate = $k[\text{substrate}]$
- Zaitsev product (most stable alkene)
- Competes with SN1 (same first step)

### 3.5 The SN1/SN2/E1/E2 Decision Tree

| Factor | SN2 | SN1 | E2 | E1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Substrate | Methyl, 1° | 3° | 2°, 3° | 3° |
| Nucleophile/Base | Strong Nu | Weak Nu | Strong base | Weak base |
| Solvent | Polar aprotic | Polar protic | — | Polar protic |
| Temperature | Low | — | High | High |
| Stereochemistry | Inversion | Racemization | Anti-periplanar | — |

### 3.6 Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes

**General mechanism:**
1. Electrophile (E⁺) attacks the π bond → carbocation intermediate
2. Nucleophile attacks carbocation

**Markovnikov's Rule:** H adds to the carbon with MORE hydrogens (electrophile goes to less substituted carbon → more stable carbocation intermediate).

**Examples:**
- HBr addition: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}=\text{CH}_2 + \text{HBr} \to \text{CH}_3\text{CHBr}\text{CH}_3$ (Markovnikov)
- Hydration (H₂O/H⁺): Same regiochemistry
- Anti-Markovnikov: HBr + peroxides (radical mechanism)

### 3.7 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS)

**General mechanism:**
1. Electrophile attacks benzene ring → arenium ion (σ-complex, carbocation)
2. Proton loss restores aromaticity

**Why substitution, not addition?** Addition would destroy the aromatic stabilization energy (~150 kJ/mol). Substitution preserves it.

**Major EAS reactions:**

| Reaction | Electrophile | Reagents |
|---|---|---|
| Halogenation | X⁺ (from X₂/FeX₃) | Br₂/FeBr₃ |
| Nitration | NO₂⁺ | HNO₃/H₂SO₄ |
| Friedel-Crafts alkylation | R⁺ | RCl/AlCl₃ |
| Friedel-Crafts acylation | RCO⁺ | RCOCl/AlCl₃ |
| Sulfonation | SO₃ | H₂SO₄(fuming) |

**Directing effects:**
- Electron-donating groups (–OH, –NH₂, –CH₃): ortho/para directors, activating
- Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –COOH, –CN): meta directors, deactivating
- Halogens: ortho/para directors but deactivating (special case)

## ✍️ 4. Worked Examples

### Example 03.7.1 — SN2 vs SN1: Predicting the Mechanism

**Problem:** Predict the mechanism and product for: (a) CH₃Br + NaOH → ? (b) (CH₃)₃CBr + H₂O → ?

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Part (a): CH₃Br + NaOH**

- Substrate: methyl (CH₃Br) — no steric hindrance
- Nucleophile: OH⁻ — strong nucleophile
- Prediction: **SN2**

Product: CH₃OH + Br⁻ (methanol)

Stereochemistry: If the carbon were chiral, we'd see inversion. For methyl, no stereocenter exists.

**Part (b): (CH₃)₃CBr + H₂O**

- Substrate: tertiary — too bulky for backside attack
- Nucleophile: H₂O — weak nucleophile
- Prediction: **SN1**

Mechanism:
1. (CH₃)₃CBr → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻ (slow, RDS)
2. (CH₃)₃C⁺ + H₂O → (CH₃)₃COH₂⁺ (fast)
3. (CH₃)₃COH₂⁺ → (CH₃)₃COH + H⁺ (deprotonation)

Product: (CH₃)₃COH (tert-butanol)

Note: E1 elimination also competes, giving (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ (isobutylene) as a side product.

</details>

### Example 03.7.2 — Electrophilic Addition: HBr to Propene

**Problem:** Draw the complete mechanism for the addition of HBr to propene (CH₃CH=CH₂). Predict the major product and explain regiochemistry.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Protonation of the alkene (electrophilic attack).

The π electrons of the double bond attack H⁺ (from HBr). Two possible carbocations:

- H adds to C1 → secondary carbocation at C2: CH₃C⁺HCH₃ ✓ (more stable)
- H adds to C2 → primary carbocation at C1: CH₃CH₂C⁺H₂ ✗ (less stable)

**Markovnikov's rule:** H adds to the less substituted carbon (forming the more stable carbocation).

**Step 2:** Nucleophilic attack by Br⁻.

Br⁻ attacks the secondary carbocation:

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{C}^+\text{HCH}_3 + \text{Br}^- \to \text{CH}_3\text{CHBrCH}_3
$$

**Major product:** 2-bromopropane (CH₃CHBrCH₃)

**FMO explanation:** The HOMO of the alkene (π bond) interacts with the LUMO of HBr (σ* of H–Br). The transition state that leads to the more stable carbocation has lower activation energy (Hammond's postulate).

</details>

---

### Example 03.7.3 — EAS: Nitration of Toluene

**Problem:** Predict the major product(s) of nitration of toluene (methylbenzene). Explain the directing effect.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Generate the electrophile.

$$
\text{HNO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \to \text{NO}_2^+ + \text{HSO}_4^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

The nitronium ion (NO₂⁺) is the electrophile.

**Step 2:** Electrophilic attack on toluene.

The methyl group is an electron-donating group (EDG) via hyperconjugation and inductive effects. It activates the ring and directs to **ortho and para** positions.

**Step 3:** Why ortho/para?

Draw the resonance structures of the arenium ion for ortho, meta, and para attack:

- **Ortho attack:** One resonance structure places the positive charge directly on the carbon bearing the methyl group → stabilized by hyperconjugation
- **Para attack:** Same stabilization
- **Meta attack:** Positive charge never lands on the methyl-bearing carbon → less stabilized

**Step 4:** Products.

Major products: ortho-nitrotoluene (~60%) and para-nitrotoluene (~37%)
Minor product: meta-nitrotoluene (~3%)

The ortho:para ratio is not exactly 2:1 (statistical prediction) because steric effects slightly disfavor ortho.

</details>

---

### Example 03.7.4 — E2 Elimination: Zaitsev's Rule

**Problem:** Predict the major product of E2 elimination of 2-bromobutane with NaOEt (sodium ethoxide).

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Identify β-hydrogens (hydrogens on carbons adjacent to the carbon bearing the leaving group).

2-Bromobutane: CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃

β-carbons: C1 (3 H's) and C3 (2 H's).

**Step 2:** Possible alkene products.

- Remove H from C1: CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ (1-butene, less substituted)
- Remove H from C3: CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (2-butene, more substituted)

**Step 3:** Apply Zaitsev's rule.

The more substituted alkene (2-butene) is thermodynamically more stable (more hyperconjugation) and is the **major product**.

**Step 4:** Stereochemistry of 2-butene.

E2 requires anti-periplanar geometry. Both *cis*-2-butene and *trans*-2-butene can form, but *trans* is more stable (less steric strain) and is the major isomer.

**Major product:** *trans*-2-butene

**Note:** With a bulky base like KOtBu (potassium tert-butoxide), the LESS substituted alkene (Hofmann product) becomes major due to steric effects. The base can't reach the more hindered β-hydrogen.

</details>

## ⚠️ 6. Common Misconceptions & Where Most Students Fail

### ❌ Misconception 1: "Organic chemistry is just memorization"

**The truth:** There are only ~6 fundamental mechanism types (SN1, SN2, E1, E2, addition, EAS). Every reaction in a 2-semester organic course is a variation on one of these. Learn the PATTERNS, not individual reactions. Once you understand electron flow, you can predict products for reactions you've never seen.

### ❌ Misconception 2: "Arrows show where atoms move"

**The truth:** Curved arrows show where ELECTRONS move. Atoms follow as a consequence. The arrow tail starts at the electron source (lone pair or bond); the arrow head points to the electron sink (electrophilic atom or antibonding orbital).

### ❌ Misconception 3: "SN1 and SN2 are completely separate"

**The truth:** They're endpoints of a continuum. Many reactions have mixed character. The "decision tree" is a simplification — real reactions can have partial SN1/SN2 character depending on conditions.

### ❌ Misconception 4: "Markovnikov's rule is about where H goes"

**The truth:** Markovnikov's rule is really about carbocation stability. H goes to the less substituted carbon because that creates the MORE STABLE carbocation at the other carbon. The rule is a consequence of Hammond's postulate + carbocation stability, not an arbitrary rule.

### ❌ Misconception 5: "Aromatic rings are unreactive"

**The truth:** Aromatic rings are LESS reactive than alkenes toward addition (because addition destroys aromaticity), but they're quite reactive toward SUBSTITUTION (which preserves aromaticity). The 150 kJ/mol aromatic stabilization energy makes substitution thermodynamically preferred over addition.

### 💪 The Pep Talk

Organic chemistry has a reputation as the "weed-out" course — the class that crushes pre-med dreams. But here's why it won't crush you:

1. **You think mechanistically.** You've been trained in physics to ask "why?" not "what?" Organic chemistry rewards this — every product can be predicted from electron flow.

2. **You know MO theory.** FMO theory (HOMO attacks LUMO) explains ALL of organic reactivity. Most students learn this as a "rule"; you understand the quantum mechanics behind it.

3. **There are only 6 mechanisms.** SN1, SN2, E1, E2, addition, EAS. That's it. Everything else is a variation. Learn these 6 cold, and you can predict any reaction.

The students who fail organic are the ones who try to memorize 200 reactions. The students who ace it are the ones who learn 6 mechanisms and apply them. You're in the second group.

**Recommended resource:** *Organic Chemistry as a Second Language* by David Klein. It's specifically designed for students who struggled the first time. It teaches mechanism-first thinking.

## 🔬 8. Advanced Derivations — Reaction Mechanisms & Selectivity

### 8.1 — The Complete SN1/SN2/E1/E2 Decision Tree

This is the most important decision framework in organic chemistry. Given a substrate + nucleophile/base, which mechanism dominates?

**The Four Mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | Rate Law | Substrate | Nucleophile/Base | Stereochemistry | Solvent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SN2** | $r = k[\text{Sub}][\text{Nuc}]$ | Methyl > 1° > 2° (3° = never) | Strong nucleophile | Inversion (Walden) | Polar aprotic |
| **SN1** | $r = k[\text{Sub}]$ | 3° > 2° > 1° (methyl = never) | Weak nucleophile (solvent) | Racemization | Polar protic |
| **E2** | $r = k[\text{Sub}][\text{Base}]$ | 3° > 2° > 1° | Strong, bulky base | Anti-periplanar required | Any |
| **E1** | $r = k[\text{Sub}]$ | 3° > 2° | Weak base, high T | Zaitsev product | Polar protic |

**The Decision Algorithm:**

```
START: Is the substrate 3°?
├── YES → SN2 impossible (too sterically hindered)
│   ├── Strong base? → E2
│   └── Weak base/nucleophile? → E1 + SN1 (competing)
│       └── High temperature? → E1 favored
│       └── Low temperature? → SN1 favored
│
└── NO → Is the substrate methyl or 1°?
    ├── YES → SN1/E1 impossible (carbocation too unstable)
    │   ├── Strong nucleophile? → SN2
    │   ├── Strong bulky base? → E2
    │   └── Strong non-bulky base/nuc? → SN2 + E2 (competing)
    │
    └── NO (2° substrate) → ALL FOUR compete
        ├── Strong nucleophile, polar aprotic → SN2
        ├── Strong bulky base → E2
        ├── Weak nuc, polar protic, low T → SN1
        └── Weak base, polar protic, high T → E1
```

**Key factors that tip the balance:**

1. **Steric bulk of base:** t-BuOK (bulky) → E2. NaOCH₃ (small) → SN2.
2. **Temperature:** Higher T always favors elimination (ΔS‡ is more positive for E — going from 2 molecules to 1 + leaving group).
3. **Solvent:** Polar aprotic (DMSO, DMF, acetone) → SN2 (naked nucleophile). Polar protic (H₂O, ROH) → SN1/E1 (stabilizes carbocation).
4. **Nucleophile strength:** Good nucleophile ≠ good base. I⁻ is a great nucleophile but weak base → SN2. t-BuO⁻ is a strong base but poor nucleophile (bulky) → E2.

### 8.2 — Carbonyl Chemistry: The Reactivity Hierarchy

The carbonyl group (C=O) is the most important functional group in organic chemistry. Its reactivity comes from:
1. The electrophilic carbon (δ+) — attacked by nucleophiles
2. The acidic α-hydrogen — removed by bases to form enolates

**Nucleophilic addition to carbonyls (aldehydes/ketones):**

$$
\text{Nu}^- + \text{R}_2\text{C=O} \to \text{R}_2\text{C(Nu)(O}^-)
$$

**Nucleophilic acyl substitution (carboxylic acid derivatives):**

$$
\text{Nu}^- + \text{RC(=O)X} \to [\text{RC(Nu)(O}^-)\text{X}] \to \text{RC(=O)Nu} + \text{X}^-
$$

The tetrahedral intermediate collapses by expelling the leaving group. This is why acid chlorides are more reactive than esters — Cl⁻ is a better leaving group than RO⁻.

**Reactivity order of carbonyl compounds:**

$$
\text{Acid chloride} > \text{Anhydride} > \text{Ester} \approx \text{Thioester} > \text{Amide} > \text{Carboxylate}
$$

**Why?** Better leaving group = more reactive. Also, resonance donation from the heteroatom (N in amides, O in esters) stabilizes the carbonyl, making it LESS electrophilic.

### 8.3 — The Aldol Condensation: Complete Mechanism

**The aldol reaction** forms a β-hydroxy carbonyl (aldol) by nucleophilic addition of an enolate to a carbonyl:

**Step 1 — Enolate formation** (base removes α-H):

$$
\text{R-CH}_2\text{-C(=O)-R'} + \text{B}^- \to \text{R-CH}^-\text{-C(=O)-R'} + \text{BH}
$$

The enolate is stabilized by resonance:

$$
\text{R-CH=C(-O}^-)\text{-R'} \longleftrightarrow \text{R-CH}^-\text{-C(=O)-R'}
$$

**Step 2 — Nucleophilic addition** (enolate attacks another carbonyl):

$$
\text{R-CH=C(-O}^-)\text{-R'} + \text{R''-CHO} \to \text{R-CH[C(=O)R']-CH(R'')-O}^-
$$

**Step 3 — Protonation** gives the aldol product (β-hydroxy carbonyl).

**Step 4 — Dehydration** (if heated) gives the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl (aldol condensation):

$$
\text{β-hydroxy carbonyl} \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{α,β-unsaturated carbonyl}
$$

**Why dehydration is favorable:** The product has extended conjugation (C=C-C=O), which is thermodynamically stabilized.

**Crossed aldol (mixed aldol):** When two different carbonyl compounds are present, selectivity is achieved by:
1. Using a non-enolizable aldehyde (e.g., benzaldehyde — no α-H) as the electrophile
2. Pre-forming the enolate of one component (LDA at -78°C) before adding the other
3. Using directed aldol with boron enolates (Zimmerman-Traxler model for stereoselectivity)

### 8.4 — The Claisen Condensation

Like aldol, but with esters instead of aldehydes/ketones. Product: a β-keto ester.

$$
2\,\text{RCH}_2\text{CO}_2\text{Et} \xrightarrow{\text{NaOEt}} \text{RCH}_2\text{C(=O)CH(R)CO}_2\text{Et} + \text{EtOH}
$$

**Key difference from aldol:** The Claisen requires a FULL equivalent of base (not catalytic) because the product is more acidic than the starting ester (the β-keto ester has pKa ~11 vs. ester α-H pKa ~25). The base deprotonates the product, driving the equilibrium forward.

### 8.5 — Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS)

**The general mechanism:**

**Step 1 — Electrophile attacks the π system** (rate-determining):

$$
\text{ArH} + \text{E}^+ \to [\text{Ar(H)(E)}]^+ \quad \text{(arenium ion / σ-complex)}
$$

**Step 2 — Proton loss** (fast, restores aromaticity):

$$
[\text{Ar(H)(E)}]^+ \to \text{ArE} + \text{H}^+
$$

**Why substitution, not addition?** Addition would destroy aromaticity (loss of ~150 kJ/mol stabilization). Substitution preserves the aromatic ring.

**Directing effects:**

| Substituent type | Position | Rate effect | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electron-donating (EDG) | ortho/para | Activating | -OH, -NH₂, -OR, -R |
| Electron-withdrawing (EWG) | meta | Deactivating | -NO₂, -CN, -COOH, -SO₃H |
| Halogens (special) | ortho/para | Deactivating | -F, -Cl, -Br, -I |

**Why halogens are ortho/para directors but deactivating:** They withdraw electrons inductively (deactivating) but donate electrons by resonance (lone pairs into ring → ortho/para directing). The resonance effect controls regiochemistry; the inductive effect controls rate.

### 8.6 — Pericyclic Reactions: The Diels-Alder

**The Diels-Alder reaction** is a [4+2] cycloaddition — a concerted (single-step, no intermediates) reaction between a diene and a dienophile:

$$
\text{Diene (4π)} + \text{Dienophile (2π)} \to \text{Cyclohexene}
$$

**Requirements:**
1. Diene must be in s-cis conformation (both double bonds on same side)
2. Electron-rich diene + electron-poor dienophile (normal demand) — or vice versa (inverse demand)
3. Suprafacial on both components (Woodward-Hoffmann rules: thermally allowed)

**Stereochemistry:**
- **Syn addition:** Both new bonds form on the same face → cis ring junction
- **Endo rule:** In the transition state, the dienophile substituents point TOWARD the diene π system (secondary orbital interactions stabilize endo TS)

**Frontier Molecular Orbital (FMO) explanation:**

The reaction is controlled by the interaction between:
- HOMO of diene + LUMO of dienophile (normal demand)
- The smaller the HOMO-LUMO gap, the faster the reaction

Electron-donating groups on the diene raise its HOMO. Electron-withdrawing groups on the dienophile lower its LUMO. Both effects decrease the gap → accelerate the reaction.

**The Cope rearrangement** ([3,3]-sigmatropic shift):

$$
\text{1,5-hexadiene} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{1,5-hexadiene (rearranged)}
$$

A concerted bond reorganization through a chair-like transition state. The oxy-Cope variant (with an OH group) is driven by tautomerization of the product enol to a ketone (irreversible).

### 8.7 — Retrosynthesis as Graph Search

**Retrosynthetic analysis** works backward from the target molecule to available starting materials. It's formally a graph search problem:

- **Nodes:** Molecules (target, intermediates, starting materials)
- **Edges:** Reactions (transforms)
- **Goal:** Find a path from commercially available starting materials to the target

**The disconnection approach (Corey):**

1. Identify the target molecule's key bonds (strategic bonds to disconnect)
2. Apply a **retrosynthetic transform** (the reverse of a known reaction)
3. Generate a **synthon** (idealized reactive fragment)
4. Find a **synthetic equivalent** (real reagent that acts as the synthon)

**Example — Retrosynthesis of 4-methylpentan-2-one:**

Target: CH₃-CO-CH₂-CH(CH₃)₂

**Disconnection 1:** α to carbonyl (aldol disconnection):

$$
\text{Target} \Rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{CHO} + \text{(CH}_3)_2\text{CHCHO}
$$

Synthons: enolate of acetaldehyde + isobutyraldehyde as electrophile.

**Disconnection 2:** At the C-C bond β to carbonyl (Michael disconnection):

$$
\text{Target} \Rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COCH=CH}_2 + \text{(CH}_3)_2\text{CuLi}
$$

Synthons: methyl vinyl ketone (Michael acceptor) + organocuprate.

**The AI connection:** Modern retrosynthesis tools (ASKCOS, IBM RXN) use transformer neural networks trained on millions of reactions to propose disconnections. This is one of the most successful applications of AI in chemistry — see [[03.8 - Biochemistry & Modern Topics]].

> [!tip] Cross-link to Track 09 (QM)
> Pericyclic reactions are governed by orbital symmetry (Woodward-Hoffmann rules), which come directly from the quantum mechanical properties of molecular orbitals. The Diels-Alder is thermally allowed because the HOMO-LUMO interaction is symmetry-matched (both suprafacial). Photochemically, the selection rules reverse. This is quantum mechanics controlling chemical reactivity.

## 📎 9. Appendix — Deep Dives & Mastery Challenges

### Appendix A — Stereochemistry Mastery

#### R/S Configuration (Cahn-Ingold-Prelog Rules)

**The algorithm:**

1. Assign priority to the four substituents on the stereocenter (highest atomic number = highest priority: 1 > 2 > 3 > 4).
2. Orient the molecule so the lowest priority group (4) points AWAY from you.
3. Trace a path from 1 → 2 → 3:
   - Clockwise = **R** (rectus, Latin for "right")
   - Counterclockwise = **S** (sinister, Latin for "left")

**Tie-breaking rules:**
- If two atoms are identical, go to the next atom along each chain until a difference is found.
- Double bonds count as two single bonds to the same atom (C=O counts as C bonded to O,O and O bonded to C,C).
- Isotopes: higher mass = higher priority (D > H, ¹⁴C > ¹²C).

**Worked Example — (S)-Alanine:**

$$
\text{H}_2\text{N-CH(CH}_3\text{)-COOH}
$$

Stereocenter: the α-carbon. Substituents:
1. NH₂ (N, atomic number 7) — highest
2. COOH (C bonded to O,O,O) — next
3. CH₃ (C bonded to H,H,H) — next
4. H (atomic number 1) — lowest

Orient with H pointing back. Trace NH₂ → COOH → CH₃: counterclockwise = **S**.

#### E/Z Configuration (for alkenes)

**The algorithm:**

1. For each carbon of the C=C, assign priority to its two substituents (same CIP rules).
2. If the two higher-priority groups are on the SAME side: **Z** (zusammen, German for "together").
3. If on OPPOSITE sides: **E** (entgegen, German for "opposite").

**Why E/Z is better than cis/trans:** cis/trans only works for disubstituted alkenes. E/Z works for ALL alkenes, including trisubstituted and tetrasubstituted.

#### Axial Chirality

Some molecules are chiral without a stereocenter — they have a chiral axis:

- **Allenes:** R₁R₂C=C=CR₃R₄ (the two planes are perpendicular)
- **Biaryl compounds:** Restricted rotation around Ar-Ar bond (atropisomerism)
  - Example: BINAP (a chiral ligand used in asymmetric catalysis)
  - Requires ortho substituents large enough to prevent rotation

**Assigning axial chirality:** View along the axis. Assign priorities to the four groups (two on each end). Apply CIP rules as if it were a tetrahedral center viewed from one end.

#### Meso Compounds and Symmetry

A **meso compound** has stereocenters but is achiral due to an internal mirror plane:

Example: (2R,3S)-tartaric acid — has two stereocenters but the molecule is superimposable on its mirror image.

**How to identify meso:** Look for an internal plane of symmetry that relates one stereocenter to another with opposite configuration.

### Appendix C — Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution (SNAr)

Unlike EAS (electrophile attacks ring), SNAr has a NUCLEOPHILE attacking the ring. This requires:
1. Strong electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) on the ring (especially ortho/para to leaving group)
2. A good leaving group (F > Cl > Br > I — opposite to SN2! Because the RDS is addition, not departure)

**Mechanism (addition-elimination):**

**Step 1:** Nucleophile adds to the ring carbon bearing the leaving group → **Meisenheimer complex** (anionic σ-complex, stabilized by EWGs):

$$
\text{Ar-X} + \text{Nu}^- \to [\text{Ar(X)(Nu)}]^- \quad \text{(rate-determining)}
$$

**Step 2:** Leaving group departs (fast):

$$
[\text{Ar(X)(Nu)}]^- \to \text{Ar-Nu} + \text{X}^-
$$

**Why F is the best leaving group in SNAr:** The RDS is nucleophilic ADDITION (Step 1). Fluorine is the most electronegative halogen → most strongly activates the ring toward nucleophilic attack (stabilizes the Meisenheimer complex). The C-F bond breaking (Step 2) is fast and not rate-determining.

**Example:** 2,4-dinitrofluorobenzene + NaOMe → 2,4-dinitroanisole + NaF

This is the **Sanger reagent** — used to identify the N-terminal amino acid of proteins (reacts with the free NH₂ group via SNAr).

> [!danger] 🧠 Common Misconceptions — Section 8 & 9 Summary
> 
> **❌ "SN1 and SN2 are the only substitution mechanisms"**
> **Truth:** There's also SNAr (nucleophilic aromatic substitution), SN1' (allylic), addition-elimination on carbonyls, and radical substitution (SRN1). The SN1/SN2 framework applies specifically to sp³ carbon with a leaving group.
> 
> **❌ "Organic chemistry is just memorizing reactions"**
> **Truth:** There are ~6 fundamental mechanistic steps (proton transfer, nucleophilic addition, elimination, electrophilic addition, radical steps, pericyclic). Every named reaction is a combination of these steps. Learn the mechanisms, not the names.
> 
> **❌ "A chiral molecule must have a stereocenter"**
> **Truth:** Axial chirality (allenes, biaryls), planar chirality (metallocenes), and helical chirality (helicenes) all produce chiral molecules without traditional sp³ stereocenters.
> 
> **❌ "The Diels-Alder always gives the endo product"**
> **Truth:** The endo product is the KINETIC product (faster to form due to secondary orbital interactions). The exo product is often the THERMODYNAMIC product (less steric strain). At high temperatures or long reaction times, exo can dominate.
> 
> **❌ "Aromatic rings are unreactive"**
> **Truth:** Aromatic rings are LESS reactive than alkenes toward electrophilic addition (because addition destroys aromaticity). But they readily undergo electrophilic SUBSTITUTION (preserves aromaticity) and, with strong EWGs, nucleophilic substitution. Aromaticity provides selectivity, not inertness.
> 
> **❌ "Retrosynthesis is creative genius that can't be taught"**
> **Truth:** Retrosynthesis is a systematic algorithm (Corey won the Nobel Prize for formalizing it). It's a graph search with heuristics. AI systems now routinely propose synthetic routes competitive with expert chemists. The skill is learnable — it's pattern recognition + strategic thinking.
