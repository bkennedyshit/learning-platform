# Subject Syllabus: 60 - History of Science & Ideas

*Back to [[00 - 09 - Learning Index|Learning Index]]*

> The difference between knowing that F=ma and knowing that Newton wrote the Principia while hiding from the plague, while Leibniz was simultaneously inventing calculus in Germany, while the Royal Society was deciding who invented it first — the second version sticks. Ideas have authors. Authors have lives. Lives have context. Context makes everything make sense.

## 📝 3. Chapter Outline

| # | Chapter | Core skill |
|---|---------|-----------|
| 60.1 | **The Ancient World — Philosophy as Proto-Science** | Thales of Miletus and the first naturalistic explanation of the world (no gods, just water); the Pre-Socratics and their questions (what is everything made of? what is change?); Pythagoras and the discovery that mathematics describes nature; Aristotle's biological work (the first systematic natural historian); the Library of Alexandria (what was actually lost and what survived); Archimedes (the most extraordinary scientist of antiquity — the Eureka story is real); Islamic Golden Age preservation and extension (820-1258 CE) — the translators who saved Greek science. |
| 60.2 | **The Scientific Revolution (1543-1687)** | Copernicus: heliocentrism (1543 — published as he was dying to avoid persecution); Tycho Brahe's data (the most accurate astronomical observations before telescopes); Kepler's laws (derived from Tycho's data; the first mathematical laws of planetary motion); Galileo: the telescope, Jupiter's moons, and the problem with the Church; the scientific method as a deliberate invention (Bacon, Descartes); Newton: calculus (simultaneously with Leibniz), Principia Mathematica (1687 — derived from plague isolation), the apple and gravity (probably apocryphal but not impossible). |
| 60.3 | **The Mathematics Story** | Euclid and the axiomatic method (everything proved from a small set of starting assumptions — the template for all rigorous thought); the invention of algebra (al-Khwarizmi, Baghdad, 820 CE — the word "algebra" is Arabic); the invention of calculus (Newton/Leibniz, 1666/1675 — simultaneously, independently, and with enormous bitterness); the discovery of non-Euclidean geometry (Gauss, Lobachevsky, Bolyai — and why it nearly broke mathematics); Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931 — there are true statements that cannot be proved within any consistent formal system; this genuinely shook the foundations of mathematics). |
| 60.4 | **Darwin & The Revolution in Biology** | Pre-Darwin biology: Linnaeus's taxonomy; Lamarck's incorrect version of evolution; the Beagle voyage (1831-1836); the Galapagos finches (actually less important to Darwin than the tortoises and mockingbirds); natural selection: the mechanism; Wallace arriving at the same idea simultaneously (Darwin's worst nightmare); *On the Origin of Species* (1859) — what it actually says vs. what people think it says; the reception (not as universally hostile as the myth suggests); Mendel's genetics (1865, discovered in obscurity, rediscovered 1900 — the missing piece Darwin never had); the modern synthesis (1940s — Darwin + Mendel = evolutionary biology). |
| 60.5 | **Einstein & The Physics Revolution** | The ether problem (Michelson-Morley experiment 1887 — light travels the same speed regardless of direction; this shouldn't be possible); Special Relativity (1905 — Einstein's annus mirabilis: 4 papers, any one of which would have made him famous); the constancy of the speed of light as the single axiom; what follows: time dilation, length contraction, mass-energy equivalence (E=mc²); General Relativity (1915 — gravity is the curvature of spacetime; the 1919 solar eclipse confirmation; Eddington's photograph); the photoelectric effect (also 1905 — this is what won him the Nobel; launched quantum mechanics); Einstein's resistance to quantum mechanics ("God does not play dice"). |
| 60.6 | **Quantum Mechanics — The Strangest Theory That Works** | Planck's blackbody radiation problem and the reluctant invention of the quantum (1900); Bohr's atomic model; the double-slit experiment (particles and waves simultaneously — still not resolved philosophically); Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (a fundamental feature of reality, not a measurement limitation); Schrödinger's equation and the wave function; the measurement problem (what does "observation" do to a quantum system?); the Copenhagen interpretation vs. many-worlds vs. pilot wave; Bell's theorem (1964) and experimental confirmation that local hidden variables can't explain quantum correlations; quantum computing as the application. Cross-link: [[01 - Math and Physics/Subject_Plan]] |
| 60.7 | **The Information Age — From Shannon to AI** | Claude Shannon and information theory (1948 — bit as the unit of information; the channel capacity theorem; entropy as information measure); von Neumann architecture (the stored-program computer); Turing's theoretical contributions (Turing machine as the theoretical model for all computation; the Halting Problem; the Turing Test); the transistor (1947, Bell Labs — the most important invention of the 20th century); Moore's Law (transistors double every 18-24 months — observed 1965, slowing now); the internet (ARPANET 1969 → TCP/IP 1974 → WWW 1991 — Berners-Lee's gift to humanity, unpatented); deep learning's arrival (2012 AlexNet won ImageNet by an enormous margin — the moment everything changed). |
| 60.8 | **The History of Psychology & Cognitive Science** | Wundt's laboratory (1879 — psychology as a separate science from philosophy); William James and functionalism; Freud (1900-1939 — enormous cultural influence, questionable scientific validity, but as an *intellectual* event, foundational); Watson and behaviorism (only observable behavior matters — led to Skinner and radical behaviorism); the cognitive revolution (1956 — Miller's "The Magical Number Seven," Chomsky's demolition of Skinner, the first computer models of cognition); Kahneman and Tversky and behavioral economics (1970s-2000s — how human reasoning actually works, not how it should); the neuroscience turn (MRI, optogenetics, connectomics). |
