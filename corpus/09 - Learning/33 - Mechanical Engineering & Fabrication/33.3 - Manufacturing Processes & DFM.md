# 33.3 — Manufacturing Processes & DFM

> *"The best DFM rule is: if you wouldn't want to make it, don't design it."*

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Describe 4 major subtractive, 4 additive, and 4 forming/shaping processes with their achievable tolerance and surface finish ranges.
2. Apply a **process selection matrix** (volume × geometry × material) to choose the right process for a given part.
3. Apply 5+ **DFM rules** for CNC machining, 5+ for FDM 3D printing, and 3+ for sheet metal.
4. Explain the **tooling cost vs unit cost** trade-off and when injection moulding beats CNC.
5. Explain DFMA (Design for Manufacture AND Assembly) and apply it to reduce part count.
6. Use Hubs/Xometry online DFM checker outputs to identify design issues before ordering.

## 📚 1. Subtractive Processes

Material is removed from a solid block (stock) to create the part. Also called "machining" or "cutting."

### 1.1 Turning (Lathe)

- Workpiece rotates; single-point cutting tool traverses
- **Best for:** Cylindrical parts, shafts, bushings, threaded components
- **Tolerance:** IT6–IT8 standard; IT5 with grinding
- **Surface finish:** Ra 0.8–3.2 µm typical
- **Key operations:** Facing, turning, boring, threading, parting, grooving
- **DFM:** Design all features that can be made by turning before resorting to milling. Combine operations.

### 1.2 Milling

- Rotating multi-tooth cutter; workpiece fixed (usually)
- **Best for:** Flat surfaces, pockets, slots, profiles, complex 3D surfaces
- **Tolerance:** ±0.025–0.1 mm typical CNC
- **Operations:** Face mill, end mill, slot mill, bore mill, thread mill
- **Variants:** 3-axis (most shops), 4-axis (rotary), 5-axis (complex undercuts)

### 1.3 Drilling

- Twist drill or spade drill for through/blind holes
- **Minimum diameter:** ~0.3mm (micro-drilling)
- **Tolerance:** ±0.1–0.2mm (drill); ±0.01mm after reaming
- **Key rule:** Use standard drill sizes. Non-standard requires custom tools or slow end-mill boring.

### 1.4 Grinding

- Abrasive wheel removes small amounts of material
- **Best for:** Very tight tolerances, hard materials (hardened steel, ceramics)
- **Tolerance:** IT4–IT6 (±0.005–0.010 mm)
- **Surface finish:** Ra 0.1–0.8 µm
- **Use cases:** Bearing races, gauge surfaces, tool sharpening

### 1.5 Laser Cutting / Waterjet

- Laser: 2D sheet parts from flat stock; kerf ~0.1–0.3mm
- Waterjet: Any material; no heat-affected zone; kerf ~0.8–1.5mm
- **Tolerance:** ±0.1–0.25mm typical
- **Best for:** Sheet metal brackets, gaskets, flat profiles, enclosure panels

## 📚 2. Additive Processes

Material is deposited, cured, or sintered layer by layer. Covered in depth in [[33.5 - 3D Printing & Additive Manufacturing]].

| Process | Material class | Tolerance | Surface Ra | Notes |
|---------|--------------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| FDM | Thermoplastics | ±0.2–0.5mm | 6–25 µm | Anisotropic, cheap, fast |
| SLA/MSLA | Photopolymer resin | ±0.05–0.1mm | 1–5 µm | Brittle, excellent detail |
| SLS | Nylon, PA, TPU powder | ±0.3mm | 8–15 µm | No support, functional |
| DMLS/SLM | Metal (SS, Ti, Al, Inconel) | ±0.1–0.2mm | 6–15 µm | Post-machine critical surfaces |

## 📚 3. Forming Processes

Shape is created by deforming material rather than removing it.

### 3.1 Sheet Metal Bending

- Press brake forces sheet over a die to create bends
- **K-factor:** The neutral axis shift ratio — affects developed length calculation
  - K = 0.33 for tight bends (inside R < thickness), K = 0.5 for large radius
  - Developed (flat) length = (A + B) - 2×(t × tan(α/2)) + 2×K×t×(π×α/180)
- **Spring-back:** Metal springs back slightly after bending — die angle overshoots by 2–5°
- **Minimum bend radius:** Typically 1–3× material thickness for most materials
- **DFM:** Holes must be ≥ 1× material thickness from bend line (else deforms on bend)

### 3.2 Deep Drawing

- Sheet metal formed into a cup/can shape by punch and die
- **Use:** Enclosures, cans, pots, automotive panels
- **Limit:** Draw ratio (punch diameter / blank diameter) < 2.0 typically

### 3.3 Injection Moulding

- Molten thermoplastic injected under pressure into a closed die
- **Tolerance:** ±0.05–0.2mm (post-shrinkage, material dependent)
- **Tooling cost:** $5,000–$200,000+ (single or multi-cavity)
- **Unit cost:** Very low at volume (pennies to dollars per part)
- **Cycle time:** 15–60 seconds typical
- **Key constraint:** Draft angles (0.5–3°) on all vertical walls for part ejection

### 3.4 Die Casting

- Molten aluminium or zinc alloy forced into steel die under high pressure
- **Tolerance:** ±0.05–0.25mm
- **Tooling:** $10,000–$100,000+
- **Surface:** Ra 1.0–2.5 µm as-cast; can be machined/polished further
- **Use:** Consumer electronics enclosures (MacBook body is die-cast Al)

### 3.5 Forging

- Metal heated and worked under compressive force (hammer/press)
- **Result:** Grain flow follows part shape → excellent fatigue and impact strength
- **Use:** Critical structural components: connecting rods, suspension arms, aircraft fittings
- **Note:** Forging does NOT produce accurate dimensions — always machine after

## 📚 4. DFM — Design for Manufacturability

### 4.1 Universal DFM Principles

1. **Minimise setups.** Every time a part must be re-fixtured, cost and error increase. Design to complete in 1–2 setups.
2. **Standardise features.** Use standard drill sizes, standard thread sizes, standard fits. Non-standard = custom tooling = cost.
3. **Design for worst tolerance.** Don't specify tight tolerances where loose will work. Each order of magnitude tighter ≈ 2–5× cost increase.
4. **Self-locating features.** Dowel pins, mating bosses, and snaps reduce assembly error without measurement.
5. **Avoid thin walls and sharp internal corners.** Both cause stress concentrations and manufacturing difficulty.

### 4.2 CNC Machining DFM Rules

| Rule | Limit | Why |
|------|-------|-----|
| Minimum pocket corner radius | = tool radius (add 10% clearance) | End mill is round — sharp internal corners are physically impossible |
| Maximum pocket depth | ≤ 4× width | Chatter and tool deflection beyond this |
| Minimum wall thickness (Al) | ≥ 0.8mm (machined) | Thinner walls vibrate and are difficult to hold |
| Thread depth | ≤ 2.5× diameter | Returns beyond this add no strength (thread strips at root) |
| Avoid undercuts | Design out if possible | Undercuts require special tools or 4/5-axis setups |
| Hole tolerance | Use standard drill sizes | Non-standard sizes require custom tooling or slow boring |
| Feature access | All features accessible from one setup direction | Avoid features needing 3+ setups |

### 4.3 FDM 3D Printing DFM Rules

| Rule | Value | Notes |
|------|-------|-------|
| Max overhang angle | ≤ 45° without support | Above this, drooping occurs |
| Minimum feature size | ≥ 1.5× nozzle diameter | 0.4mm nozzle → ≥ 0.6mm feature |
| Minimum wall | ≥ 2× wall lines = 0.8mm | Below this, walls are inconsistent |
| Hole compensation | Add 0.2–0.3mm | FDM holes print undersized |
| Threads | Use heat-set inserts (M3+) | Printed threads strip easily |
| Layer direction | Align load with XY plane | Z layers are weakest |
| Avoid large flat areas | Add chamfer/fillets | Warping and adhesion issues |

### 4.4 Sheet Metal DFM Rules

| Rule | Limit | Why |
|------|-------|-----|
| Minimum hole diameter | ≥ material thickness | Punch strength limit |
| Hole to edge | ≥ 1.5× material thickness | Edge deformation |
| Hole to bend | ≥ 1.5× material thickness | Hole distorts on bend |
| Minimum bend radius | ≥ 1× thickness (ductile materials) | Cracking at bend |
| Maximum aspect ratio | ≤ 4:1 (long narrow parts) | Difficult to press flat |

### 4.5 Injection Moulding DFM Rules

| Rule | Guidance |
|------|---------|
| Draft angles | 0.5–3° on all surfaces parallel to draw direction |
| Nominal wall thickness | 1.5–4mm; avoid thick bosses (sink marks) |
| Ribs | Height ≤ 3× wall; thickness = 0.5–0.7× wall |
| Gate location | Gate on thick section; uniform flow distance |
| Undercuts | Avoid or use side action/lifters (cost $$$) |

## 📚 5. DFMA — Design for Manufacture AND Assembly

**Boothroyd & Dewhurst methodology:** Count and reduce:
1. **Part count** — can this feature be integral to the parent part?
2. **Assembly operations** — screws, welds, adhesive bonds
3. **Reorientation** — every time assembler flips the part is waste

**Questions for each part:**
1. Does it move relative to other parts during operation? (If no, candidate for integration)
2. Must it be different material from adjacent parts? (If no, candidate for integration)
3. Must it be separate for assembly or maintenance? (If no, integrate)

**Target:** Reduce part count by 30–50% on initial design review. Every screw eliminated is a screw that can't fall out, can't be over-torqued, and doesn't need to be in inventory.

## 🛠️ 6. Worked Example — Electronics Enclosure Process Selection

**Part:** Waterproof electronics enclosure, 150×100×40mm, IP54 rating needed.

**Volume options and break-even:**

| Process | Tooling cost | Unit cost (100) | Unit cost (1000) | Break-even with FDM |
|---------|-------------|----------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| FDM (ASA) | $0 | $8 | $6 | — |
| CNC Al | $0 (no tooling) | $120 | $90 | Never (use for custom) |
| Sheet metal | ~$500 (laser+bend fixtures) | $35 | $22 | ~25 units |
| Die cast Al | $30,000 | $5 | $3 | ~2,000 units |
| Injection mould (ABS) | $20,000 | $4 | $2 | ~3,000 units |

**Decision:**
- Prototype: FDM ASA (UV stable, reasonably watertight with silicone gasket)
- Small production run (50–200 units): Sheet metal + laser cut + FDM printed front panel
- Mass production (1,000+): Injection moulded ABS or die-cast Al

## ⚠️ 7. Common Misconceptions

1. **"CNC can make any shape."** 3-axis CNC cannot make undercuts, internal spherical cavities, or features that require the tool to approach from more than 3 orthogonal directions. 5-axis extends this dramatically but costs more.

2. **"3D printing is always faster than CNC for prototypes."** For simple machined parts, CNC can be faster and stronger. FDM wins on complexity (undercuts, internal channels) and absolute speed for large flat parts.

3. **"Injection moulded parts are perfectly precise."** Plastic shrinks after moulding (0.5–3% depending on material). Draft angles and shrinkage must be designed in. Weld lines (where melt fronts meet) are weak points.

4. **"DFM is done after the design."** DFM must be considered *during* design, not as a review step. Late DFM changes are expensive. Early DFM changes are free.

5. **"Forged = machined to tighter tolerances."** Forging produces rough shape with good grain flow. Always requires machining to final dimensions. Forging + machining = superior fatigue properties, not just "stronger metal."

6. **"I can calculate tolerances after choosing the process."** Wrong order. Choose the process first — it determines the achievable tolerance. Then check if that tolerance satisfies the design requirement. If not, choose a tighter-tolerance process.
