# 43.7 — Advanced Syntax: Subordinate Clauses & Rhetorical Style

*Back to [[Bill's Vault/05-Knowledge_Foundation/09 - Learning/43 - Latin/Subject_Plan]] | [[43.6 - Participles, Gerunds & The Subjunctive|← 43.6]] | [[43.8 - Reading Latin — Caesar, Cicero, Virgil & Ovid|43.8 →]]*

> **Chapter goal:** Command the full range of Latin subordinate clause types; master conditional sentences in all three types; understand the Ciceronian periodic sentence and Caesar's parataxis — the two dominant prose styles of classical Latin.

---

## 1. Conditional Sentences — Three Types

Latin conditionals are the most systematically organised in any classical language. Each type uses a specific combination of moods and tenses:

### Type 1: Simple/Open Conditions (real or assumed as possible)

**If... (present/past fact or logical possibility)**

| Clause | Mood/Tense | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| si-clause | Indicative (any tense) | *Sī hoc facis,* |
| main clause | Indicative (any tense) | *errās.* — If you do this, you are wrong. |

*Sī Caesar vēnit, gaudeō.* — If Caesar comes/came, I am/was glad.
(Both indicative — no special subjunctive needed for real/simple conditions)

### Type 2: Future More Vivid (future possible)

| Clause | Mood/Tense | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| si-clause | **Future** or **Future perfect** indicative | *Sī hoc facies/fēceris,* |
| main clause | **Future** indicative | *errābis.* — If you do/will do this, you will be wrong. |

*Sī vēnerit, loquar.* — If he comes (lit. will have come), I will speak.

### Type 3: Present Contrary-to-Fact (hypothetical — not happening now)

| Clause | Mood/Tense | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| si-clause | **Imperfect subjunctive** | *Sī hoc facerēs,* |
| main clause | **Imperfect subjunctive** | *errārēs.* — If you were doing this (but you're not), you would be wrong. |

*Sī essem dīves, multum darem.* — If I were rich (but I'm not), I would give much.

### Type 4: Past Contrary-to-Fact (impossible — didn't happen)

| Clause | Mood/Tense | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| si-clause | **Pluperfect subjunctive** | *Sī hoc fēcissēs,* |
| main clause | **Pluperfect subjunctive** | *errāvissēs.* — If you had done this (but you didn't), you would have been wrong. |

*Sī vēnisset, gauīssem.* — If he had come (but he didn't), I would have rejoiced.

> **Mixed conditions** are possible: pluperfect subj. in si-clause + imperfect subj. in main clause when the condition is past but the result is present: *Sī Rōmae nātus essēs, Rōmānus nunc essēs.* — If you had been born in Rome, you would now be Roman.

## 2. Cum Clauses

*Cum* + subjunctive is one of the most frequent constructions in all Latin prose, especially Caesar.

| Type | Mood | Tense | Meaning |
|------|------|-------|---------|
| **Circumstantial** | Subjunctive | Imperfect/Pluperfect | when / at the time that |
| **Causal** | Subjunctive | Imperfect/Pluperfect | since / because |
| **Concessive** | Subjunctive | Imperfect/Pluperfect | although |
| **Temporal (inversum)** | Indicative | Present/Perfect | just when / suddenly when |

*Cum Caesar in Galliam pervēnisset, Helvētiī iam profectī erant.*
→ When Caesar had arrived in Gaul, the Helvetii had already set out. (circumstantial — plupf. subj.)

*Cum id scirem, tamen dīxī.*
→ Although I knew this, I nevertheless said it. (concessive — impf. subj.)

*Cum haec dīcēbat, omnēs rīdēbant.*
→ When(ever) he said these things, everyone laughed. (circumstantial — impf. subj.)

## 3. Indirect Command (*ut/nē* + subjunctive)

After verbs of commanding, requesting, urging, persuading, and permitting, Latin uses *ut/nē* + subjunctive (not accusative + infinitive as in indirect statement):

**Verbs:** *imperō, rogō, ōrō, petō, moneō, hortor, persuādeō, cūrō*

*Imperāvit mīlitibus ut pugnārent.* — He ordered the soldiers to fight.
*Ōrāvit eam nē discēderet.* — He begged her not to leave.
*Caesar mīlitēs hortātus est ut fortiter pugnārent.* — Caesar urged the soldiers to fight bravely.

> **Contrast with indirect statement:** *dīxī + acc. + inf.* vs. *imperāvī + ut + subj.* — the construction depends on whether the main verb is one of saying/thinking (acc. + inf.) or commanding/requesting (ut/nē + subj.).

---

## 4. Relative Clauses of Characteristic

A relative clause with the subjunctive expresses **characteristic or generic quality** — "the kind of person who would..." rather than identifying a specific person:

*Est quī hoc faciat.* (subj.) — There is someone who would do this. / There is the kind of person who does this.
*Est quī hoc facit.* (ind.) — There is someone who does this. (specific person)

*Nēmō est quī tē nōn amet.* — There is no one who does not love you. (characteristic)
*Quaerēbant quī haec fēcisset.* — They were looking for someone who had done these things.

---

## 5. Indirect Question (Review + Extension)

Introduced by an interrogative word (*quis, quid, ubi, cur, quōmodo, quandō, utrum...an, -ne*) + subjunctive. Follow sequence of tenses.

*Rogāvit cūr hoc fēcissem.* — He asked why I had done this. (secondary: plpf. subj. for prior action)
*Nescīō utrum veniat an maneat.* — I don't know whether he is coming or staying. (primary)
*Mīrātus est quōmodo id factum esset.* — He wondered how that had been done.

---

## 6. Result Clauses (*ita/tam/tantus/tālis...ut* + subjunctive)

**Signal words in main clause:** *tam* (so), *ita* (in such a way), *adeo* (to such a degree), *tantus* (so great), *tālis* (such)

*Tam fortis erat ut omnēs eum admīrārentur.* — He was so brave that everyone admired him.
*Ita dīxit ut omnēs crēderent.* — He spoke in such a way that everyone believed.
*Tantus timor incessit ut nēmo pugnāret.* — Such great fear came upon them that no one fought.

> **Result vs. Purpose:** Purpose (*ut* + subj.) = intention of the subject; Result (*ut* + subj. with signal word) = consequence of the action. Both use *ut* + subjunctive — the signal word and context distinguish them.

## 7. Two Latin Prose Styles

### 7.1 Caesar's Parataxis

Caesar's *Commentarii de Bello Gallico* is the **gold standard of clear Latin prose.** His style is characterised by:

- **Parataxis** — short main clauses coordinated with *et, -que, atque, sed, tamen* rather than nested subordination
- **Third person throughout** — Caesar writes about himself as "Caesar" (*"Caesar iussit"*) — creates the effect of a military dispatch
- **Ablative absolute** at the start of sentences to set the scene: *"His rebus cognitīs, Caesar..."* — When these things had been learned, Caesar...
- **Indirect statement** constantly — Caesar is always reporting what he was told, what he observed, what the enemy thought
- **Brevity:** long periodic sentences are rare; every word carries weight
- **Impersonal passive:** *"nūntiātum est"* (it was reported), *"constitūtum est"* (it was decided) — typical of Roman military/administrative style

**Model sentence:**
*Eōdem die ab explōrātōribus certior factus hostēs sub monte consēdisse mīlitēsque eōrum ad pēdem montis posuisse, decimā legiōne praesidiō relictā, reliquās cōpiās in tria corpora distribuit.*
→ On the same day, having been informed by scouts that the enemies had settled below the mountain and had stationed their troops at the foot of the mountain, with the tenth legion left as a guard, he distributed the remaining forces into three bodies.

### 7.2 Cicero's Periodic Style (Hypotaxis)

Cicero's speeches and philosophical works use **hypotaxis** — deeply nested subordinate clauses that build to a climactic main clause at the end. The reader (listener) is kept in suspense until the final word resolves the sentence.

**Characteristics:**
- **Long periodic sentences** built from *cum* clauses, *si* clauses, relative clauses, ablative absolutes, all cascading before a main clause
- **Tricolon** (three parallel elements, often in ascending order of length/importance)
- **Anaphora** (repetition of a word at the start of successive phrases): *"Quō usque tandem... Quō usque..."*
- **Chiasmus** (A-B-B-A structure): *"fortis in armīs, in pace modestus"* — brave in arms, moderate in peace
- **Clausulae** (rhythmic endings to cola and periods — Cicero's prose has specific rhythmic patterns *esse videātur* etc.)
- **Periodic structure:** everything subordinated until the final verb; the main verb often last

**Model sentence (opening of *In Catilinam* I):**
*Quō usque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā? Quam diū etiam furor iste tuus nōs ēlūdet? Quem ad fīnem sēsē effrēnāta iactābit audācia?*
→ How long, Catiline, will you abuse our patience? How much longer will that madness of yours elude us? To what end will your unbridled audacity flaunt itself?

## 8. Key Rhetorical Devices in Latin Literature

| Device | Definition | Latin example |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **Anaphora** | Repetition at start of successive clauses | *"Nihil agis, nihil moliris, nihil cogitas..."* |
| **Chiasmus** | A-B-B-A structure | *"Ōrātor fit, poēta nāscitur"* — an orator is made, a poet is born |
| **Tricolon** | Three parallel elements (ascending) | *"vēnī, vīdī, vīcī"* |
| **Litotes** | Understatement by double negation | *"haud ignōbilis"* — not ignoble = quite distinguished |
| **Hendiadys** | One concept expressed by two nouns with *et* | *"sceptrō et lūmine"* = with the light of the scepter |
| **Asyndeton** | Omission of conjunctions for speed/punch | *"vēnī, vīdī, vīcī"* |
| **Polysyndeton** | Many conjunctions slowing/emphasising | *"et... et... et..."* |
| **Apostrophe** | Direct address to absent person/thing | *"Ō patria! Ō dī immortālēs!"* |

## 9. Common Misconceptions / Where Students Fail

1. **Using subjunctive in open conditions:** Type 1 conditions use the indicative — no subjunctive. *"Sī hoc facis, errās."* Not *"Sī hoc faciās, errēs."* (that would be a different meaning — contrary-to-fact).
2. **Confusing purpose and result clauses:** Both use *ut* + subjunctive. The signal word (*tam, ita, tantus*) in the main clause marks result; the absence of such a word favours purpose.
3. **Confusing cum + indicative and cum + subjunctive:** *cum* + indicative = purely temporal ("at the time when"); *cum* + subjunctive = circumstantial/causal/concessive ("when/since/although").
4. **Indirect command vs. indirect statement:** After *imperō/rogō* → *ut* + subj. After *dīcō/putō* → accusative + infinitive. The construction depends entirely on the governing verb.
5. **Sequence of tenses in indirect questions:** Students use the wrong subjunctive tense because they don't identify whether the main verb is primary or secondary sequence.
