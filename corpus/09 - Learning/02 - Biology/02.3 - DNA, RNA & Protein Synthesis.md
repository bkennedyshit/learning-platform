# 02.3 — DNA, RNA & Protein Synthesis

> *"We wish to suggest a structure for the salt of deoxyribose nucleic acid (D.N.A.). This structure has novel features which are of considerable biological interest."*
> — **James Watson & Francis Crick**, *Nature* 171:737 (1953)

The Central Dogma of molecular biology — DNA → RNA → Protein — is the information pipeline of life. This chapter derives each step from molecular first principles: how DNA stores information, how transcription copies it to mRNA, how the ribosome decodes mRNA into protein, and how cells regulate this flow at every level. For the AI-minded: this is biology's original sequence-to-sequence model, complete with encoding, decoding, attention mechanisms (transcription factors), and post-processing (protein folding).

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 02.3.1 — DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid)

**DNA** is a double-stranded polymer of nucleotides that stores genetic information. Structure:
- **Backbone**: Alternating deoxyribose sugar and phosphate groups linked by 3'→5' phosphodiester bonds
- **Bases**: Purines (Adenine, Guanine — two rings) and Pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine — one ring)
- **Base pairing**: A=T (2 hydrogen bonds), G≡C (3 hydrogen bonds)
- **Antiparallel**: One strand runs 5'→3', the complementary strand runs 3'→5'
- **Right-handed helix**: B-form DNA has 10 bp per turn, 3.4 nm pitch, 2 nm diameter

**Information content:** 4 bases → 2 bits per base pair. Human genome: 3.2 × 10⁹ bp = 6.4 Gbits ≈ 800 MB (uncompressed).

### Definition 02.3.2 — The Central Dogma (Crick, 1958)

The **Central Dogma** describes the flow of genetic information:

$$
\text{DNA} \xrightarrow{\text{transcription}} \text{RNA} \xrightarrow{\text{translation}} \text{Protein}
$$

Additional flows:
- DNA → DNA (replication)
- RNA → DNA (reverse transcription — retroviruses, telomerase)
- RNA → RNA (RNA replication — RNA viruses)

**Forbidden flow:** Protein → DNA/RNA (information does not flow backward from protein to nucleic acid).

### Definition 02.3.3 — Genetic Code

The **genetic code** maps 64 codons (triplets of nucleotides) to 20 amino acids + 1 stop signal:
- **Degenerate** (redundant): Most amino acids encoded by 2–6 codons (wobble position)
- **Unambiguous**: Each codon specifies exactly one amino acid
- **Universal**: Nearly identical across all life (minor variations in mitochondria, some protists)
- **Non-overlapping**: Read in consecutive, non-overlapping triplets from a fixed start (AUG)

Start codon: AUG (methionine) — also sets the reading frame
Stop codons: UAA (ochre), UAG (amber), UGA (opal)

### Definition 02.3.4 — Transcription

**Transcription** is the synthesis of RNA from a DNA template by RNA polymerase:

$$
\text{DNA template (3'→5')} + \text{NTPs} \xrightarrow{\text{RNA Pol}} \text{mRNA (5'→3')} + \text{PP}_i
$$

Key features:
- RNA polymerase reads template 3'→5', synthesizes mRNA 5'→3'
- Uses ribonucleoside triphosphates (ATP, UTP, GTP, CTP)
- No primer required (unlike DNA replication)
- Error rate: ~1 per 10⁴–10⁵ bases (no proofreading)

### Definition 02.3.5 — Translation

**Translation** is the synthesis of protein from mRNA by ribosomes:

$$
\text{mRNA} + \text{tRNAs} + \text{amino acids} + \text{energy} \xrightarrow{\text{ribosome}} \text{polypeptide}
$$

Energy cost per amino acid added:
- 1 ATP (aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase charging)
- 1 GTP (EF-Tu delivery to A-site)
- 1 GTP (translocation by EF-G)
- Total: ~4 high-energy phosphate bonds per peptide bond

### Definition 02.3.6 — Gene Regulation

**Gene regulation** controls when, where, and how much of a gene product is made. Levels:

| Level | Mechanism | Timescale |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Epigenetic | DNA methylation, histone modification | Hours–permanent |
| Transcriptional | Transcription factors, enhancers, silencers | Minutes–hours |
| Post-transcriptional | Splicing, mRNA stability, miRNA | Minutes |
| Translational | Initiation factor regulation, mTOR | Minutes |
| Post-translational | Phosphorylation, ubiquitination, proteolysis | Seconds–minutes |

## 🔬 2. Biological Mechanisms

### 2.1 — DNA Replication (Semiconservative)

DNA replication produces two identical copies, each containing one original and one new strand:

**Step 1 — Origin Recognition:**
- Origin Recognition Complex (ORC) binds replication origins (humans: ~30,000–50,000 origins)
- Helicase (MCM complex) loaded in G1 phase (licensing)

**Step 2 — Unwinding:**
- Helicase unwinds double helix (ATP-dependent), creating replication fork
- Single-strand binding proteins (SSB/RPA) stabilize single-stranded DNA
- Topoisomerase relieves torsional strain ahead of fork

**Step 3 — Priming:**
- Primase synthesizes short RNA primer (~10 nt) — provides 3'-OH for DNA polymerase

**Step 4 — Elongation:**
- **Leading strand**: DNA Pol III (prokaryotes) / Pol ε (eukaryotes) synthesizes continuously 5'→3'
- **Lagging strand**: Synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments (~100–200 nt in eukaryotes)
  - Each fragment needs a new RNA primer
  - DNA Pol I (prok) / Pol δ (euk) removes primers, fills gaps
  - DNA ligase seals nicks (phosphodiester bond formation)

**Step 5 — Proofreading:**
- 3'→5' exonuclease activity of DNA polymerase (error rate: 1 per 10⁷)
- Mismatch repair (MMR) post-replication (final error rate: ~1 per 10⁹–10¹⁰)

**Replication speed:** ~1000 nt/s (prokaryotes), ~50 nt/s (eukaryotes, but many simultaneous forks)

### 2.2 — Transcription (Eukaryotic, RNA Pol II)

**Phase 1 — Initiation:**

1. **TFIID** binds TATA box (located ~25–30 bp upstream of transcription start site)
   - TBP (TATA-binding protein) subunit recognizes TATA via minor groove
2. **TFIIB** bridges TFIID and RNA Pol II, positions polymerase
3. **RNA Pol II** recruited (12-subunit complex, ~500 kDa)
4. **TFIIH** phosphorylates CTD (C-terminal domain) of Pol II → promoter escape
   - CTD has 52 repeats of YSPTSPS heptad; Ser5 phosphorylation = initiation

**Phase 2 — Elongation:**

$$
\text{RNA Pol II} + \text{NTP} \rightarrow \text{RNA}_{n+1} + \text{PP}_i \quad (\text{rate: 20–40 nt/s})
$$

- Pol II moves along template strand (3'→5'), synthesizing mRNA (5'→3')
- Elongation factors (TFIIS, P-TEFb) prevent pausing and arrest
- CTD Ser2 phosphorylation during elongation recruits RNA processing factors

**Phase 3 — Termination:**
- Polyadenylation signal (AAUAAA) recognized by CPSF/CstF complex
- RNA cleaved ~20 nt downstream of signal
- Poly(A) polymerase adds 100–250 adenines (poly-A tail)
- Pol II continues transcribing; Rat1 exonuclease degrades remaining RNA → Pol II release (torpedo model)

### 2.3 — RNA Processing (Pre-mRNA → Mature mRNA)

Three co-transcriptional modifications convert pre-mRNA to export-ready mature mRNA:

**1. 5' Capping** (occurs after ~20–30 nt synthesized):
- Guanylyltransferase adds GMP in 5'→5' linkage
- Methyltransferase adds methyl group → 7-methylguanosine cap (m⁷G)
- Functions: Ribosome recognition, mRNA stability, splicing of first intron

**2. Splicing** (removal of introns):

The **spliceosome** (5 snRNPs: U1, U2, U4, U5, U6 + ~200 proteins) catalyzes intron removal:

$$
\text{Exon 1}—\underbrace{\text{GU...branch point A...AG}}_{\text{intron}}—\text{Exon 2}
$$

Mechanism (two transesterification reactions):
1. **First step:** 2'-OH of branch-point adenosine attacks 5' splice site (GU) → lariat intermediate + free exon 1
2. **Second step:** 3'-OH of exon 1 attacks 3' splice site (AG) → exons joined + lariat released

**Alternative splicing:** One gene → multiple mRNAs → multiple proteins. Human genes average ~8 exons; ~95% undergo alternative splicing. This explains how ~20,000 genes produce ~100,000+ protein variants.

**3. 3' Polyadenylation:**
- AAUAAA signal + downstream GU-rich element
- Cleavage + poly(A) polymerase adds ~200 A residues
- Functions: mRNA stability, nuclear export, translation initiation

### 2.4 — Translation (Ribosomal Protein Synthesis)

**Phase 1 — Initiation (rate-limiting):**

Eukaryotic initiation:
1. **eIF4E** binds 5' cap; **eIF4G** scaffolds; **eIF4A** (helicase) unwinds 5' UTR secondary structure
2. **43S pre-initiation complex** (40S subunit + eIF1, eIF1A, eIF3, eIF5, Met-tRNAi·eIF2·GTP) recruited to 5' cap
3. **Scanning**: 43S complex scans 5'→3' until AUG in Kozak context (gcc**A/G**ccAUGG)
4. **Start codon recognition**: eIF2 hydrolyzes GTP → Pi released → 60S subunit joins → 80S ribosome

**Phase 2 — Elongation (~6 amino acids/second):**

Three sites on ribosome: A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl), E (exit)

Cycle per amino acid:
1. **Decoding**: Aminoacyl-tRNA·EF-Tu·GTP enters A-site; codon-anticodon match → GTP hydrolysis → EF-Tu released
2. **Peptide bond formation**: Peptidyl transferase (23S rRNA = ribozyme) catalyzes peptide bond between P-site peptide and A-site amino acid
3. **Translocation**: EF-G·GTP → ribosome moves one codon 5'→3'; A-site tRNA → P-site; P-site tRNA → E-site

**Phase 3 — Termination:**
1. Stop codon (UAA/UAG/UGA) enters A-site
2. Release factor (eRF1) recognizes stop codon (mimics tRNA shape)
3. eRF3·GTP hydrolysis → peptide released from P-site tRNA
4. Ribosome recycling factor + eIF3 → subunits dissociate

### 2.5 — Post-Translational Modifications (PTMs)

The polypeptide chain is not the final product. PTMs expand the proteome:

| Modification | Enzyme | Function | Example |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Phosphorylation | Kinases / phosphatases | Signal switching (on/off) | MAPK cascade |
| Glycosylation | Glycosyltransferases | Protein stability, cell recognition | Antibodies, mucins |
| Ubiquitination | E1/E2/E3 ligases | Proteasomal degradation tag | Cell cycle regulation |
| Acetylation | HATs / HDACs | Histone regulation, protein stability | Histone H3K27ac |
| Methylation | Methyltransferases | Epigenetic marks, protein-protein interaction | Histone H3K4me3 |
| Proteolytic cleavage | Proteases | Activation of zymogens | Insulin (proinsulin → insulin) |
| Disulfide bonds | PDI (ER) | Protein folding stability | Antibodies, extracellular proteins |

### 2.6 — Transcription Factor Logic

Gene expression is controlled by combinatorial transcription factor (TF) binding:

**Promoter elements:**
- TATA box (−25 to −30): Core promoter, positions Pol II
- Initiator (Inr): Alternative core promoter element
- CpG islands: GC-rich regions, often unmethylated in active genes

**Regulatory elements:**
- **Enhancers**: Increase transcription (can be 100s of kb away; act via DNA looping)
- **Silencers**: Decrease transcription
- **Insulators**: Block enhancer-promoter communication

**Combinatorial control:** A typical human gene has 5–10 TF binding sites. Expression requires the correct combination of TFs present — this is biological **AND/OR logic**:

$$
\text{Gene ON} = (\text{TF}_A \text{ AND } \text{TF}_B) \text{ AND NOT } \text{Repressor}_C
$$

This combinatorial logic with ~1,500 human TFs controlling ~20,000 genes is how 200+ cell types arise from one genome.

## 📐 3. Mathematical Models

### 3.1 — Information Content of the Genetic Code

Shannon entropy of the genetic code:

$$
H = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log_2 p_i
$$

For DNA with 4 equiprobable bases: $H = -4 \times (1/4) \log_2(1/4) = 2$ bits per position.

For a codon (3 positions): $4^3 = 64$ possible codons → $\log_2 64 = 6$ bits per codon.

But only 20 amino acids + stop → $\log_2 21 = 4.39$ bits needed.

**Redundancy** of the genetic code:

$$
R = 1 - \frac{H_{\text{used}}}{H_{\text{max}}} = 1 - \frac{4.39}{6} = 0.268 = 26.8\%
$$

This redundancy (wobble base pairing) provides error tolerance — many point mutations at the 3rd codon position are synonymous (silent).

### 3.2 — Codon Adaptation Index (CAI)

Organisms preferentially use certain synonymous codons (codon bias). The CAI measures how well a gene's codon usage matches the organism's preferred codons:

$$
\text{CAI} = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{L} w_i\right)^{1/L}
$$

where $w_i$ = relative adaptiveness of codon $i$ (frequency of that codon / frequency of most common synonym), $L$ = gene length in codons.

CAI = 1.0 → all codons are the most preferred. High CAI correlates with high expression level.

### 3.3 — mRNA Half-Life and Steady-State Expression

mRNA concentration at steady state:

$$
\frac{d[\text{mRNA}]}{dt} = k_{\text{transcription}} - k_{\text{degradation}} \cdot [\text{mRNA}]
$$

At steady state ($d/dt = 0$):

$$
[\text{mRNA}]_{ss} = \frac{k_{\text{transcription}}}{k_{\text{degradation}}} = k_{\text{txn}} \cdot t_{1/2} / \ln 2
$$

Typical mRNA half-lives:
- c-Myc, c-Fos (proto-oncogenes): ~30 min (tight regulation)
- β-globin: ~17 hours (stable, high expression needed)
- Histone mRNAs: ~45 min (cell-cycle regulated)

### 3.4 — Protein Production Rate

Combining transcription and translation:

$$
\text{Protein production rate} = [\text{mRNA}] \times k_{\text{translation}} \times \text{ribosome loading}
$$

For a highly expressed gene (e.g., ribosomal proteins):
- ~50 mRNA copies per cell
- ~10 ribosomes per mRNA (polysome)
- ~6 aa/s per ribosome
- Protein of 300 aa → 50 s per protein
- Rate: 50 × 10 / 50 = 10 proteins/s = 36,000 proteins/hour

<details>
<summary>🔍 Worked Example 02.3.1 — Transcription and Translation of a Gene</summary>

**Problem:** A gene has the following template strand sequence (3'→5'):
`3'-TACAAGGCCTTTAATCGAACT-5'`

Determine: (a) mRNA sequence, (b) amino acid sequence, (c) identify any issues.

**Step 1:** mRNA is complementary to template, same as coding strand with U replacing T:

Template: `3'-TACAAGGCCTTTAATCGAACT-5'`
mRNA:     `5'-AUGUUCCGGAAAUUAGCUUGA-3'`

**Step 2:** Translate from AUG start codon in triplets:

```
AUG - UUC - CGG - AAA - UUA - GCU - UGA
Met - Phe - Arg - Lys - Leu - Ala - STOP
```

**Step 3:** The polypeptide is: Met-Phe-Arg-Lys-Leu-Ala (6 amino acids, then UGA stop codon terminates translation).

**Step 4:** Note that the initial Met may be cleaved by methionine aminopeptidase if the second amino acid is small (Phe is not small, so Met likely retained).

</details>

<details>
<summary>🔍 Worked Example 02.3.2 — Effect of Point Mutations</summary>

**Problem:** The wild-type codon is GAG (Glu). Classify each mutation:
(a) GAG → GAA
(b) GAG → GUG
(c) GAG → UAG

**Step 1:** Look up each codon:
- (a) GAA = Glu (same amino acid) → **Silent/synonymous mutation**
- (b) GUG = Val (different amino acid) → **Missense mutation** (this is the sickle-cell mutation! Glu6Val in β-globin)
- (c) UAG = Stop (amber) → **Nonsense mutation** (premature termination → truncated, likely nonfunctional protein)

**Step 2:** Functional consequences:
- (a) No effect on protein (wobble position degeneracy)
- (b) Val is hydrophobic vs Glu is charged → β-globin polymerizes under low O₂ → sickle-shaped RBCs
- (c) Truncated protein → likely degraded by nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) → loss of function

</details>

<details>
<summary>🔍 Worked Example 02.3.3 — Splicing: Calculating Protein Variants</summary>

**Problem:** A gene has 8 exons. Exons 3, 5, and 7 can each be independently included or excluded (cassette exons). How many possible mRNA variants exist?

**Step 1:** Exons 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 are constitutive (always included).

**Step 2:** Each of exons 3, 5, 7 has 2 states (included or excluded):

$$
\text{Variants} = 2^3 = 8
$$

**Step 3:** But if excluding all three simultaneously produces a non-functional protein (frameshift or missing essential domain), the biologically relevant variants = 7.

**Step 4:** In reality, alternative splicing is more complex — mutually exclusive exons, alternative 5'/3' splice sites, and intron retention add further diversity. The Drosophila DSCAM gene produces 38,016 variants from one gene through combinatorial alternative splicing.

</details>

<details>
<summary>🔍 Worked Example 02.3.4 — Information Theory: Coding Capacity</summary>

**Problem:** The human genome has 3.2 × 10⁹ bp. Only ~1.5% codes for protein. Calculate: (a) total information content, (b) protein-coding information, (c) number of average-sized proteins encodable.

**Step 1:** Total information:

$$
I_{\text{total}} = 3.2 \times 10^9 \times 2 \text{ bits/bp} = 6.4 \times 10^9 \text{ bits} = 800 \text{ MB}
$$

**Step 2:** Protein-coding:

$$
I_{\text{coding}} = 0.015 \times 6.4 \times 10^9 = 9.6 \times 10^7 \text{ bits} = 12 \text{ MB}
$$

**Step 3:** Average protein = 400 amino acids = 1200 nt of coding sequence.

$$
\text{Genes} = \frac{0.015 \times 3.2 \times 10^9}{1200} = \frac{4.8 \times 10^7}{1200} = 40{,}000
$$

**Step 4:** Actual protein-coding genes: ~20,000. The discrepancy is because:
- Many genes are larger than average (introns within coding regions counted in the 1.5%)
- Pseudogenes (non-functional copies)
- The 1.5% figure includes UTRs in some estimates

</details>

<details>
<summary>🔍 Worked Example 02.3.5 — Regulation: lac Operon Logic</summary>

**Problem:** For the E. coli lac operon, predict expression level under these conditions:
(a) No glucose, no lactose
(b) No glucose, lactose present
(c) Glucose present, lactose present
(d) Glucose present, no lactose

**Step 1:** The lac operon requires:
- **Inducer** (allolactose, from lactose): Removes lac repressor from operator
- **CAP-cAMP** (low glucose signal): Activates transcription via CAP binding site

**Step 2:** Truth table:

| Glucose | Lactose | Repressor | CAP-cAMP | Expression |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| No | No | ON (blocks) | High | OFF |
| No | Yes | OFF (induced) | High | **HIGH** |
| Yes | Yes | OFF (induced) | Low | Low (basal) |
| Yes | No | ON (blocks) | Low | OFF |

**Step 3:** This is AND logic: Expression = (Inducer present) AND (CAP-cAMP present)

The lac operon is a biological **priority encoder**: use glucose first (preferred carbon source); only express lactose genes when glucose is absent AND lactose is available.

</details>

## 🧠 5. Connections to AI / Computing

### 5.1 — Central Dogma → Sequence-to-Sequence Models

| Biology | AI/ML |
|:---|:---|
| DNA (stored information) | Training data / model weights |
| Transcription (DNA → mRNA) | Encoding (input → latent representation) |
| mRNA (intermediate) | Hidden state / embedding |
| Translation (mRNA → protein) | Decoding (latent → output) |
| Codon table (triplet → amino acid) | Lookup table / embedding matrix |
| Reading frame | Tokenization window |
| Alternative splicing | Multi-head attention (multiple interpretations) |

### 5.2 — Transcription Factors → Attention Mechanisms

Transcription factors are biological attention:
- **Enhancers** = attention weights (amplify signal from specific inputs)
- **Combinatorial TF binding** = multi-head attention (multiple TFs attend to different regulatory elements)
- **DNA looping** (enhancer contacts promoter over distance) = long-range attention (transformer self-attention over distant tokens)
- **Mediator complex** (bridges TFs to Pol II) = attention aggregation layer

### 5.3 — The Genetic Code → Error-Correcting Codes

The degeneracy of the genetic code is an **error-correcting code**:
- 3rd position wobble: Most mutations at position 3 are synonymous
- Similar amino acids share similar codons (e.g., all Ser codons start with UC or AG)
- This minimizes the phenotypic impact of point mutations — a form of **graceful degradation**

In coding theory terms: the genetic code has a Hamming distance structure that clusters similar amino acids near each other in codon space.

### 5.4 — Protein Folding → Optimization Landscapes

Protein folding (Levinthal's paradox):
- A 100-residue protein has ~$3^{100} \approx 5 \times 10^{47}$ possible conformations
- Random search would take longer than the age of the universe
- Yet proteins fold in milliseconds → **the energy landscape is funneled**

This is identical to the optimization landscape problem in ML:
- Loss landscape has exponentially many local minima
- Yet SGD finds good solutions quickly → **the loss landscape is structured**

**AlphaFold** (DeepMind, 2020) solved protein structure prediction by learning the relationship between sequence (MSA) and structure — essentially learning the folding funnel from evolutionary data.

### 5.5 — Epigenetics → Transfer Learning

Epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation, histone marks) are **meta-parameters** that control which genes are accessible:
- **Cell differentiation** = fine-tuning a pre-trained model for a specific task
- **Epigenetic memory** = retaining learned features across cell divisions
- **Reprogramming** (Yamanaka factors → iPSCs) = resetting to base model

### 6.1 — Gene Expression and Training Adaptation

Exercise triggers specific gene expression programs:

| Training Type | Activated TFs | Target Genes | Adaptation |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|

**Molecular mechanism of muscle memory:**
Resistance training causes myonuclear accretion (satellite cell fusion → more nuclei per fiber). These nuclei persist even during detraining — when you retrain, the extra nuclei rapidly resume protein synthesis. This is epigenetic muscle memory.

## 🔬 8. Extended Worked Examples & Deep Dives

### 8.1 — Transcription Factor Binding Kinetics: DNA-Protein Affinity

Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression by binding specific DNA sequences. The thermodynamics and kinetics of this interaction are fundamental to understanding gene regulation.

**Equilibrium Binding (Law of Mass Action):**

For a TF binding to a promoter site:

$$
\text{TF} + \text{DNA} \underset{k_{\text{off}}}{\overset{k_{\text{on}}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{TF:DNA}
$$

The dissociation constant:

$$
K_d = \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}} = \frac{[\text{TF}][\text{DNA}]}{[\text{TF:DNA}]}
$$

Fractional occupancy (probability the site is bound):

$$
\theta = \frac{[\text{TF}]}{[\text{TF}] + K_d}
$$

**Typical Values for Prokaryotic TFs:**

| TF | Target | $K_d$ (nM) | $k_{\text{on}}$ (M⁻¹s⁻¹) | $k_{\text{off}}$ (s⁻¹) | Residence time |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Lac repressor | *lac* operator | 0.01 | 10¹⁰ | 6×10⁻⁴ | ~28 min |
| Lac repressor | Non-specific DNA | 10⁴ | 10⁸ | 10³ | ~1 ms |
| CRP | *lac* promoter | 1–10 | 10⁸ | 0.1–1 | 1–10 s |
| σ70-RNAP | Consensus promoter | 1–100 | 10⁷ | 0.01–1 | 1–100 s |

**Specificity Ratio:**

$$
\text{Specificity} = \frac{K_d(\text{non-specific})}{K_d(\text{specific})} = \frac{10^4 \text{ nM}}{0.01 \text{ nM}} = 10^6
$$

The lac repressor discriminates its target from random DNA by a factor of one million — equivalent to ~14 $k_BT$ of binding energy difference.

**Energy of Specific Binding:**

$$
\Delta G_{\text{specific}} = -RT \ln \frac{1}{K_d} = -RT \ln(10^{11} \text{ M}^{-1})
$$

$$
= -8.314 \times 310 \times 25.3 = -65.2 \text{ kJ/mol} \approx -02.6 \text{ kcal/mol}
$$

This energy comes from ~20 hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts in the major groove.

**Facilitated Diffusion: How TFs Find Their Target**

The observed $k_{\text{on}} \approx 10^{10}$ M⁻¹s⁻¹ for lac repressor exceeds the 3D diffusion limit ($\sim 10^8$ M⁻¹s⁻¹). This is explained by **facilitated diffusion** — a combination of:

1. **3D diffusion** in the cytoplasm (hopping between DNA segments)
2. **1D sliding** along the DNA backbone (~50 bp per binding event)
3. **Intersegment transfer** (jumping between nearby DNA segments via looping)

The effective search time:

$$
t_{\text{search}} = \frac{1}{k_{\text{on}} \cdot [\text{TF}]} \approx \frac{1}{10^{10} \times 10^{-8}} = 10 \text{ s}
$$

With ~10 copies of lac repressor per cell and a 4.6 Mb genome, the repressor finds its single operator site in ~10 seconds — remarkably fast for searching 4.6 million base pairs.

### 8.2 — Ribosome Elongation: The Molecular Machine

The ribosome is a 2.5 MDa molecular machine that translates mRNA into protein at ~20 amino acids per second in bacteria (5–6 aa/s in eukaryotes).

**Elongation Cycle Kinetics:**

Each amino acid addition involves three steps:

| Step | Process | Rate | Duration |
|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|
| 1. Decoding | Codon-anticodon recognition + GTP hydrolysis (EF-Tu) | ~20 s⁻¹ | ~50 ms |
| 2. Peptide bond formation | Peptidyl transferase (23S rRNA catalysis) | ~200 s⁻¹ | ~5 ms |
| 3. Translocation | EF-G·GTP moves ribosome 3 nt along mRNA | ~30 s⁻¹ | ~33 ms |

Total cycle time: ~50 + 5 + 33 ≈ 88 ms → ~11 aa/s (actual measured: ~20 aa/s due to parallel processing)

**Error Rate and Proofreading:**

Initial codon-anticodon selection: error rate ~10⁻² (1 in 100)
After kinetic proofreading (Hopfield, 1974): error rate ~10⁻⁴ (1 in 10,000)

**Kinetic Proofreading Mechanism:**

The ribosome uses two sequential irreversible steps (GTP hydrolysis) to amplify discrimination:

$$
\text{Discrimination} = \left(\frac{k_{\text{correct}}}{k_{\text{incorrect}}}\right)^n
$$

where $n$ = number of proofreading steps. With 2 steps and initial discrimination of 100×:

$$
\text{Final accuracy} = 100^2 = 10{,}000\text{-fold}
$$

**Energy Cost of Translation:**

Per amino acid incorporated:
- 2 GTP for EF-Tu (decoding) + EF-G (translocation) = 2 GTP
- 2 ATP equivalents for aminoacyl-tRNA charging (amino acid activation)
- Total: **4 high-energy phosphate bonds per amino acid**

For a 300 aa protein: 1,200 ATP equivalents — translation is energetically expensive.

**Polysome Organization:**

Multiple ribosomes translate the same mRNA simultaneously:
- Spacing: ~80 nucleotides apart (one ribosome per ~27 codons)
- A 1000-codon mRNA can accommodate ~12 ribosomes
- This increases protein output per mRNA by 12×

### 8.3 — CRISPR-Cas9: Molecular Mechanism of Genome Editing

**The Natural CRISPR Immune System:**

CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a bacterial adaptive immune system:

1. **Adaptation**: Cas1/Cas2 capture ~30 bp fragments from invading phage DNA and insert them as "spacers" into the CRISPR array
2. **Expression**: The CRISPR array is transcribed into pre-crRNA, processed into individual crRNAs
3. **Interference**: Cas9 (or other Cas proteins) uses crRNA as a guide to find and cleave matching foreign DNA

**Cas9 Cleavage Mechanism (Step by Step):**

1. **Guide RNA loading**: Cas9 binds the single-guide RNA (sgRNA = crRNA + tracrRNA fusion)
2. **PAM recognition**: Cas9 scans DNA for PAM sequences (5'-NGG-3' for *S. pyogenes* Cas9)
3. **R-loop formation**: If PAM is found, Cas9 unwinds DNA and the guide RNA hybridizes with the target strand (20 bp)
4. **Conformational change**: Complete R-loop triggers HNH and RuvC nuclease domain activation
5. **Double-strand break**: HNH cuts the target strand (complementary to guide); RuvC cuts the non-target strand
6. **Result**: Blunt-ended DSB, 3 bp upstream of PAM

**Specificity and Off-Target Effects:**

Cas9 tolerates mismatches, especially in the PAM-distal region (positions 1–8 of the guide):

| Mismatch position | Effect on cleavage |
|:---|:---|
| Positions 1–8 (PAM-distal) | Often tolerated (off-target risk) |
| Positions 9–12 (mid-seed) | Moderate reduction |
| Positions 13–20 (PAM-proximal, "seed") | Severe reduction or abolition |

**Kinetic Parameters:**

$$
k_{\text{on}}(\text{target}) \approx 10^6 \text{ M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}
$$

$$
k_{\text{cleavage}} \approx 0.3\text{–}1 \text{ s}^{-1} \text{ (after R-loop completion)}
$$

$$
t_{\text{search}} \approx 6 \text{ hours to scan entire human genome (in vitro)}
$$

**DNA Repair Outcomes:**

After Cas9 creates a DSB, the cell repairs it via:
- **NHEJ** (Non-Homologous End Joining): Error-prone, creates insertions/deletions (indels) → gene knockout
- **HDR** (Homology-Directed Repair): Template-guided, precise editing → gene correction (requires donor template, only in S/G2 phase)

Efficiency: NHEJ ~50–90%; HDR ~5–50% (cell-type dependent)

### 8.4 — Gene Regulation: The Lac Operon as a Genetic Switch

**Boolean Logic of the Lac Operon:**

The lac operon integrates two signals (lactose presence, glucose absence) using AND logic:

$$
\text{Expression} = \text{NOT}(\text{Repressor bound}) \;\text{AND}\; \text{CAP-cAMP bound}
$$

| Glucose | Lactose | cAMP | Repressor | CAP active | Expression |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| + | − | Low | Bound | No | OFF |
| + | + | Low | Released | No | LOW (basal) |
| − | − | High | Bound | Yes | OFF |
| − | + | High | Released | Yes | **HIGH** |

**Thermodynamic Model of Promoter Occupancy:**

The probability of RNA polymerase binding the promoter depends on the statistical weights of all possible promoter states:

$$
P(\text{RNAP bound}) = \frac{Z_{\text{RNAP}}}{Z_{\text{total}}}
$$

$$
Z_{\text{total}} = 1 + \frac{[\text{RNAP}]}{K_d^{\text{RNAP}}} + \frac{[\text{Rep}]}{K_d^{\text{Rep}}} + \frac{[\text{RNAP}][\text{CAP}]}{K_d^{\text{RNAP}} K_d^{\text{CAP}} / \omega}
$$

where $\omega > 1$ is the cooperativity factor between CAP and RNAP.

```python
import numpy as np

def lac_operon_expression(lactose, glucose, 
                           K_rep=0.01,    # nM, repressor Kd for operator
                           K_cap=10,      # nM, CAP Kd for promoter
                           K_rnap=100,    # nM, RNAP Kd for promoter
                           omega=50,      # CAP-RNAP cooperativity
                           rep_total=10,  # nM total repressor
                           cap_total=100):# nM total CAP
    """Thermodynamic model of lac operon regulation."""
    # Allolactose (from lactose) inactivates repressor
    if lactose > 0:
        free_rep = rep_total * K_rep / (K_rep + lactose)
    else:
        free_rep = rep_total
    
    # Low glucose -> high cAMP -> active CAP
    cAMP = 100 / (1 + glucose/0.1)  # cAMP inversely proportional to glucose
    active_cap = cap_total * cAMP / (cAMP + 50)
    
    # Statistical thermodynamic partition function
    Z_empty = 1
    Z_rep = free_rep / K_rep          # Repressor bound (blocks RNAP)
    Z_rnap = 1 / K_rnap              # RNAP alone
    Z_rnap_cap = omega * active_cap / (K_rnap * K_cap)  # RNAP + CAP cooperative
    
    Z_total = Z_empty + Z_rep + Z_rnap + Z_rnap_cap
    P_expression = (Z_rnap + Z_rnap_cap) / Z_total
    
    return P_expression

# Test all four conditions
print("Glucose+, Lactose-:", lac_operon_expression(lactose=0, glucose=10))
print("Glucose+, Lactose+:", lac_operon_expression(lactose=5, glucose=10))
print("Glucose-, Lactose-:", lac_operon_expression(lactose=0, glucose=0))
print("Glucose-, Lactose+:", lac_operon_expression(lactose=5, glucose=0))
```

### 8.5 — Protein Folding: Levinthal's Paradox and the Energy Landscape

**Levinthal's Paradox (1969):**

A 100-residue protein with 3 rotatable bonds per residue has $3^{200} \approx 10^{95}$ possible conformations. If the protein sampled one conformation per picosecond:

$$
t_{\text{exhaustive}} = \frac{10^{95}}{10^{12} \text{ s}^{-1}} = 10^{83} \text{ s} \gg \text{age of universe} (4.3 \times 10^{17} \text{ s})
$$

Yet proteins fold in milliseconds to seconds. Resolution: proteins don't search randomly — they follow a **funnel-shaped energy landscape**.

**The Folding Funnel:**

The free energy landscape has a funnel topology:

$$
F(Q) = E(Q) - TS(Q)
$$

where $Q$ = fraction of native contacts formed (reaction coordinate).

- **Entropy** decreases as folding progresses: $S(Q) \propto (1-Q) \ln \Omega$
- **Energy** decreases as native contacts form: $E(Q) \propto -\varepsilon \cdot Q \cdot N_{\text{contacts}}$
- The funnel shape means most paths lead downhill toward the native state

**Folding Rate Prediction (Contact Order):**

Plaxco et al. (1998) showed that folding rate correlates with **relative contact order** (RCO):

$$
\text{RCO} = \frac{1}{L \cdot N} \sum_{\text{contacts}} |i - j|
$$

where $L$ = chain length, $N$ = number of native contacts, $|i-j|$ = sequence separation.

$$
\ln k_f = -17.0 \times \text{RCO} + 14.4 \quad (r^2 = 0.77)
$$

Proteins with mostly local contacts (helical) fold faster than those with many long-range contacts (β-sheets).

### 9.1 — AlphaFold-2 Architecture: From Sequence to Structure

**Cross-link [[10.3 - Vision Transformers & ViT]]**

AlphaFold-2 (Jumper et al., 2021) solved the 50-year protein structure prediction problem, achieving experimental accuracy (median GDT-TS > 92). Its architecture is a masterclass in combining biological priors with deep learning.

**Input Representations:**

1. **Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA)**: Evolutionary information from homologous sequences
   - Shape: $(N_{\text{seq}} \times L_{\text{residues}} \times d_{\text{MSA}})$
   - Encodes co-evolutionary constraints (correlated mutations reveal contacts)

2. **Pair representation**: Pairwise relationships between all residue pairs
   - Shape: $(L \times L \times d_{\text{pair}})$
   - Initialized from relative positional encoding + template features

**Core Architecture — Evoformer (48 blocks):**

Each Evoformer block alternates:

1. **MSA row-wise attention** (within each sequence):
   - Standard multi-head self-attention along the sequence dimension
   - Captures intra-sequence dependencies

2. **MSA column-wise attention** (across sequences at each position):
   - Attention across the MSA depth at each residue position
   - Captures co-evolutionary patterns

3. **Pair representation update**:
   - Triangular multiplicative update (mimics distance geometry constraints)
   - If residue $i$ is close to $j$, and $j$ is close to $k$, then $i$ and $k$ are constrained (triangle inequality)

$$
z_{ij} \leftarrow z_{ij} + \text{Linear}\left(\sum_k \sigma(z_{ik}) \odot \sigma(z_{jk})\right)
$$

4. **Outer product mean** (MSA → pair):
   - Projects MSA information into the pair representation
   - $z_{ij} \leftarrow z_{ij} + \frac{1}{N_{\text{seq}}} \sum_s m_{si}^T m_{sj}$

**Structure Module (8 blocks):**

Converts the pair representation into 3D coordinates using:
- **Invariant Point Attention (IPA)**: Attention mechanism that respects SE(3) symmetry
- Each residue is represented as a rigid body frame (rotation + translation)
- Iteratively refines backbone coordinates

$$
T_i = (R_i, \mathbf{t}_i) \in SE(3) \quad \text{(rotation matrix + translation vector per residue)}
$$

**Loss Function:**

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{FAPE}} + \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{distogram}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{pLDDT}} + \lambda_3 \mathcal{L}_{\text{masked\_MSA}}
$$

- **FAPE** (Frame Aligned Point Error): Measures structural accuracy in local reference frames
- **Distogram**: Cross-entropy on predicted distance distributions
- **pLDDT**: Per-residue confidence score (predicted LDDT)

**Key Biological Insight:** AlphaFold-2 works because evolution has already "solved" the protein folding problem billions of times. The MSA contains the solution — co-evolved residues reveal 3D contacts. AlphaFold-2 is essentially a very sophisticated method for extracting this evolutionary information.

### 9.2 — RNA Velocity: Inferring Cell Trajectories from Single-Cell Data

**Cross-link [[10.1 - Neural Networks & Deep Learning]]**

RNA velocity (La Manno et al., 2018) infers the future state of individual cells by exploiting the kinetics of mRNA splicing.

**The Splicing Kinetics Model:**

For each gene $g$ in each cell $c$:

$$
\frac{du_g}{dt} = \alpha_g - \beta_g u_g \quad \text{(unspliced/pre-mRNA)}
$$

$$
\frac{ds_g}{dt} = \beta_g u_g - \gamma_g s_g \quad \text{(spliced/mature mRNA)}
$$

where:
- $u_g$ = unspliced mRNA count (introns still present)
- $s_g$ = spliced mRNA count (mature, translatable)
- $\alpha_g$ = transcription rate
- $\beta_g$ = splicing rate
- $\gamma_g$ = degradation rate

**Steady-State Relationship:**

At steady state ($du/dt = 0$, $ds/dt = 0$):

$$
u_g^{ss} = \frac{\alpha_g}{\beta_g}, \quad s_g^{ss} = \frac{\beta_g u_g^{ss}}{\gamma_g} = \frac{\alpha_g}{\gamma_g}
$$

Therefore: $u_g = \frac{\gamma_g}{\beta_g} s_g$ (linear relationship at steady state)

**RNA Velocity Calculation:**

The velocity of spliced mRNA is:

$$
v_g = \frac{ds_g}{dt} = \beta_g u_g - \gamma_g s_g
$$

- If $v_g > 0$: Gene is being upregulated (cell is moving toward higher expression)
- If $v_g < 0$: Gene is being downregulated (cell is moving toward lower expression)

**Estimating $\gamma_g$:**

From the steady-state assumption, fit a linear regression of $u$ vs $s$ across all cells:

$$
u_g = \gamma_g / \beta_g \cdot s_g + \text{offset}
$$

The slope gives $\gamma_g/\beta_g$. Cells above the line have $v_g > 0$ (upregulating); cells below have $v_g < 0$ (downregulating).

**Projecting Velocity into Embedding Space:**

The gene-level velocities are projected into a low-dimensional embedding (UMAP/t-SNE) to visualize cell trajectories:

$$
\mathbf{v}_c = \sum_g v_{g,c} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{e}}_g
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_g$ is the direction in embedding space corresponding to gene $g$.

```python
import numpy as np

def compute_rna_velocity(unspliced, spliced):
    """
    Compute RNA velocity for each gene across cells.
    
    unspliced: (n_cells, n_genes) matrix of unspliced counts
    spliced: (n_cells, n_genes) matrix of spliced counts
    """
    n_cells, n_genes = spliced.shape
    velocity = np.zeros_like(spliced, dtype=float)
    
    for g in range(n_genes):
        u = unspliced[:, g].astype(float)
        s = spliced[:, g].astype(float)
        
        # Fit steady-state ratio (gamma/beta) via linear regression
        # u = (gamma/beta) * s at steady state
        if np.std(s) > 0:
            gamma_over_beta = np.dot(u, s) / np.dot(s, s)  # OLS without intercept
        else:
            gamma_over_beta = 0
        
        # Velocity = beta * u - gamma * s = beta * (u - gamma/beta * s)
        # We report in units of beta (which cancels in relative comparisons)
        velocity[:, g] = u - gamma_over_beta * s
    
    return velocity

# Example usage:
# velocity = compute_rna_velocity(adata.layers['unspliced'], adata.layers['spliced'])
# Positive velocity = gene being upregulated in that cell
```

**scVelo (Bergen et al., 2020) — Dynamical Model:**

The improved dynamical model fits the full ODE system per gene, allowing:
- Time-varying transcription rates (gene induction/repression)
- Latent time estimation (ordering cells along differentiation trajectories)
- Identification of driver genes for each transition

> **AI Bridge:** RNA velocity is conceptually similar to **optical flow** in computer vision — estimating the "motion field" of cells in gene expression space. Just as optical flow predicts where pixels will move in the next video frame, RNA velocity predicts where cells will move in transcriptomic space.

### 9.3 — Information Theory of the Genetic Code

**Shannon Entropy of Codons:**

The genetic code maps 64 codons to 20 amino acids + 1 stop signal. The information content:

$$
H(\text{codon}) = \log_2 64 = 6 \text{ bits}
$$

$$
H(\text{amino acid}) = -\sum_{i=1}^{20} p_i \log_2 p_i \approx 4.18 \text{ bits (uniform)} 
$$

**Redundancy of the genetic code:**

$$
\text{Redundancy} = 1 - \frac{H(\text{amino acid})}{H(\text{codon})} = 1 - \frac{4.18}{6} = 0.30
$$

This 30% redundancy provides error tolerance — most single-nucleotide mutations at the 3rd codon position (wobble) are synonymous.

**Error Minimization:**

The standard genetic code is optimized to minimize the impact of mutations and translation errors. Quantitatively, the code minimizes:

$$
\text{MS}_0 = \sum_{\text{all point mutations}} \Delta(\text{amino acid property change})
$$

The natural code has lower $\text{MS}_0$ than >99.99% of random alternative codes (Freeland & Hurst, 1998) — strong evidence for evolutionary optimization of the code itself.

### 9.4 — Sequence Alignment as Dynamic Programming

**The Needleman-Wunsch Algorithm (Global Alignment):**

Sequence alignment is the foundational algorithm of bioinformatics — and it's a classic dynamic programming problem identical in structure to edit distance.

**Scoring:**
- Match: $+1$ (or substitution matrix score)
- Mismatch: $-1$
- Gap: $-2$ (linear gap penalty)

**Recurrence:**

$$
F(i,j) = \max \begin{cases}
F(i-1, j-1) + s(x_i, y_j) & \text{(match/mismatch)} \\
F(i-1, j) + d & \text{(gap in sequence Y)} \\
F(i, j-1) + d & \text{(gap in sequence X)}
\end{cases}
$$

**Time complexity:** $O(nm)$ where $n, m$ are sequence lengths.

```python
import numpy as np

def needleman_wunsch(seq1, seq2, match=1, mismatch=-1, gap=-2):
    """Global sequence alignment via dynamic programming."""
    n, m = len(seq1), len(seq2)
    # Initialize score matrix
    F = np.zeros((n+1, m+1))
    F[0, :] = np.arange(m+1) * gap
    F[:, 0] = np.arange(n+1) * gap
    
    # Fill matrix
    for i in range(1, n+1):
        for j in range(1, m+1):
            score = match if seq1[i-1] == seq2[j-1] else mismatch
            F[i,j] = max(
                F[i-1, j-1] + score,  # diagonal (match/mismatch)
                F[i-1, j] + gap,       # up (gap in seq2)
                F[i, j-1] + gap        # left (gap in seq1)
            )
    
    # Traceback
    align1, align2 = '', ''
    i, j = n, m
    while i > 0 or j > 0:
        if i > 0 and j > 0:
            score = match if seq1[i-1] == seq2[j-1] else mismatch
            if F[i,j] == F[i-1,j-1] + score:
                align1 = seq1[i-1] + align1
                align2 = seq2[j-1] + align2
                i -= 1; j -= 1; continue
        if i > 0 and F[i,j] == F[i-1,j] + gap:
            align1 = seq1[i-1] + align1
            align2 = '-' + align2
            i -= 1
        else:
            align1 = '-' + align1
            align2 = seq2[j-1] + align2
            j -= 1
    
    return align1, align2, F[n,m]

# Example: align two short protein sequences
a1, a2, score = needleman_wunsch("HEAGAWGHEE", "PAWHEAE")
print(f"Seq1: {a1}")
print(f"Seq2: {a2}")
print(f"Score: {score}")
```

> **AI Bridge — Cross-link [[10.1 - Neural Networks]]:** Modern protein language models (ESM-2, ProtTrans) learn sequence representations that implicitly capture alignment information. The attention patterns in protein transformers correlate with structural contacts — the model learns "alignment" and "structure" simultaneously from raw sequences, without explicit dynamic programming.

### 9.5 — Epigenetics as Cellular Memory: Bistable Switches

**Gene Regulatory Networks as Dynamical Systems:**

A simple mutual-repression circuit (e.g., λ phage CI/Cro switch) creates bistability:

$$
\frac{d[A]}{dt} = \frac{\alpha_A}{1 + ([B]/K_B)^n} - \gamma_A [A]
$$

$$
\frac{d[B]}{dt} = \frac{\alpha_B}{1 + ([A]/K_A)^n} - \gamma_B [B]
$$

With Hill coefficient $n \geq 2$, this system has **two stable steady states** (A-high/B-low OR A-low/B-high) separated by an unstable saddle point.

**Epigenetic Landscape Quantification:**

The quasi-potential $U(\mathbf{x})$ of the gene regulatory network:

$$
U(\mathbf{x}) = -\int_{\text{path}} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) \cdot d\mathbf{x}
$$

where $\mathbf{F}$ is the deterministic flow field. Stable states are minima of $U$; transition rates between states follow Kramers' escape rate:

$$
k_{\text{transition}} \propto e^{-\Delta U / D}
$$

where $D$ is the noise intensity (from stochastic gene expression).

**Biological Significance:** This explains how genetically identical cells (same DNA) can maintain different stable phenotypes (e.g., lysogeny vs. lysis in phage λ, or different cell types in multicellular organisms). Epigenetic memory is a dynamical systems phenomenon — not just DNA methylation.

> **AI Bridge:** Bistable gene circuits are analogous to **Hopfield networks** and **energy-based models** in ML. Both store information as attractors in a dynamical system. Cell reprogramming (Yamanaka factors converting fibroblasts to iPSCs) is equivalent to pushing the system over an energy barrier between attractors — analogous to simulated annealing escaping local minima.
