## 📝 3. Chapter Outline

| # | Chapter | Core skill |
|---|---------|-----------|
| 59.1 | **Clarity — The Foundation** | Orwell's six rules (the most important being: if a word can be cut, cut it); the fog index (Gunning) — measuring sentence complexity; active vs. passive voice (not a rule against passive, but a preference for active when the agent matters); concrete vs. abstract language; specific vs. vague; the curse of knowledge (you know too much about your subject — your reader doesn't; writing for the reader, not for yourself); Zinsser's principle: every word must work; read your work aloud — the ear catches what the eye misses. |
| 59.2 | **Sentence Mechanics & Rhythm** | Sentence length variation (monotony kills reading; vary between short punches and longer flowing sentences); the periodic sentence (main clause at the end — builds suspense); the cumulative sentence (main clause first, then qualification — the most common in good nonfiction); parallel structure (lists and comparisons must be grammatically parallel); subordination (deciding which idea gets the main clause tells the reader what matters); the em dash — and when to use it; semicolons; colons. The rhythm of prose is a real thing: read Churchill, read Baldwin, read Orwell and hear it. |
| 59.3 | **Argument Structure** | The difference between an argument and an assertion (an assertion makes a claim; an argument provides reasons + evidence + addresses counterarguments); Toulmin model (claim → grounds → warrant → backing → qualifier → rebuttal); the three modes of persuasion: logos (logical argument), ethos (the writer's credibility/authority), pathos (emotional resonance — not manipulation, but connection); the essay as a form (thesis that isn't obvious, body that surprises the reader, conclusion that earns its weight); how the best technical writing argues: state the claim, demonstrate, address the objection, restate stronger. |
| 59.4 | **Voice & Style Development** | Voice = the sense that a specific human being wrote this (not a committee, not a bot); the elements of voice: word choice, sentence rhythm, what you notice and include, what you leave out, your relationship to the reader; how to develop voice: read widely, write daily, steal intentionally (steal the *structure* and *rhythm* of writers you admire — never the words); Hemingway's iceberg principle (seven-eighths underwater — say less than you know, imply more); the persona trap (the fake-formal academic voice that makes all writing sound the same); what AI can't fake: your specific perspective, your specific examples, your specific weirdness. |
| 59.5 | **Technical & Documentation Writing** | Technical writing is a genre with its own demands: precision over beauty; the reader is trying to accomplish a task; every ambiguity is a bug; documentation structures (tutorials vs. how-to guides vs. explanations vs. reference — these are fundamentally different and should not be mixed); the docs-as-code movement; README files that people actually read; API documentation; error messages as UX; the principle of progressive disclosure (start simple, go deep as the reader needs); changelog writing; the Google Developer Documentation Style Guide (free online). |
| 59.6 | **Copywriting & Product Writing** | Copywriting is writing that causes action. Different from essay or documentation writing. Key principles: the headline is 80% of the work (most people read only the headline); the AIDA framework (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action); the before/after/bridge structure; social proof and specificity ("37 developers use this" > "used by developers"); features vs. benefits (people buy the outcome, not the capability); the landing page as a single argument; email subject lines; UX microcopy (button labels, error states, empty states, onboarding copy — each one is a writing decision). |
| 59.7 | **Editing — The Real Work** | First drafts are for getting ideas out; editing is where writing actually happens. The editing levels: structural (does the argument hold? is the order right? is anything missing?), paragraph (transitions, topic sentences, unity), sentence (clarity, rhythm, word choice), word (precision, concision). The cold read (wait 24hrs, read as if someone else wrote it); cutting ruthlessly (the first 10% of almost any piece can be cut without loss); kill your darlings (the sentences you love most are often the ones most in love with themselves, not the reader); peer review; reading aloud as final check. |
| 59.8 | **Writing for the Digital Age** | How people read online: they scan, not read linearly (F-pattern and Z-pattern eye tracking); writing for scanners (headers, bold key phrases, short paragraphs, bullet points for lists); SEO writing vs. literary writing (they're different skills; knowing which one you're doing); social media writing (Twitter/X as a compression exercise — every character costs); newsletter writing (the most intimate digital writing form); the thread as a writing form; AI-assisted editing (using LLMs to find clarity problems, not to write for you — because your voice is the asset). |
