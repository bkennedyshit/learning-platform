# 35.1 — Music Theory for Producers

> *"You don't need to know theory to make music. But theory is a map — without it, you're exploring in the dark."*

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

1. Construct any major or minor scale and identify its intervals.
2. Build chords (major, minor, dim, aug, 7th) from scale degrees.
3. Recognise and use the primary **diatonic chord progressions** (I–V–vi–IV, ii–V–I, I–IV–V).
4. Explain what a **mode** is and identify the character of each of the 7 modes.
5. Read and write MIDI note numbers (C4=60, A4=69) and convert to/from frequency (A4=440Hz).
6. Apply rhythm concepts: time signature, beat subdivision (8th, 16th notes), swing/groove.

## 📚 1. Intervals & The Chromatic Scale

The Western musical system divides an octave into **12 equal semitones** (equal temperament). An octave is the interval where frequency doubles (A4=440Hz → A5=880Hz).

**Interval names:**

| Semitones | Interval name | Example (from C) |
|----------|--------------|----------------|
| 0 | Unison | C |
| 1 | Minor 2nd | C→C# |
| 2 | Major 2nd | C→D |
| 3 | Minor 3rd | C→D# |
| 4 | Major 3rd | C→E |
| 5 | Perfect 4th | C→F |
| 6 | Tritone | C→F# |
| 7 | Perfect 5th | C→G |
| 8 | Minor 6th | C→G# |
| 9 | Major 6th | C→A |
| 10 | Minor 7th | C→A# |
| 11 | Major 7th | C→B |
| 12 | Octave | C→C |

---

## 📚 2. Major Scale Formula

The major scale uses the pattern: **W-W-H-W-W-W-H** (W = whole step, 2 semitones; H = half step, 1 semitone).

**C Major:** C – D – E – F – G – A – B – C

Scale degrees (numbers): 1–2–3–4–5–6–7–(8)

**Any major key:** Apply the same W-W-H-W-W-W-H pattern from any starting note.

**Natural minor scale formula:** W-H-W-W-H-W-W (relative minor starts on degree 6 of major)

## 📚 3. Chord Construction

Chords are built by stacking thirds (every other scale note).

**Triad (3 notes):**
- **Major triad:** root + M3 + P5 (1–3–5)
- **Minor triad:** root + m3 + P5 (1–b3–5)
- **Diminished:** root + m3 + d5 (1–b3–b5)
- **Augmented:** root + M3 + A5 (1–3–#5)

**7th chords (4 notes):**
- **Major 7th (maj7):** 1–3–5–7
- **Dominant 7th (dom7):** 1–3–5–b7
- **Minor 7th (m7):** 1–b3–5–b7
- **Half-diminished (m7b5):** 1–b3–b5–b7

### 3.1 Diatonic Chords in Major Key

Every note of the major scale can be a chord root, using only notes from the scale:

| Degree | Roman | C major | Quality |
|--------|-------|---------|---------|
| 1 | I | C maj | Major (tonic) |
| 2 | ii | D min | Minor (subdominant function) |
| 3 | iii | E min | Minor (tonic-like) |
| 4 | IV | F maj | Major (subdominant) |
| 5 | V | G maj | Major (dominant — tension) |
| 6 | vi | A min | Minor (relative minor tonic) |
| 7 | vii° | B dim | Diminished (leading tone) |

## 📚 4. Essential Chord Progressions

### 4.1 The Axis Progression (Pop)
**I–V–vi–IV** — one of the most common in popular music:
C – G – Am – F in C major

Variations: start from vi (Am–F–C–G, same chords), or V (G–Am–F–C).

### 4.2 ii–V–I (Jazz)
Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 in C major.

The dominant 7th chord (G7) creates maximum harmonic tension; resolving to I is the strongest possible resolution. Used in virtually all jazz vocabulary.

### 4.3 12-Bar Blues
```
I  |  I  |  I  |  I
IV |  IV |  I  |  I
V  |  IV |  I  |  V
```
In A: A – A – A – A / D – D – A – A / E – D – A – E

## 📚 5. The 7 Modes

Modes are scales generated by starting on different degrees of the major scale. All 7 modes use the same notes as their parent major scale but start from a different root.

Using C major (all white keys) as parent:

| Mode | Start note | Formula relative to major | Character | Common use |
|------|-----------|--------------------------|----------|-----------|
| **Ionian** | C (1) | 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 | Bright, happy | Pop, folk |
| **Dorian** | D (2) | 1 2 b3 4 5 6 b7 | Minor + raised 6th | Jazz, funk, modal rock |
| **Phrygian** | E (3) | 1 b2 b3 4 5 b6 b7 | Dark, Spanish/flamenco | Metal, EDM intros |
| **Lydian** | F (4) | 1 2 3 #4 5 6 7 | Dreamy, floating | Film scores, vaporwave |
| **Mixolydian** | G (5) | 1 2 3 4 5 6 b7 | Bluesy, rock | Blues, funk, rock |
| **Aeolian** | A (6) | 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7 | Natural minor, melancholic | Pop ballads, classic minor |
| **Locrian** | B (7) | 1 b2 b3 4 b5 b6 b7 | Dissonant, unstable | Rarely used; tension/horror |

**Producer shortcut:**
- Want dark but not depressing? → **Dorian** (minor with a brighter 6th)
- Want dreamy or otherworldly? → **Lydian** (#4 creates that hovering quality)
- Want bluesy rock feel? → **Mixolydian** (major with a b7)

## 📚 6. Rhythm & Time

### 6.1 Time Signatures
**4/4** — 4 quarter-note beats per bar. Default for most pop, hip-hop, EDM.
**3/4** — 3 quarter-note beats (waltz). Ballads.
**6/8** — 6 eighth-note beats (compound duple). Compound feel, folk.
**7/8, 5/4** — Odd time. Prog rock, Balkan, Radiohead.

### 6.2 Note Values
```
Whole note     = 4 beats    (1 per bar in 4/4)
Half note      = 2 beats    
Quarter note   = 1 beat     (the "click")
Eighth note    = 0.5 beats  (2 per click)
Sixteenth note = 0.25 beats (4 per click — hi-hat subdivisions)
Triplet        = 3 in the time of 2 (1.5 clicks ÷ 3 = 0.5 clicks each)
```

### 6.3 Swing / Groove

**Straight 16ths:** Every subdivision is equal (50% position).
**Swing:** Second 16th of each pair is pushed later (55–75%). Creates a lopsided shuffle feel.

In DAWs: set **Swing %** on the sequencer/groove quantise setting. 50% = straight, 75% = heavy swing.

### 6.4 MIDI Note Numbers

```
C4 (Middle C) = MIDI note 60
A4 (440 Hz)   = MIDI note 69
Frequency = 440 × 2^((note - 69) / 12)
```

**Velocity:** 0–127. 0 = note off. 64 = medium. 127 = maximum. Vary velocity to humanise patterns — identical velocities sound mechanical.

## 🛠️ 7. Worked Example — Write a Progression for a Lo-Fi Beat

**Goal:** Relaxed, melancholic lo-fi hip-hop progression in 4/4, 90 BPM.

1. **Key:** F minor (relative: Ab major)
2. **Scale:** F–G–Ab–Bb–C–Db–Eb (Aeolian mode on F)
3. **Progression:** vi–IV–I–V in Ab major = Fm–Db–Ab–Eb
   - Romanised in F minor: i–VI–III–VII
4. **Voicings:** Add 7ths for jazz-adjacent feel:
   - Fm7 (F–Ab–C–Eb)
   - Dbmaj7 (Db–F–Ab–C)
   - Abmaj7 (Ab–C–Eb–G)
   - Eb7 (Eb–G–Bb–Db)
5. **Rhythm:** 4 chords, each 2 bars; dotted 8th note arpeggio pattern
6. **Bass:** Root notes on beats 1 and 3, passing notes on 4-and
7. **Groove:** 55% swing on 16th notes, velocity range 60–100

## ⚠️ 8. Common Misconceptions

1. **"You need to know theory before making music."** False. Many great producers work by ear first. Theory is a translation layer — it helps you understand and communicate what your ear already tells you.

2. **"Minor keys are always sad."** Dorian minor is used in funk and jazz and doesn't feel sad. Mixolydian (technically major) is used in blues and rock and can feel ambiguous. Mood depends on tempo, timbre, rhythm, and context more than major/minor.

3. **"The circle of 5ths is what you use to write progressions."** The circle tells you key relationships (modulation, chord borrowing). Diatonic chord progressions come from the scale. Both useful, different purposes.

4. **"Melody must stay within the scale."** Chromatic notes (outside the key) are used constantly — as passing tones, chromatic approaches, blue notes. They create tension that resolves back to scale tones.

5. **"Swing is just making 8ths unequal."** Swing applies to multiple levels of the beat structure and interacts with velocity, timing micro-deviations, and articulation. A drum machine with swing % set correctly still sounds robotic without velocity variation and slight timing humanisation.

6. **"MIDI velocity is the same as audio volume."** Velocity is a MIDI value (0–127) that maps to volume AND timbre for most instruments. A piano at velocity 90 doesn't just sound louder than velocity 40 — it sounds brighter and more aggressive.
