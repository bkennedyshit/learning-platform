# 28.2 — Quaternions & Rotations

> *"Time is said to have only one dimension, and space to have three dimensions. … The mathematical quaternion partakes of both these elements; in technical language it may be said to be 'time plus space', or 'space plus time': and in this sense it has, or at least involves a reference to, four dimensions."* — William Rowan Hamilton (1843)

Euler angles suffer from gimbal lock. Rotation matrices use 9 numbers where 3 suffice and accumulate floating-point drift. Quaternions solve both problems: they represent any 3D rotation with exactly 4 numbers, compose via multiplication, interpolate smoothly via SLERP, and remain numerically stable through simple renormalization. This chapter derives quaternion algebra from first principles, proves the rotation formula $\mathbf{v}' = q\mathbf{v}q^{-1}$, and connects quaternions to axis-angle, Euler angles, and rotation matrices.

---

## 📚 1. Definitions

### Definition 28.2.1 — The Quaternion Algebra $\mathbb{H}$

A **quaternion** $q \in \mathbb{H}$ is a hypercomplex number of the form:

$$
q = w + xi + yj + zk
$$

where $w, x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i, j, k$ are imaginary units satisfying Hamilton's fundamental relations:

$$
i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1
$$

From these, all multiplication rules follow:

$$
ij = k, \quad jk = i, \quad ki = j
$$

$$
ji = -k, \quad kj = -i, \quad ik = -j
$$

Quaternion multiplication is **associative** but **not commutative**.

We write $q = (w, \mathbf{v})$ where $w$ is the **scalar part** and $\mathbf{v} = (x, y, z)$ is the **vector part**.

### Definition 28.2.2 — Quaternion Multiplication (Full Expansion)

Given $q_1 = (w_1, \mathbf{v}_1) = w_1 + x_1 i + y_1 j + z_1 k$ and $q_2 = (w_2, \mathbf{v}_2) = w_2 + x_2 i + y_2 j + z_2 k$:

$$
q_1 q_2 = (w_1 w_2 - \mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2,\; w_1 \mathbf{v}_2 + w_2 \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2)
$$

Expanding component-by-component:

$$
q_1 q_2 = (w_1 w_2 - x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2 - z_1 z_2)
$$

$$
+ (w_1 x_2 + x_1 w_2 + y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2)\,i
$$

$$
+ (w_1 y_2 - x_1 z_2 + y_1 w_2 + z_1 x_2)\,j
$$

$$
+ (w_1 z_2 + x_1 y_2 - y_1 x_2 + z_1 w_2)\,k
$$

### Definition 28.2.3 — Quaternion Conjugate, Norm, and Inverse

The **conjugate** of $q = w + xi + yj + zk$:

$$
\bar{q} = q^* = w - xi - yj - zk
$$

The **norm** (magnitude):

$$
\|q\| = \sqrt{q\bar{q}} = \sqrt{w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2}
$$

The **inverse** (for $q \neq 0$):

$$
q^{-1} = \frac{\bar{q}}{\|q\|^2}
$$

For **unit quaternions** ($\|q\| = 1$): $q^{-1} = \bar{q}$.

### Definition 28.2.4 — Unit Quaternion (Versor)

A quaternion with $\|q\| = 1$ is called a **unit quaternion** or **versor**. The set of unit quaternions forms the 3-sphere $S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4$.

Every unit quaternion can be written in **axis-angle form**:

$$
q = \cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}(u_x i + u_y j + u_z k)
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = (u_x, u_y, u_z)$ is a unit vector (the rotation axis) and $\theta$ is the rotation angle.

### Definition 28.2.5 — Pure Quaternion (Vector Embedding)

A 3D vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y, v_z)$ is embedded as a **pure quaternion** (scalar part zero):

$$
p = 0 + v_x i + v_y j + v_z k = (0, \mathbf{v})
$$

### Definition 28.2.6 — Quaternion Rotation Action

The rotation of vector $\mathbf{v}$ by unit quaternion $q$ is:

$$
\mathbf{v}' = q \, p \, q^{-1} = q \, p \, \bar{q}
$$

where $p = (0, \mathbf{v})$ is the pure quaternion embedding of $\mathbf{v}$, and the result is also a pure quaternion whose vector part gives the rotated vector.

### Definition 28.2.7 — SLERP (Spherical Linear Interpolation)

Given two unit quaternions $q_0$ and $q_1$ and parameter $t \in [0, 1]$:

$$
\text{slerp}(q_0, q_1, t) = q_0 \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega} + q_1 \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}
$$

where $\Omega = \arccos(q_0 \cdot q_1)$ is the angle between the quaternions on $S^3$.

Equivalently: $\text{slerp}(q_0, q_1, t) = q_0 (q_0^{-1} q_1)^t$.

### Definition 28.2.8 — Gimbal Lock

**Gimbal lock** occurs when using Euler angles $(pitch, yaw, roll)$ and two rotation axes align, causing loss of one degree of freedom. Specifically, when pitch $= \pm 90°$, the yaw and roll axes become parallel, and the system can only rotate about a single remaining axis.

### Definition 28.2.9 — Euler Angles (Tait-Bryan Convention)

The rotation is decomposed as three sequential rotations about fixed or body axes:

$$
R = R_z(\gamma) \cdot R_y(\beta) \cdot R_x(\alpha)
$$

where $\alpha$ = roll, $\beta$ = pitch, $\gamma$ = yaw (extrinsic $XYZ$ convention). There are 12 valid Euler angle conventions; engines differ in their choice.

## 📐 2. Axioms / Postulates

### Axiom 28.2.A1 — Hamilton's Fundamental Relations

The quaternion imaginary units satisfy:

$$
i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1
$$

These four equations, together with distributivity over $\mathbb{R}$, completely determine the multiplication table of $\mathbb{H}$.

### Axiom 28.2.A2 — Quaternion Multiplication Is Associative

For all $p, q, r \in \mathbb{H}$:

$$
(pq)r = p(qr)
$$

This is essential for composing rotations: $q_2(q_1 \mathbf{v} q_1^{-1})q_2^{-1} = (q_2 q_1)\mathbf{v}(q_2 q_1)^{-1}$.

### Axiom 28.2.A3 — The Double Cover Property

The unit quaternions $q$ and $-q$ represent the **same rotation**:

$$
q\,p\,\bar{q} = (-q)\,p\,(-\bar{q})
$$

This means the map from unit quaternions to rotations is 2-to-1. The unit quaternion group $S^3$ is a double cover of $SO(3)$.

## 🛡️ 3. Lemmas

### Lemma 28.2.1 — Deriving the Multiplication Table from $ijk = -1$

**Claim:** From $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$, we can derive all six cross-products.

**Proof.**

Start with $ijk = -1$. Multiply both sides on the right by $k$:

$$
ijk \cdot k = -1 \cdot k
$$

$$
ij(k^2) = -k
$$

$$
ij(-1) = -k
$$

$$
-ij = -k
$$

$$
ij = k
$$

Now multiply $ijk = -1$ on the left by $i$:

$$
i \cdot ijk = i \cdot (-1)
$$

$$
(i^2)jk = -i
$$

$$
(-1)jk = -i
$$

$$
-jk = -i
$$

$$
jk = i
$$

Multiply $ijk = -1$ on the right by $j$ (after first multiplying by $k$):

From $ij = k$, multiply on the right by $j$:

$$
ij \cdot j = k \cdot j
$$

$$
i(j^2) = kj
$$

$$
i(-1) = kj
$$

$$
kj = -i
$$

From $jk = i$, multiply on the left by $j$:

$$
j \cdot jk = j \cdot i
$$

$$
(j^2)k = ji
$$

$$
-k = ji
$$

$$
ji = -k
$$

From $ij = k$, multiply on the left by $k$:

$$
k \cdot ij = k \cdot k
$$

$$
ki \cdot j = k^2 \quad \text{(by associativity)}
$$

Wait — let us use a cleaner route. From $jk = i$, multiply both sides on the right by $i$:

$$
jki = i^2 = -1
$$

But we also know $ijk = -1$, so $jki = -1$ as well. Now from $jk = i$, multiply on the left by $k$:

$$
k(jk) = ki
$$

$$
(kj)k = ki
$$

$$
(-i)k = ki
$$

$$
-ik = ki
$$

This gives us $ki = -ik$. Now from $ijk = -1$, multiply on the left by $-i$ and right by $-k$:

Actually, let us derive $ki$ directly. From $ijk = -1$, multiply on the left by $-j$:

$$
-j(ijk) = -j(-1) = j
$$

$$
-(j \cdot i)(jk) = j
$$

$$
-(ji)(i) = j \quad \text{... this is getting circular.}
$$

**Cleaner approach:** From $ij = k$, multiply both sides on the left by $k$:

$$
k(ij) = kk = k^2 = -1
$$

By associativity: $(ki)j = -1$. We need $(ki)j = -1$. If $ki = j$, then $jj = j^2 = -1$. ✓

Therefore $ki = j$.

From $ki = j$, multiply on the right by $i$:

$$
ki \cdot i = j \cdot i
$$

$$
k(i^2) = ji
$$

$$
k(-1) = ji
$$

$$
ji = -k \quad \checkmark \text{ (consistent)}
$$

From $ki = j$, multiply on the left by $i$:

$$
i(ki) = ij = k
$$

$$
(ik)i = k
$$

If $ik = -j$, then $(-j)i = -ji = -(-k) = k$. ✓

Therefore $ik = -j$.

**Summary of derived rules:**

| Product | Result |
|---------|--------|
| $ij$ | $k$ |
| $jk$ | $i$ |
| $ki$ | $j$ |
| $ji$ | $-k$ |
| $kj$ | $-i$ |
| $ik$ | $-j$ |

$\blacksquare$

### Lemma 28.2.2 — The Product of Unit Quaternions Is a Unit Quaternion

**Claim:** If $\|q_1\| = 1$ and $\|q_2\| = 1$, then $\|q_1 q_2\| = 1$.

**Proof.** The quaternion norm is multiplicative:

$$
\|q_1 q_2\|^2 = (q_1 q_2)\overline{(q_1 q_2)} = (q_1 q_2)(\bar{q}_2 \bar{q}_1) = q_1 (q_2 \bar{q}_2) \bar{q}_1 = q_1 \|q_2\|^2 \bar{q}_1 = q_1 \cdot 1 \cdot \bar{q}_1 = q_1 \bar{q}_1 = \|q_1\|^2 = 1
$$

Therefore $\|q_1 q_2\| = 1$. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 28.2.3 — Quaternion Rotation Preserves Vector Magnitude

**Claim:** If $\|q\| = 1$ and $p = (0, \mathbf{v})$, then $\|q p \bar{q}\| = \|p\| = |\mathbf{v}|$.

**Proof.** Using norm multiplicativity:

$$
\|q p \bar{q}\| = \|q\| \cdot \|p\| \cdot \|\bar{q}\| = 1 \cdot \|p\| \cdot 1 = \|p\|
$$

(We used $\|\bar{q}\| = \|q\| = 1$.) $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 28.2.4 — The Result of $qp\bar{q}$ Is a Pure Quaternion

**Claim:** If $q$ is a unit quaternion and $p = (0, \mathbf{v})$ is pure, then $qp\bar{q}$ is also pure (scalar part = 0).

**Proof.** Let $q = (w, \mathbf{u})$ and $p = (0, \mathbf{v})$.

The scalar part of a product $(a, \mathbf{A})(b, \mathbf{B})$ is $ab - \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}$.

First compute $qp = (w, \mathbf{u})(0, \mathbf{v})$:
- Scalar: $w \cdot 0 - \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$
- Vector: $w\mathbf{v} + 0 \cdot \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = w\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$

So $qp = (-\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v},\; w\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v})$.

Now compute $(qp)\bar{q}$ where $\bar{q} = (w, -\mathbf{u})$:

Scalar part of $(qp)\bar{q}$:

$$
(-\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})(w) - (w\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \cdot (-\mathbf{u})
$$

$$
= -w(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + w(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}) + (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{u}
$$

$$
= -w(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + w(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + 0
$$

$$
= 0
$$

(The cross product $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{u}$, so their dot product is zero.)

Therefore $qp\bar{q}$ has zero scalar part — it is a pure quaternion. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 28.2.5 — Composition of Quaternion Rotations

**Claim:** Applying rotation $q_1$ followed by rotation $q_2$ is equivalent to the single rotation $q_2 q_1$.

**Proof.**

$$
q_2(q_1 \, p \, \bar{q}_1)\bar{q}_2 = (q_2 q_1) \, p \, (\bar{q}_1 \bar{q}_2)
$$

Note that $\overline{q_2 q_1} = \bar{q}_1 \bar{q}_2$ (conjugate reverses order). Therefore:

$$
= (q_2 q_1) \, p \, \overline{(q_2 q_1)}
$$

This is the rotation by the composite quaternion $q_{\text{total}} = q_2 q_1$. $\blacksquare$

## 👑 4. Theorems

### Theorem 28.2.1 — The Quaternion Rotation Formula Equals Rodrigues' Formula

For unit quaternion $q = \cos(\theta/2) + \sin(\theta/2)\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ and pure quaternion $p = (0, \mathbf{v})$:

$$
q\,p\,\bar{q} = \left(0,\; \mathbf{v}\cos\theta + (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})\sin\theta + \hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})(1 - \cos\theta)\right)
$$

This is exactly Rodrigues' rotation formula (Theorem 28.1.4).

### Theorem 28.2.2 — Quaternion to Rotation Matrix Conversion

Given unit quaternion $q = (w, x, y, z)$, the equivalent 3×3 rotation matrix is:

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 2(y^2 + z^2) & 2(xy - wz) & 2(xz + wy) \\ 2(xy + wz) & 1 - 2(x^2 + z^2) & 2(yz - wx) \\ 2(xz - wy) & 2(yz + wx) & 1 - 2(x^2 + y^2) \end{pmatrix}
$$

### Theorem 28.2.3 — Rotation Matrix to Quaternion (Shepperd's Method)

Given rotation matrix $R$, the quaternion can be recovered via:

$$
w = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 + R_{11} + R_{22} + R_{33}}
$$

$$
x = \frac{R_{32} - R_{23}}{4w}, \quad y = \frac{R_{13} - R_{31}}{4w}, \quad z = \frac{R_{21} - R_{12}}{4w}
$$

(When $w \approx 0$, use the largest diagonal element to avoid division by zero.)

### Theorem 28.2.4 — SLERP Produces Constant Angular Velocity

The SLERP interpolation $\text{slerp}(q_0, q_1, t)$ traces a great-circle arc on $S^3$ at constant angular speed. The angular velocity $\omega = \Omega$ (the total angle) is constant with respect to $t$.

### Theorem 28.2.5 — Quaternion to Axis-Angle Extraction

Given unit quaternion $q = (w, x, y, z)$:

$$
\theta = 2\arccos(w)
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{(x, y, z)}{\sin(\theta/2)} = \frac{(x, y, z)}{\sqrt{1 - w^2}}
$$

(When $\theta \approx 0$, the axis is undefined — the rotation is negligible.)

## ✍️ 5. Proofs / Derivations

### 5.1 Full Derivation of the Quaternion Rotation Formula (Theorem 28.2.1)

**Goal:** Show that $q\,p\,\bar{q}$ with $q = \cos(\theta/2) + \sin(\theta/2)\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ produces Rodrigues' formula.

**Setup:** Let $c = \cos(\theta/2)$, $s = \sin(\theta/2)$, so $q = (c, s\hat{\mathbf{u}})$ and $\bar{q} = (c, -s\hat{\mathbf{u}})$.

Let $p = (0, \mathbf{v})$.

**Step 1: Compute $qp$.**

Using the quaternion product formula $(a, \mathbf{A})(b, \mathbf{B}) = (ab - \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B},\; a\mathbf{B} + b\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})$:

$$
qp = (c, s\hat{\mathbf{u}})(0, \mathbf{v})
$$

Scalar part:

$$
c \cdot 0 - s\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})
$$

Vector part:

$$
c\mathbf{v} + 0 \cdot s\hat{\mathbf{u}} + s\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{v} + s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})
$$

So: $qp = (-s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v}),\; c\mathbf{v} + s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}))$

**Step 2: Compute $(qp)\bar{q}$.**

Let $A = -s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})$ (scalar of $qp$) and $\mathbf{B} = c\mathbf{v} + s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})$ (vector of $qp$).

$(qp)\bar{q} = (A, \mathbf{B})(c, -s\hat{\mathbf{u}})$

Scalar part:

$$
Ac - \mathbf{B} \cdot (-s\hat{\mathbf{u}}) = Ac + s(\mathbf{B} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}})
$$

$$
= -sc(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + s\left[c(\mathbf{v} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}) + s((\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}})\right]
$$

$$
= -sc(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + sc(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + s^2 \cdot 0
$$

$$
= 0
$$

(Confirming Lemma 28.2.4: the result is pure.)

Vector part:

$$
A(-s\hat{\mathbf{u}}) + c\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B} \times (-s\hat{\mathbf{u}})
$$

$$
= -s \cdot (-s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}} + c(c\mathbf{v} + s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})) - s\mathbf{B} \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}
$$

$$
= s^2(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}} + c^2\mathbf{v} + cs(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) - s(c\mathbf{v} + s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}
$$

**Step 3: Expand the cross product term.**

$$
-s(c\mathbf{v} + s(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}} = -sc(\mathbf{v} \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}) - s^2((\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}})
$$

Note: $\mathbf{v} \times \hat{\mathbf{u}} = -(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})$, so $-sc(\mathbf{v} \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}) = sc(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})$.

For the triple cross product, use the BAC-CAB rule:

$$
(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}} = \hat{\mathbf{u}}((\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}) - (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}})
$$

Wait — the BAC-CAB rule states $\mathbf{A} \times (\mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{C}) - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B})$.

We need $(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}$. Let $\mathbf{A} = \hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{u}}$. Then:

$$
\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B} = -\mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{A} = -\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})
$$

Using BAC-CAB on $\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})$:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) = \hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v}) - \mathbf{v}(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}) = (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}} - \mathbf{v}
$$

Therefore:

$$
(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}} = -[\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v}) - \mathbf{v}] = \mathbf{v} - (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}}
$$

**Step 4: Substitute back.**

$$
-s^2((\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) \times \hat{\mathbf{u}}) = -s^2(\mathbf{v} - (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}}) = -s^2\mathbf{v} + s^2(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}}
$$

**Step 5: Collect all terms for the vector part.**

$$
\mathbf{v}' = s^2(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}} + c^2\mathbf{v} + cs(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) + sc(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) - s^2\mathbf{v} + s^2(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}}
$$

$$
= (c^2 - s^2)\mathbf{v} + 2cs(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v}) + 2s^2(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})\hat{\mathbf{u}}
$$

**Step 6: Apply double-angle identities.**

Recall $c = \cos(\theta/2)$, $s = \sin(\theta/2)$:

$$
c^2 - s^2 = \cos^2(\theta/2) - \sin^2(\theta/2) = \cos\theta
$$

$$
2cs = 2\cos(\theta/2)\sin(\theta/2) = \sin\theta
$$

$$
2s^2 = 2\sin^2(\theta/2) = 1 - \cos\theta
$$

**Step 7: Final result.**

$$
\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}\cos\theta + (\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v})\sin\theta + \hat{\mathbf{u}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v})(1 - \cos\theta)
$$

This is exactly Rodrigues' rotation formula. $\blacksquare$

### 5.2 Derivation of the Quaternion-to-Matrix Conversion (Theorem 28.2.2)

**Goal:** Express the rotation $\mathbf{v}' = q\mathbf{v}\bar{q}$ as a matrix multiplication $\mathbf{v}' = R\mathbf{v}$.

**Method:** Apply the rotation to each basis vector $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 = (1,0,0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2 = (0,1,0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_3 = (0,0,1)$. The columns of $R$ are the rotated basis vectors.

**Column 1:** Rotate $\mathbf{v} = (1, 0, 0)$ using Rodrigues' formula with $q = (w, x, y, z)$, $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = (x, y, z)/\sin(\theta/2)$:

Using the expanded formula directly from Step 5 above with $c^2 - s^2 = 1 - 2s^2 = 1 - 2(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) + 2w^2 - 1$... 

Actually, let us use the direct expansion. For $q = (w, x, y, z)$ with $w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$:

The $x$-component of $q(0, 1, 0, 0)\bar{q}$:

From the full product formula, the vector part of $qp\bar{q}$ for $p = (0, v_x, v_y, v_z)$ is:

$$
v'_x = (1 - 2y^2 - 2z^2)v_x + (2xy - 2wz)v_y + (2xz + 2wy)v_z
$$

$$
v'_y = (2xy + 2wz)v_x + (1 - 2x^2 - 2z^2)v_y + (2yz - 2wx)v_z
$$

$$
v'_z = (2xz - 2wy)v_x + (2yz + 2wx)v_y + (1 - 2x^2 - 2y^2)v_z
$$

Reading off the coefficients gives the matrix in Theorem 28.2.2.

**Verification of entry $R_{11} = 1 - 2y^2 - 2z^2$:**

Set $\mathbf{v} = (1, 0, 0)$. Then $\hat{\mathbf{u}} \cdot \mathbf{v} = x/s$ (where $s = \sin(\theta/2) = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$) and $\hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \mathbf{v} = (0, z/s, -y/s)$.

From Rodrigues:

$$
v'_x = 1 \cdot \cos\theta + 0 + (x/s)(x/s)(1-\cos\theta)
$$

$$
= \cos\theta + \frac{x^2}{s^2}(1-\cos\theta)
$$

Using $\cos\theta = 1 - 2s^2$ and $s^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$:

$$
= 1 - 2s^2 + \frac{x^2}{s^2} \cdot 2s^2 = 1 - 2s^2 + 2x^2 = 1 - 2(y^2 + z^2)
$$

$\blacksquare$

### 5.3 Proof That Gimbal Lock Cannot Occur with Quaternions

**Claim:** Quaternion representation has no singularities for any rotation in $SO(3)$.

**Proof.** Euler angles parameterize $SO(3)$ using three angles $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$. The Jacobian of the map $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \to R$ becomes singular when $\beta = \pm\pi/2$ (the pitch axis aligns with yaw). At these configurations, changes in $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ produce the same physical rotation — one degree of freedom is lost.

Quaternions parameterize rotations via the map $\phi: S^3 \to SO(3)$ defined by $\phi(q)(v) = qv\bar{q}$. The domain $S^3$ is a smooth, compact manifold without boundary. The map $\phi$ is a smooth surjection (every rotation is reachable) and a local diffeomorphism everywhere on $S^3$ (the differential $d\phi_q$ has rank 3 for all $q \in S^3$).

To see this: the tangent space $T_q S^3$ is 3-dimensional (since $S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4$ is a 3-manifold). The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is also 3-dimensional. The differential $d\phi_q$ maps $T_q S^3 \to T_{\phi(q)} SO(3)$ and is surjective for every $q$ (no rank drop). Therefore, there is no configuration where a degree of freedom is lost.

The only "redundancy" is the global 2-to-1 nature ($q$ and $-q$ map to the same rotation), which is a topological property of the double cover, not a local singularity. $\blacksquare$

### 5.4 Derivation of SLERP

**Goal:** Derive the formula for constant-speed interpolation on $S^3$.

**Step 1:** Two unit quaternions $q_0, q_1$ define an angle $\Omega$ on $S^3$:

$$
\cos\Omega = q_0 \cdot q_1 = w_0 w_1 + x_0 x_1 + y_0 y_1 + z_0 z_1
$$

**Step 2:** We seek $q(t)$ on the great circle from $q_0$ to $q_1$ such that the arc-length parameter is linear in $t$. By analogy with 2D circular interpolation:

$$
q(t) = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega} q_0 + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega} q_1
$$

**Step 3: Verify boundary conditions.**

At $t = 0$: $q(0) = \frac{\sin\Omega}{\sin\Omega} q_0 + \frac{\sin 0}{\sin\Omega} q_1 = q_0$. ✓

At $t = 1$: $q(1) = \frac{\sin 0}{\sin\Omega} q_0 + \frac{\sin\Omega}{\sin\Omega} q_1 = q_1$. ✓

**Step 4: Verify unit length.**

$$
\|q(t)\|^2 = \frac{\sin^2((1-t)\Omega) + 2\sin((1-t)\Omega)\sin(t\Omega)\cos\Omega + \sin^2(t\Omega)}{\sin^2\Omega}
$$

Using the identity $\sin^2 A + \sin^2 B + 2\sin A \sin B \cos(A+B) = \sin^2(A+B)$ with $A = (1-t)\Omega$, $B = t\Omega$, $A + B = \Omega$:

$$
= \frac{\sin^2\Omega}{\sin^2\Omega} = 1
$$

$\blacksquare$

### 6.1 Python+NumPy: Quaternion Operations

```python
import numpy as np

def quat_multiply(q1, q2):
    """Multiply two quaternions q = (w, x, y, z)."""
    w1, x1, y1, z1 = q1
    w2, x2, y2, z2 = q2
    return np.array([
        w1*w2 - x1*x2 - y1*y2 - z1*z2,
        w1*x2 + x1*w2 + y1*z2 - z1*y2,
        w1*y2 - x1*z2 + y1*w2 + z1*x2,
        w1*z2 + x1*y2 - y1*x2 + z1*w2
    ])

def quat_conjugate(q):
    """Conjugate: (w, -x, -y, -z)."""
    return np.array([q[0], -q[1], -q[2], -q[3]])

def quat_from_axis_angle(axis, angle_rad):
    """Unit quaternion from axis (unit vector) and angle."""
    half = angle_rad / 2.0
    s = np.sin(half)
    return np.array([np.cos(half), axis[0]*s, axis[1]*s, axis[2]*s])

def quat_rotate(q, v):
    """Rotate 3D vector v by unit quaternion q."""
    p = np.array([0.0, v[0], v[1], v[2]])
    result = quat_multiply(quat_multiply(q, p), quat_conjugate(q))
    return result[1:]  # vector part

def quat_to_matrix(q):
    """Convert unit quaternion to 3x3 rotation matrix."""
    w, x, y, z = q
    return np.array([
        [1-2*(y*y+z*z), 2*(x*y-w*z),   2*(x*z+w*y)],
        [2*(x*y+w*z),   1-2*(x*x+z*z), 2*(y*z-w*x)],
        [2*(x*z-w*y),   2*(y*z+w*x),   1-2*(x*x+y*y)]
    ])

def slerp(q0, q1, t):
    """Spherical linear interpolation between unit quaternions."""
    dot = np.dot(q0, q1)
    # Ensure shortest path
    if dot < 0:
        q1 = -q1
        dot = -dot
    # If very close, fall back to linear interpolation
    if dot > 0.9995:
        result = q0 + t * (q1 - q0)
        return result / np.linalg.norm(result)
    omega = np.arccos(np.clip(dot, -1, 1))
    sin_omega = np.sin(omega)
    return (np.sin((1-t)*omega)/sin_omega) * q0 + (np.sin(t*omega)/sin_omega) * q1

# Example: rotate (1,0,0) by 90° about Y-axis
q = quat_from_axis_angle(np.array([0, 1, 0]), np.radians(90))
v = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
v_rot = quat_rotate(q, v)
print(f"Rotated: {v_rot}")  # Expected: (0, 0, -1)
```

### 6.2 Unity C#: Quaternion Usage

```csharp
using UnityEngine;

public class QuaternionDemo : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private Transform target;
    [SerializeField] private float rotationSpeed = 90f; // degrees/sec

    void Update()
    {
        // Rotate around world Y-axis
        Quaternion deltaRot = Quaternion.AngleAxis(rotationSpeed * Time.deltaTime, Vector3.up);
        transform.rotation = deltaRot * transform.rotation;

        // SLERP toward target rotation
        if (target != null)
        {
            transform.rotation = Quaternion.Slerp(
                transform.rotation,
                target.rotation,
                Time.deltaTime * 2f
            );
        }

        // Quaternion from Euler (beware: Unity uses ZXY intrinsic order)
        Quaternion fromEuler = Quaternion.Euler(30f, 45f, 0f);

        // Extract axis-angle
        fromEuler.ToAngleAxis(out float angle, out Vector3 axis);
        Debug.Log($"Axis: {axis}, Angle: {angle}°");

        // Compose rotations: first pitch, then yaw
        Quaternion pitch = Quaternion.AngleAxis(30f, Vector3.right);
        Quaternion yaw = Quaternion.AngleAxis(45f, Vector3.up);
        Quaternion combined = yaw * pitch; // yaw applied AFTER pitch
        transform.rotation = combined;
    }
}
```

### Example 28.2.1 — Quaternion Multiplication by Hand

**Problem:** Compute $q_1 q_2$ where $q_1 = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ (not unit) and $q_2 = (2, -1, 0, 3)$.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

Using the component formula from Definition 28.2.2:

$w_1 = 1, x_1 = 2, y_1 = 3, z_1 = 4$

$w_2 = 2, x_2 = -1, y_2 = 0, z_2 = 3$

**Scalar part:**

$$
w = w_1 w_2 - x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2 - z_1 z_2
$$

$$
= (1)(2) - (2)(-1) - (3)(0) - (4)(3) = 2 + 2 - 0 - 12 = -8
$$

**$i$-component:**

$$
x = w_1 x_2 + x_1 w_2 + y_1 z_2 - z_1 y_2
$$

$$
= (1)(-1) + (2)(2) + (3)(3) - (4)(0) = -1 + 4 + 9 - 0 = 12
$$

**$j$-component:**

$$
y = w_1 y_2 - x_1 z_2 + y_1 w_2 + z_1 x_2
$$

$$
= (1)(0) - (2)(3) + (3)(2) + (4)(-1) = 0 - 6 + 6 - 4 = -4
$$

**$k$-component:**

$$
z = w_1 z_2 + x_1 y_2 - y_1 x_2 + z_1 w_2
$$

$$
= (1)(3) + (2)(0) - (3)(-1) + (4)(2) = 3 + 0 + 3 + 8 = 14
$$

**Result:** $q_1 q_2 = (-8, 12, -4, 14)$

**Verification:** $\|q_1\| = \sqrt{1+4+9+16} = \sqrt{30}$, $\|q_2\| = \sqrt{4+1+0+9} = \sqrt{14}$.

$\|q_1 q_2\| = \sqrt{64 + 144 + 16 + 196} = \sqrt{420} = \sqrt{30 \cdot 14} = \|q_1\|\|q_2\|$. ✓

</details>

### Example 28.2.2 — Rotating a Vector with a Quaternion

**Problem:** Rotate $\mathbf{v} = (1, 0, 0)$ by $90°$ about the $z$-axis using quaternion multiplication. Show every step.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Construct the quaternion.

$$
q = \cos(45°) + \sin(45°)(0i + 0j + 1k) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}k = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, 0, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)
$$

$$
\bar{q} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, 0, -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)
$$

**Step 2:** Embed $\mathbf{v}$ as pure quaternion: $p = (0, 1, 0, 0)$.

**Step 3:** Compute $qp$.

Let $c = \sqrt{2}/2 \approx 0.7071$.

$$
qp = (c, 0, 0, c)(0, 1, 0, 0)
$$

Scalar: $c \cdot 0 - (0)(1) - (0)(0) - (c)(0) = 0$

$i$: $c \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 - c \cdot 0 = c$

$j$: $c \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + c \cdot 1 = c$

$k$: $c \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1 + c \cdot 0 = 0$

So $qp = (0, c, c, 0)$.

**Step 4:** Compute $(qp)\bar{q} = (0, c, c, 0)(c, 0, 0, -c)$.

Scalar: $0 \cdot c - c \cdot 0 - c \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot (-c) = 0$ ✓ (pure)

$i$: $0 \cdot 0 + c \cdot c + c \cdot (-c) - 0 \cdot 0 = c^2 - c^2 = 0$

$j$: $0 \cdot 0 - c \cdot (-c) + c \cdot c + 0 \cdot 0 = c^2 + c^2 = 2c^2 = 1$

$k$: $0 \cdot (-c) + c \cdot 0 - c \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot c = 0$

**Result:** $qp\bar{q} = (0, 0, 1, 0)$, so $\mathbf{v}' = (0, 1, 0)$.

This confirms: rotating $(1, 0, 0)$ by $90°$ about $z$ gives $(0, 1, 0)$. ✓

</details>

### Example 28.2.3 — SLERP Between Two Orientations

**Problem:** Interpolate at $t = 0.5$ between $q_0 = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ (identity) and $q_1 = (0, 0, 1, 0)$ (180° about $y$-axis).

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

**Step 1:** Compute the angle between quaternions.

$$
\cos\Omega = q_0 \cdot q_1 = (1)(0) + (0)(0) + (0)(1) + (0)(0) = 0
$$

$$
\Omega = \arccos(0) = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$

**Step 2:** Apply SLERP formula.

$$
q(0.5) = \frac{\sin((1-0.5)\pi/2)}{\sin(\pi/2)} q_0 + \frac{\sin(0.5 \cdot \pi/2)}{\sin(\pi/2)} q_1
$$

$$
= \frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{1} (1, 0, 0, 0) + \frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{1} (0, 0, 1, 0)
$$

$$
= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1, 0, 0, 0) + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(0, 0, 1, 0)
$$

$$
= \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0\right)
$$

**Step 3:** Interpret the result.

This quaternion represents rotation by $\theta = 2\arccos(\sqrt{2}/2) = 2 \cdot 45° = 90°$ about axis $(0, 1, 0)$.

This makes sense: halfway between 0° and 180° about $y$ is 90° about $y$. ✓

**Verify unit length:** $(\sqrt{2}/2)^2 + 0 + (\sqrt{2}/2)^2 + 0 = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1$. ✓

</details>

### Example 28.2.4 — Quaternion to Rotation Matrix

**Problem:** Convert $q = (\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, 0)$ (90° about $x$-axis) to a rotation matrix.

<details>
<summary>🔍 View Step-by-Step Solution</summary>

$w = \sqrt{2}/2$, $x = \sqrt{2}/2$, $y = 0$, $z = 0$.

Using Theorem 28.2.2:

$$
R_{11} = 1 - 2(y^2 + z^2) = 1 - 0 = 1
$$

$$
R_{12} = 2(xy - wz) = 2(0 - 0) = 0
$$

$$
R_{13} = 2(xz + wy) = 2(0 + 0) = 0
$$

$$
R_{21} = 2(xy + wz) = 2(0 + 0) = 0
$$

$$
R_{22} = 1 - 2(x^2 + z^2) = 1 - 2(\frac{1}{2} + 0) = 1 - 1 = 0
$$

$$
R_{23} = 2(yz - wx) = 2(0 - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) = 2(-\frac{1}{2}) = -1
$$

$$
R_{31} = 2(xz - wy) = 0
$$

$$
R_{32} = 2(yz + wx) = 2(0 + \frac{1}{2}) = 1
$$

$$
R_{33} = 1 - 2(x^2 + y^2) = 1 - 2(\frac{1}{2}) = 0
$$

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

This is indeed $R_x(90°)$. ✓

</details>
