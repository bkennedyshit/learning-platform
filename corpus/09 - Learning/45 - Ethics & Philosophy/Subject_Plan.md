# Subject Syllabus: 58 - Ethics & Philosophy

*Back to [[00 - 09 - Learning Index|Learning Index]]*

> **Why this matters for AI development:** Every AI product you build makes ethical choices — what data it uses, what it optimizes for, who it serves, what it refuses to do, how it presents uncertainty. Those choices are philosophical whether you name them as such or not. Building them deliberately requires a framework. This track provides one.

## 📝 3. Chapter Outline

| # | Chapter | Core skill |
|---|---------|-----------|
| 58.1 | **Logic & Critical Thinking** | Deductive vs. inductive reasoning; validity vs. soundness; logical fallacies (the 20 most important: ad hominem, straw man, false dichotomy, slippery slope, appeal to authority, appeal to nature, gambler's fallacy, etc.); argument mapping; Bayesian reasoning (updating beliefs in proportion to evidence — the most important rational framework); the difference between a strong argument and a rhetorically persuasive one. |
| 58.2 | **Epistemology — How We Know What We Know** | The justified true belief definition of knowledge (and Gettier's demolition of it); empiricism (Hume: all knowledge from sensory experience) vs. rationalism (Descartes, Kant: some knowledge is a priori); skepticism and its responses (Descartes' method of doubt; Hume's problem of induction — you can never logically prove the future will resemble the past); Karl Popper's falsificationism (science progresses by being falsifiable and falsified — not by confirmation); Kuhn's paradigm shifts (how scientific revolutions actually happen). |
| 58.3 | **Ethical Frameworks — The Three Systems** | Consequentialism/Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill, Singer): the right action maximizes well-being; strengths and problems (who counts? can you harm one to save five?); Deontological ethics (Kant): the categorical imperative — act only according to maxims you could will to be universal laws; rights and duties as constraints on outcomes; Virtue ethics (Aristotle): the right action is what a virtuous person would do; character over rules or outcomes; *eudaimonia* (flourishing) as the goal; meta-ethics: where do moral facts come from? (realism vs. anti-realism). |
| 58.4 | **Political Philosophy** | The social contract (Hobbes: life is nasty, brutish, short without it; Locke: natural rights precede the state; Rousseau: the general will); liberty (Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty — freedom from interference — vs. positive liberty — freedom to actually do things); justice (Rawls' veil of ignorance: design society not knowing your place in it → leads to his two principles of justice); libertarianism (Nozick: only minimal state is justified); communitarianism; democracy's philosophical foundations and vulnerabilities. |
| 58.5 | **Philosophy of Mind & Free Will** | The hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers: why is there subjective experience at all?); physicalism vs. dualism; functionalism (mental states are defined by functional roles — the view that makes AI consciousness theoretically possible); free will: compatibilism (free will is compatible with determinism — most philosophers' view) vs. hard determinism vs. libertarianism; personal identity (what makes you the same person you were 10 years ago?). Cross-link: [[07 - Philosophy of Mind & Consciousness/Subject_Plan|Track 07]] |
| 58.6 | **Nietzsche — Power, Value Creation & The Übermensch** | Nietzsche deserves his own chapter because he is genuinely different from everyone else in the Western tradition. He is not building a system — he is destroying the foundations of all previous systems and asking you to create your own. Key concepts: the death of God (and what that means for value); the will to power (not domination — the drive to express, create, overcome); master morality vs. slave morality; the eternal recurrence (as a test of affirmation, not a cosmological theory); the Übermensch (the person who creates values rather than inheriting them); Nietzsche and the Irish connection (his Dionysian vs. Apollonian framework maps onto the Fomorian/Tuatha Dé tension in Irish mythology). |
| 58.7 | **AI Ethics — The Applied Field** | Alignment (how do you ensure an AI system pursues the goals you actually want?); value alignment problem (specifying human values precisely enough for a machine to optimize for them); Goodhart's Law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") — relevant to every ML objective function; fairness in ML (demographic parity vs. equal opportunity vs. individual fairness — these are mathematically incompatible in general); bias in training data; privacy and surveillance; the trolley problem applied to autonomous vehicles (genuinely not solved); existential risk from AI (the Bostrom/Yudkowsky view and its critics); the effective altruism connection. |
| 58.8 | **Stoicism & Applied Philosophy** | Stoicism is the philosophy that has the most direct daily application — Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca. Core concepts: the dichotomy of control (distinguish what is and isn't in your power; focus entirely on the former); the view from above (zooming out to see your problems in cosmic context); *memento mori* (mortality awareness as a motivational tool, not nihilism); *amor fati* (love of fate — Nietzsche's restatement of the Stoic affirmation); daily practices (the evening review, the morning meditation, negative visualization); how Stoicism applies to the specific challenges of: building a startup (delayed gratification, handling rejection), chronic stress and C-PTSD (locus of control is the clinical intervention), and learning under uncertainty. |
